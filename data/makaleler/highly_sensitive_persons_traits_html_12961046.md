A highly sensitive person (HSP) is an individual who perceives and processes sensory information intensely. HSPs exhibit profound emotional responses, demonstrateheightened empathy, require periods of seclusion for relief, and typically avoid distressing situations.

According to Dr. Julie Smith, a clinical psychologist, these are the signs and characteristics of a highly sensitive person:

- Notices subtleties:Highly sensitive people (HSPs) notice subtleties in their environment that other people miss.

- Feels others’ moods:HSPs feel deeply affected by the moods of the people around them.

- Easily overwhelmed:Being asked to do too many things at once quickly overwhelms them.

- Needs to withdraw:In noisy, crowded, or busy environments, they feel a need to withdraw for relief.

- Dislikes change:HSPs avoid having a lot going on, because of the strong emotions this provokes.

- Performance nerves:Competing or performing in front of others makes them so nervous it hurts their performance.

- Moved by art:HSPs can feel deeply moved by art or music.

- Senses discomfort:When others are uncomfortable in a space, HSPs sense it and know what would help.

- Absorbs others’ distress:Violent movies or other people’s distress affect HSPs deeply, and they spend a long time processing it.

- Avoids upset:HSPs often make it a priority to avoid anything upsetting.

### Background

The termHighly Sensitive Person (HSP)was introduced by psychologistElaine Aronin the mid-1990s to describe individuals with heightened sensitivity to physical, emotional, and social stimuli.

In the research literature, the trait is usually calledsensory processing sensitivity(SPS). This is not sensory processing disorder. That is a separate condition affecting how the brain processes sensory information.

HSP is not a disorder. It’s a personality characteristic found in about 15–20% of the population.

SPS is distinct from sensory processing disorder and should not be confused withintroversionorneuroticism. Though often overlapping with these traits, Aron emphasized that high sensitivity is a separate and biologically rooted temperament.

### Highly dislike violence

Whilst it is typical to dislike violence, for HSPs, they are likely to be more distressed or deeply disturbed by violence. Even hearing about violence or cruelty can be extremely upsetting for them.

HSPs will likely make effort to avoid situations where they may witness violence such as avoiding certain movies, TV shows, or hearing stories of animal cruelty.

### Emotional responsiveness and empathy

This is the emotional responsiveness to other people.

HSPs tend to experience other people’s emotions. They may enter a room and immediately sense the mood of the people in it. This is because they are sensitive to subtleties such as facial expressions, body language, and tone of voice.

Being able to tap into other people’s feelings at this level is a huge strength, especially for creative work or work involving people. It comes at a cost, too.

It carries the vulnerability of feeling others’ distress deeply. Their own mood can shift to mirror the people around them, which can feel emotionally exhausting.

### Often feel overstimulated

HSPs might notice small details that other people don’t. That’s a wonderful thing when those details make life beautiful, but processing all that extra information means they need to rest from the world periodically.

Since HSPs are very sensitive to environmental stimuli, they can become overwhelmed by sensory input such as noise, bright lights, the fabric of clothes, and strong smells. They may find it hard to concentrate if there is a lot going on in their environment.

### Often withdraw

HSPs may often require a lot of downtime, usually on their own in a place which is comforting to them.

They may feel more of a need to withdraw after a particularly stimulating day so they can recharge and lower their stimulation levels.

### Startle easily

Many HSPs have a highly sensitive ‘startle reflex’. If someone sneaks up on them, or there is a loud noise, they are more likely to be very startled.

This may be because their central nervous system is very sensitive, reacting as though there is danger even in non-threatening situations.

### A vivid imagination

Since HSPs think and process things deeply, they often have a rich and complex inner world.

They often have deep thoughts and strong feelings to go with them, as well as having vividly realistic dreams. As a child, they may have had imaginary friends, enjoyed fantasy-based play, and daydreamed.

### Deeply moved by beauty

HSPs are likely to have a deeper emotional response to beauty, such as artwork, music, and fine food.

They may have a deeper appreciation of life and find it hard to understand how other people are not as moved as they are.

### Perceptive

As HSPs are more likely to notice subtleties in the environment, they may be seen as perceptive and insightful.

This may make them better at solving problems or able to adapt to more situations due to noticing thing that others may not.

### Strong emotional responses

HSPs have strongeremotional responses, both positively and negatively. This means they feel higher levels of joy and happiness, but easily upset at times of sadness.

They may also get angry when they are hungry or feel hurt when criticized.

### How High Sensitivity Affects Daily Life

Being a highly sensitive person (HSP) can bring both emotional richness and practical challenges. Elaine Aron summarizes the trait with the acronymDOES:

- Depth of processing– things are thought through very thoroughly, with a longer processing time.

- Overstimulation– bright lights or noises can feel too much and may be actively avoided

- Emotional reactivity and empathy– knowing how someone is feeling, sensing others’ moods, and hating conflict.

- Sensing the subtle– noticing small changes in people and the environment, adjusting their approach accordingly.

By understanding how these patterns affect daily life, HSPs can manage overwhelm and use their sensitivity as a strength.

### Social and Relationship Challenges

HSPs are often deeply empathetic, intuitive, and nurturing in relationships. However, they may also be prone to emotional overload in social situations.

Misreading neutral comments as criticism, avoiding conflict, or feeling deeply wounded by minor disagreements is common.

Without clear boundaries, HSPs can becomepeople-pleasers, especially vulnerable to emotionally manipulative individuals such asnarcissists.

### Sensory and Environmental Sensitivity

While HSPs often appreciate beauty, nature, and fine sensory experiences, they can be easily overwhelmed by noise, bright lights, crowds, or strong smells.

Even mild stressors, like a cluttered room or a skipped meal, can quickly drain their energy and lead to irritability, headaches, or emotional fatigue.

### Emotional Intensity and Self-Criticism

HSPs tend to feel both joy and sadness more deeply. They may cry during emotional movies, be profoundly moved by kindness, orruminateover perceived mistakes.

Many areperfectionistsand harsh self-critics, particularly in performance settings or when under pressure. This sensitivity can fuel negative self-talk and anxiety about being “too much.”

### Struggles with Boundaries and Busy Environments

Busy days, looming deadlines, or multitasking can be especially stressful for HSPs, who often need time alone to recover from overstimulation.

Their desire to help others can lead to overcommitment, burnout, or resentment when they neglect their own needs to keep the peace.

### Workplace and School

Sensitivity does not only shape how stressful an environment feels. It also shapes how much an HSP gains from a genuinely supportive one.

Pluess and Boniwell (2015) followed a group of adolescent girls at heightened risk of depression through a school-based prevention programme. A year later, the most highly sensitive girls showed the biggest drop in depressive symptoms and the biggest rise in self-esteem. Girls low in sensitivity barely changed.

Open-plan offices, constant interruptions, and back-to-back meetings tend to push HSPs toward overstimulation faster than their colleagues.

But the same sensitivity often brings above-average conscientiousness, attention to detail, and interpersonal attunement to a role that fits it. Predictable schedules, quiet workspace options, and some autonomy over pacing and breaks are practical accommodations that help.

### Thriving as a Highly Sensitive Person

Although there may be times when things an get overwhelming and stressful due to being highly sensitive, being sensitive should not be viewed as a negative trait.

The highs of life can bring more joyful experiences and happiness than someone who is not an HSP. Finding ways to cope with the stress when it does come can be helpful for those with sensitive personalities.

A number of strategies can help HSPs limit the negative side of sensitivity while making the most of its positives:

- Identify triggers:Working out what causes stress and overwhelm makes those situations easier to avoid or plan around.

- Add positivity:On an unavoidably stimulating day, set aside time for something enjoyable, even a simple rest break.

- Learn to say no:Creating boundaries feels unnatural at first, but saying no protects an HSP’s own happiness and peace.

- Set up a retreat:Having a relaxing place to withdraw to, at home or elsewhere, makes it easier to recharge.

- Use sensitivity as an advantage:Deliberately enjoying what sensitivity makes pleasurable, like art or nice smells, makes the most of the trait.

- Educate others:Explaining what being an HSP means helps people understand what feels too stimulating.

- Break tasks down:Splitting a long task list into smaller, timed pieces makes it easier to judge what is realistic.

- Be kind to oneself:Negative self-talk is neither helpful nor supportive, and an HSP is not responsible for everyone else’s happiness.

### Knowing the triggers

By trying to find out what is causing stress and feelings of being overwhelmed, HSPs can learn to avoid these.

Whilst some stressors cannot always be avoided in every instance, a lot can be controlled for. For instance, if a person knows that violent movies make them feel stressed, they should be easy to avoid.

### Add positivity

If the HSP knows they are going to have a highly stimulating day that they cannot avoid, they can dedicate some time to an activity they enjoy. This keeps the stress from becoming overwhelming.

This can even include simple rest breaks of not doing anything, so that the person has time to recharge.

### Learn to say no

Whilst it may feel unnatural to create boundaries with others, it is important to say no sometimes if the person’s own happiness and peace are being sacrificed for others’ needs.

Deciding what can reasonably be done and what cannot be done can help with choosing what to do for others. It is likely that other people do not realize that the HSP is feeling overwhelmed from their demands and would not want them to suffer because of their own actions.

### Set up a relaxing atmosphere

When things inevitably get too hectic, it can be really beneficial for the HSP to have a place that they can retreat to when they need to relax.

This place can be at home, in a bedroom, or at a park. As long as the place is somewhere where the HSP can truly relax and recharge when things become too stimulating.

### Use sensitivity as an advantage

If the HSP finds pleasure in art or nice smells, for instance, they can make sure to spend some time enjoying those activities. This makes the most of their sensitivity, rather than only managing its costs.

### Educate others

Explaining to others about HSPs and what this means can help them understand what is too stimulating. They can then make a conscious effort to make their presence or the environment more comfortable for the person.

### Break things down

When given a long list of tasks to complete, it may be helpful to break these down into manageable bits.

This could include writing a to-do list and dedicating a set amount of time to each task. That way, the person can work out what they can reasonably get done in the time they have.

### Being kind to oneself

Being highly sensitive may mean that the HSP is overly critical of themselves and their actions. However, the negative self-talk can be invalidating and is neither helpful nor supportive.

The HSP is not responsible for everyone else’s happiness. If someone is upset, they should not blame themselves unless they directly caused it.

It is not possible to make everyone happy all the time. Other people’s feelings cannot always be controlled.

As Aron (2013) explains, understanding overstimulation as a physiological response rather than a personal failing is itself a therapeutic step. Reframing it this way can ease the shame and self-criticism that often come with being highly sensitive.

### Are Highly Sensitive Traits the Same as Autism, ADHD, Anxiety, or Introversion?

Many people confuse high sensitivity (HSP traits) with autism spectrum traits, social anxiety, or introversion.

While there are some overlaps (e.g., sensitivity to stimuli or preference for calm environments), they are not the same. Below is a brief comparison clarifying overlaps and key differences:

### High Sensitivity vs Autism (ASD)

Both highly sensitive people andautisticindividuals can besensitive to sensory input (e.g.,loud noises, crowded spaces).

However, autism spectrum disorder is a neurodevelopmental diagnosis with distinct social-communication differences and often repetitive behaviors, traits not inherent to HSPs.

High sensitivity is a personality trait (sensory processing sensitivity), not a disorder, and HSPs generally do not exhibit the social difficulties or restricted interests that define autism.

### High Sensitivity vs Social Anxiety

Both HSPs and people withsocial anxietymay avoid intense, crowded, or overwhelming situations.

The difference lies in why. Social anxiety is driven by fear of negative evaluation or judgment and is a clinical condition, whereas HSPs withdraw due to sensory overstimulation rather than fear.

Being highly sensitive is not ananxiety disorder,while severe anxiety is diagnosable and can significantly impair daily life.

### High Sensitivity vs Introversion

HSPs and introverts both may prefer quiet or less chaotic environments to avoid feeling overwhelmed. But the two are not the same.

Introversion is simply a personality orientation about energy (introverts recharge alone), distinct from sensory sensitivity. In fact, a sizable minority of HSPs (around 30%) areextroverts, so not all highly sensitive people are introverts.

That distinction matters. High sensitivity relates to how deeply someone processes stimuli and emotions. Introversion relates to how someone gains or drains energy in social settings.

### High Sensitivity vs ADHD

Sensory overwhelm and distractibility can look similar from the outside, so high sensitivity andADHDare sometimes mistaken for one another. The two traits also co-occur more than chance would predict.

Panagiotidi, Overton, and Stafford (2020) gave the Highly Sensitive Person Scale and the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale to 274 non-clinical adults recruited from a university. They found a strong positive correlation between the number of self-reported ADHD traits and sensory sensitivity.

An exploratory factor analysis even revealed a factor that combined ADHD characteristics with sensory-sensitivity items, consistent with separate evidence of atypical sensory processing in clinically diagnosed ADHD.

In practice, this means the two are not mutually exclusive. A highly sensitive person and someone with ADHD traits can show overlapping overstimulation and distractibility, and each still needs its own assessment route.

### Is There Scientific Evidence for the Highly Sensitive Person?

The concept of the Highly Sensitive Person (HSP), based on the trait ofsensory processing sensitivity(SPS), is supported by growing but still developing scientific research.

### Support for the Trait

Psychologists have validated the trait with reliable self-report tools. The Highly Sensitive Person Scale, for instance, consistently identifies people who experience greater emotional reactivity, sensory sensitivity, and depth of processing.

Brain imagingsupports this picture. Acevedo et al. (2014) found heightened activation in regions linked to empathy and emotion regulation when highly sensitive people viewed others’ emotional expressions. Agenetic studyadds support too: Chen et al. (2011) linked dopamine-related gene variation to the sensitivity phenotype.

### Ongoing Debate

Some researchers question whether SPS is a distinct trait, or simply an overlap of existing personality factors such as neuroticism or openness. A 2019 meta-analysis tested this directly.

Pooling published data across children and adults, it found sensitivity moderately linked to neuroticism in both groups, and to openness in adults only. It concluded the trait is“related but relatively distinct”rather than a simple restatement of existing personality dimensions.

A wider critical review reaches a similarly cautious verdict. As Greven and colleagues (2019) conclude, sensitivity is a heritable pattern of individual differences, but its boundaries with related traits still need clearer, more objective measurement.

SPS is not yet recognized in diagnostic manuals, and it is not yet widely accepted across clinical psychology.

In short, the HSP framework is grounded in real biological and psychological patterns. It remains a topic of ongoing research and debate. Understanding its emerging science can help validate the lived experience of sensitive individuals, while avoiding overgeneralization.

### Contemporary Research

Aron’s original theory assumed high sensitivity was a single continuous spectrum, not a set of separate types. A 2018 study tested that.

Lionetti et al. (2018) gave the Highly Sensitive Person Scale to 906 adults, then ran statistical tests for natural groupings. The analysis found three groups, not the simple high/low split earlier theory assumed. The old two-group model fell short.

A highly sensitive group, nicknamed “orchids,” made up 31% of the sample, and a low-sensitivity “dandelion” group made up 29%. The remaining 40% formed a previously undescribed medium-sensitivity group, nicknamed “tulips.”

Orchids scored highest in neuroticism and lowest in extraversion. This revises Aron’s original two-group model into a three-way spectrum.

### Key Takeaways

- Not a Disorder:Being an HSP is a personality trait, found in roughly 15-20% of people, not a diagnosis or a disorder.

- DOES Framework:Aron sums up the trait as Depth of processing, Overstimulation, Emotional reactivity and empathy, and Sensing the subtle.

- Not Autism/Anxiety:HSPs can share sensory sensitivity with autistic people and people with social anxiety, but the underlying cause differs in each case.

- Not Just Introverts:Around 30% of HSPs are extroverts, so high sensitivity is about depth of processing, not where someone gets their energy.

- Evidence Is Growing:A validated scale, brain-imaging studies, and a 2018 three-group finding (orchids, tulips, dandelions) support the trait, though it remains related to, not the same as, neuroticism and openness.

- Coping:Knowing personal triggers, building in recovery time, and reframing sensitivity as a strength all help HSPs thrive.
