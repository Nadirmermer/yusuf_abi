### What Bill Taught



### W. P. Kinsella believed in output, and the man could write.

You may have read that W. P. Kinsella died last week. He wrote [_Shoeless Joe_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoeless_Joe_(novel)), the novel that became the movie "[Field of Dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_Dreams)."

I spent a week with Bill, as he liked to be called, in 1989. He was teaching at the Iowa Summer Writer’s Workshop, and I was in his small class on short fiction. So was Abraham Verghese, who, also last week, was [awarded](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2016/09/abraham-verghese-to-receive-national-humanities-medal.html) the National Humanities Medal. Years back, in this blog, I [wrote](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/in-practice/200902/his-own-country) about how Abe and I met en route to the workshop.

As a writer, I pride myself on being self-taught, but the time with Bill was formative.

He reminded us that writers read extensively and analytically. What works? How did the author pull it off?

He was a great collector of idiosyncratic approaches to narration. He read us a story told in an auctioneer’s chant. He introduced me to second-person fiction—a lesson I applied when writing Should You Leave? Of course, Shoeless Joe [famously](http://www.nytimes.com/2016/09/18/books/william-p-kinsella-author-of-shoeless-joe-dies-at-81.html) takes the magical realism of Latin American fiction and adapts it to the baseball yarn.

Bill taught that writers treat writing as a day job. They don't wait for inspiration.

For years, Bill helped support himself by supplying round-up reviews to a Canadian newspaper. He described his day. He would walk to the post office to pick up a stack of books. On his way home, he would catechize himself.

Bill, what are you?

A writer.

Bill, what do writers do?

They write.

He’d head straight to his desk.

Workshop teachers were required to meet individually with students and critique their work. Bill, who was notoriously [difficult](http://www.nytimes.com/2016/09/18/books/william-p-kinsella-author-of-shoeless-joe-dies-at-81.html), tried to duck that task. He was a writer. He protected his time.

I called him out. He reversed course and demonstrated his talent for attentive reading, helping me reshape material that would, in 12 years' time, form part of my novel, Spectacular [Happiness](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/happiness "Psychology Today looks at Happiness"). (Bill’s endorsement appears on the jacket of the hardcover.) He focused on function: How would the parts fit together? How would each passage move the reader?

I’ve read a half-dozen obituaries. They ignore or underplay that signal trait, his insistent, obsessive devotion to the craft. Bill believed in technical skill. Bill believed in output.

In 1997, Bill suffered an apparent brain injury when a car struck him. For 14 years or so, he gave up writing in favor of playing Scrabble, in tournaments. But to that point, he had been a writing machine.

One fact the obits missed: Bill held a world record. He had written more short stories on one topic, and with one set of characters, than any writer in history. (Previously, a French author had held the record.) The subject was not baseball. Bill had contributed endless comic pieces about native Americans on a “First Nations reserve.” Although in recent years he faced [criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._P._Kinsella) for “cultural appropriation,” he was proud of that run. Writers write.

However reluctant he was as a teacher, Bill Kinsella had extraordinary impact. I can’t think of anyone else who has influenced me so strongly in so short at time. He shaped my habits as a reader. He shaped my habits as a writer.

Since learning of Bill’s death, I’ve tried to pin down what he conveyed. At the core was a lesson about discipline and freedom. You indenture yourself to your readers, you lock yourself in your study, and within that [narrow room](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems-and-poets/poems/detail/52299), you let your [imagination](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/imagination "Psychology Today looks at imagination") range.

Postscript: A reader has directed my [attention](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/attention "Psychology Today looks at attention") to an [obituary](https://www.thestar.com/entertainment/books/2016/09/16/shoeless-joe-author-wp-kinsella-has-doctor-assisted-death.html) in the Toronto _Star_ that contains this familar-sounding quote, contributed by Bill's biographer, Willie Steele: "He would tell writing students that to become a writer, you don’t sit around and talk about writing, you don’t sit around and think about writing, you sit down and write."
