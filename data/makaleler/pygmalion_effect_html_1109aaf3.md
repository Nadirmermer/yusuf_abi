### Giriş

The Pygmalion Effect is a psychological phenomenon in which higher expectations placed on a person raise that person’s actual performance. Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson demonstrated it in their classic study,Pygmalion in the Classroom(1968).

### Pygmalion Effect Experiment

Pygmalion in the Classroom(1968) is a landmark sociological and psychological study conducted by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson.

The experiment investigated howlabelingandself-fulfilling propheciesimpact student performance.

### The Laboratory Origins: Rosenthal’s Rat Studies

Before the classroom study, Robert Rosenthal had already shown something striking. An experimenter’s own expectations can bias results. No deliberate dishonesty is required.

Student experimenters were each handed ordinary lab rats. Researchers told half of them their rats were “maze-bright.” They told the other half their rats were “maze-dull.”

The rats had actually been assigned at random. They did not differ at all. Yet the “maze-bright” rats learned mazes and discrimination tasks faster (Rosenthal & Fode, 1963). That gap needed an explanation.

The animals were identical, so the gap had to come from somewhere else. It came from how the experimenters handled and recorded them once they held an expectation. This is why Rosenthal and Jacobson’s later work is also the textbook example of the investigator effect.

### The Experiment Design

The study was conducted at a California elementary school to test the hypothesis that teacher expectations can induce performance gains even when those expectations are based on false information.

- The Deception: Researchers administered an IQ examination to all students but misled teachers by claiming the test was a specialized tool used to identify “intellectual bloomers”, students with high academic potential.

- Random Selection: The researchers ignored the actual test results. Instead, they used a table of random numbers to select approximately 20% of the student population.

- Prediction: They provided the names of these students to the teachers, identifying them as the “bloomers” who would show dramatic intellectual growth in the coming year.

- The Reality: There was no actual difference in potential between the “bloomers” and their peers; the distinction existed solely as a label in the minds of the teachers.

### Findings and Mechanisms

At the end of the school year, all students retook the test.

The results supported the existence of aself-fulfilling prophecy, an idea that becomes true because it is acted upon.

- IQ Gains: The students arbitrarily labeled as “bloomers” made significantly greater progress in IQ scores than the remaining 80% of the student body. The “blooming” group saw an average 12-point gain compared to an 8-point gain for the rest of the class.

- Age Significance: The effect was most pronounced among the youngest children. First graders in the experimental group gained over 27 IQ points (compared to 12 for the control), and second graders gained 16.5 points (compared to 7).

- The Psychological Mechanism: Rosenthal concluded that inconspicuous factors such as teacher attitude, mood, and subtle behavioral changes impact outcomes.

- Subtle Treatment: Teachers socialized these students into “high-achiever” roles by providing in-depth feedback and bestowing more attention upon them.

### Criticisms of the Original Study

Pygmalion in the Classroom reshaped debates about teacher bias and inequality. It also drew sharp criticism. Here are the main concerns:

- Unreliable Measurement:Thorndike (1968) found the IQ test’s baseline scores implausible, which let small changes look like dramatic “gains.”

- Data Re-Analysis:Elashoff and Snow (1971) showed the headline effect rested on a handful of extreme scores in one or two grades.

- Small, Conditional Effect:Raudenbush’s (1984) meta-analysis of 18 experiments found a real but small effect that depends on timing.

- Naturalistic Accuracy:Brophy (1983) found that teachers’ everyday expectations are usually accurate, so self-fulfilling effects add only a modest increment.

- Individual Susceptibility:Younger children and less self-assured people are more susceptible; a strong internallocus of controlhelps people resist external labels.

- Reciprocity:Expectation and performance shape each other continuously, so isolating a clean one-way cause is difficult outside tightly controlled designs.

- Ethical Concerns:The study deceived teachers, and deliberately inducing damaging low expectations to test thegolem effectis not ethically possible.

Thorndike’s (1968) review targeted the study’s IQ instrument. Its reasoning subscale produced implausible pre-test scores. Some whole classes averaged “reasoning IQs” far below what any real cohort would score.

That made the baseline shaky from the start. Small, meaningless shifts on an unreliable test could then look like dramatic year-on-year “gains.”

Thorndike concluded the data were too weak to support the book’s conclusions. He predicted wide citation but little scrutiny, and he was right.

That is the whole critique in miniature. Every other finding in the book depends on that same baseline. If the pre-test scores cannot be trusted, neither can the reported IQ gain.

Elashoff and Snow (1971) re-examined the original data directly. They found the whole-school effect rested on a very small number of children.

Just one or two grades drove it. A handful of extreme, improbable scores did most of the work.

Once scrutinised, the evidence for a school-wide effect looked far weaker than the book implied. Their re-analysis does not deny the local effect happened.

It argues the book overstated how far that effect generalised. This is the scrutiny Thorndike predicted the study would never get.

Aim:Raudenbush (1984) asked how large teacher-expectancy effects on IQ really are. He also asked what makes them appear or vanish.

Method:He synthesised 18 experiments that each tried to replicate the Pygmalion design. He compared them by how much time had passed before the expectation was planted.

Results:The effects were real but small on average. They were substantial only when the expectation preceded a teacher’s own first impressions.

Conclusion:Two weeks of contact was enough to override the label. This limits how far the original numbers generalise.

Brophy (1983) reviewed real classrooms directly. Teachers’ everyday expectations, he found, are usually accurate. They are based on genuine differences between pupils.

Self-fulfilling effects add only a modest increment on top of that. Often it is just a few percent of the variance in achievement.

They matter most for a minority of rigid teachers, or unusually susceptible pupils. Brophy argued the bigger risk runs the other way.

He called it a “sustaining-expectation effect.” Teachers fail to notice genuine change in a pupil. That is the real danger, not manufactured ability.

### How the Pygmalion Effect works

The operation of the Pygmalion effect, similar to that of a self-fulfilling prophecy, can be understood as progressing through 4 stages in a cyclical pattern:

- Others’ Beliefs Shape Their Conduct:The expectations or labels held by an authority figure (such as a teacher or manager) dictate how they interact with an individual.

- Individuals Internalize the Expectations as Part of Their Self-Concept:This interaction acts as alooking-glass self, where we develop our personal sense of identity based on how we imagine we appear to others.

- Our Beliefs Impact Our Actions:Once we adopt these socially constructed beliefs about our own abilities, our subsequent behaviors begin to align with those expectations.

- Our Actions Reinforce Their Beliefs:Our performance confirms the authority figure’s original (and perhaps originally false) idea, thereby returning the cycle to the initial stage.

A central tenet of this effect is that external expectations can eventually influence behavior in a significant way.

In an educational or professional setting, this often manifests throughlabeling theory, where an individual “lives up” to an optimistic label or “lives down” to a negative one.

Consequently, low expectations can lead to poor performance, while optimistic expectations are likely to induce intellectual or professional growth.

Rosenthal and Rubin (1978) synthesized 345 such studies. They confirmed that interpersonal expectancy effects are real and widespread, though their size varied a great deal by setting.

### The “Ideal Pupil” and Social Class(Howard Becker, 1971)

Sociologist Howard Becker provided an example of how the Pygmalion Effect operates based on social class stereotypes rather than test scores.

- The Expectation:Through interviews with 60 Chicago teachers, Becker found that teachers operated with a mental image of the “ideal pupil,” whom they perceived as white, middle-class, and female.

- The Interaction:Teachers saw working-class students as furthest from this ideal and viewed them as hard to control. They lowered their standards, settling for “just the basics.”

- The Result:Middle-class students got positive reinforcement, built confidence, and worked harder. Working-class students got negative feedback, grew demotivated, and underachieved, confirming the teachers’ low expectations.

### Gender Differences in Teacher Expectations

The Pygmalion Effect has historically played a role in gender socialization.

Studies have shown that teachers often devoted more time and attention to boys, asking them more questions and praising them more frequently.

This differential treatment can signal to girls that they are less suited for subjects like math and science, potentially dampening their competitiveness and shaping their future career choices.

### Kindergarten Labeling(Ray Rist, 1970)

Ray Rist’s study of a kindergarten class provides a specific example of how early labeling creates a trajectory of achievement or failure.

- The Grouping:By the eighth day of school, the teacher had divided the children into three tables. This decision was based not on ability tests, but on the teacher’s judgment of the children’s appearance and home background.

- The Treatment:The “Tigers”:This table consisted of middle-class, neat-appearing children. They were seated closest to the teacher and received the most encouragement and high-level instruction.The “Cardinals” and “Clowns”:These tables were comprised of working-class children. They were seated further away, given lower-level books, and received less attention.

### Race and Teacher Expectations

The effect also applies to race. Teachers may unconsciously condition themselves to view certain minority groups as less motivated or more threatening.

For instance, research has shown that teachers may underestimate the academic ability of African-Caribbean pupils or view their behavior as aggressive, leading to lower academic placement and achievement.

A positive example of the Pygmalion Effect, termed “stereotype promise,” is observed in the treatment of Asian American students.

- The Stereotype:Jennifer Lee and Min Zhou (2014) found that teachers often hold the positive stereotype that Asian American students are part of a “model minority” and are innately high achievers.

- The Effect:This label makes students more likely to be placed in Advanced Placement classes, get higher grades, and receive favorable treatment. Average students can then outperform their prior potential.

### Gender and “Invisibility”

Research by Spender (1983) provides examples of how the Pygmalion Effect can disadvantage girls.

- The Expectation:Spender (1983) found that teachers asked boys more questions, gave them more feedback, and expected them to have careers, while underestimating girls’ ambitions.

- The Result:Even bright girls were often described as likely to become “personal assistants” rather than leaders. Consequently, girls became “invisible” in the classroom, blending into the background and lowering their own academic assertiveness to match the lower expectations placed upon them.

### Management and Leadership

The same mechanism operates in workplaces. A manager’s expectations shape who gets challenging assignments and visible opportunities.

This is a recurring theme in the field. Reviews of work motivation treat it as genuine, not incidental (Mitchell & Daniels, 2003).

A manager who believes in an employee tends to coach them more. They also explain decisions more fully.

Dov Eden’s field experiments carried the effect directly into organisations. He raised leaders’ expectations of subordinates, often by telling supervisors that certain workers had “high potential.”

That single false label improved those subordinates’ objective performance (Eden, 1990). The pattern mirrors the climate and input channels Rosenthal identified in classrooms. Belief did the work.

An employee rises, or falls, to meet whatever level of investment they are shown.

### The Military

Some of the strongest field evidence comes from the armed forces, not the classroom. The setting is controlled and high-stakes.

In “Pygmalion goes to boot camp,” instructors on an Israeli Defense Forces combat course were told something specific. Certain randomly selected trainees supposedly had unusually high “command potential.”

Those trainees scored higher on objective achievement tests. They also scored higher on expert evaluations and on their own attitudes (Eden & Shani, 1982).

No such expectation had been induced for the comparison trainees. That single difference explains the gap.

This is one of the most persuasive real-world demonstrations of the effect. A boot camp lets researchers control who trains whom. That control is exactly what makes the result so hard to explain away.

### Sport Coaching

Coaches, like teachers, transmit expectations. They do it through attention, instruction, and constructive feedback given to each athlete.

Players a coach visibly expects little of tend to receive lower-quality coaching. They often conform to that low estimate over a season.

Expectation becomes performance on the field, too. Rosenthal and Babad (1985) extended the analysis explicitly to physical education and the gymnasium.

They showed the same four expectancy channels at work in sport. The same four channels transfer directly onto the pitch. Climate, input, output, and feedback all operate on a training pitch exactly as they do in a classroom.

A coach’s tone, word choice, and the drills assigned can all trace back to an expectation formed long before a single match is played.

### Healthcare

Staff expectations about a patient’s capacity to recover can shape how much they encourage and involve that patient. This in turn affects engagement and outcomes.

The same loop plays out here, too. A clinician who expects a rehabilitation patient to regain function tends to set more ambitious targets.

They also offer warmer encouragement, the same climate and input channels seen in the classroom. Low expectations work the other way.

They can quietly translate into less rehabilitative effort and a slower recovery. That is a golem effect playing out on the ward.

The mechanism overlaps with the expectancy dynamics Rosenthal first documented in experimenters. An assessor who expects a patient to do poorly may elicit exactly that through subtle cues.

That is why structured, criterion-based assessment matters in care.

### 1. Cultivate High Expectations and Reject Biases

The core of the effect is the belief that an individual possesses high potential, regardless of past performance or background.

- The Power of Belief:Research shows that when teachers expect students to improve, those students often show greater growth than their peers. This holds even when the expectation is based on false information.

- Reject Group Stereotypes:Successful application requires actively rejectingstereotypes,oversimplified generalizations about groups of people based on race, ethnicity, or class.

- Challenge Occupational Sorting:Avoid assuming potential based onoccupational sorting, where society traditionally funnels certain genders or races into specific roles.

### 2. Modify Interpersonal Interactions

When authority figures believe individuals are high-potential, they subtly change their behavior. To replicate this effect, one should:

- Improve the Socio-Emotional Climate:Offer more positive non-verbal cues, such as smiling and nodding, to create a supportive environment.

- Increase Academic Input:Set higher goals and teach more challenging material rather than “dumbing down” content.

- Grant Response Opportunities:Exhibitpatienceby giving individuals more time to answer questions and calling on them more frequently.

- Provide Differentiated Feedback:Offer detailed, genuine praise and helpful redirection rather than simple criticism.

### 3. Influence Self-Concept through the “Looking-Glass Self”

The mechanism behind the Pygmalion effect is the transformation of the individual’s self-image.

- The Looking-Glass Self:Individuals build their self-concept from how they believe others see them. Treating someone as capable helps them internalize that identity.

- Counter Negative Labeling:Avoid deviant labels like “troublemaker.” Labeling theory warns that a labeled person may start to fulfill that role.

### 4. Remove Institutional Barriers

The effect can be applied at an organizational level by eliminating structures that signal low expectations.

- Avoid Rigid Tracking:Tracking sorts students onto fixed “low achiever” paths. These students then get less individual attention and live down to the lower expectations set for them.

- Promote Universal Access:Ensureuniversal access, providing equal ability for all people to participate in the education system regardless of disabilities or socioeconomic status.

### The Myth and the Name

Robert Rosenthal identified the effect through a study conducted in 1964. He published it with Lenore Jacobson in their 1968 book,Pygmalion in the Classroom.

They took the name from Ovid’sMetamorphoses.

In that Greek myth, a Cypriot sculptor named Pygmalion carves an ivory statue. It is so beautiful that he falls in love with his own creation.

He pleads with the gods for a wife identical to the statue. They respond by bringing the ivory figure to life.

George Bernard Shaw mirrored this theme centuries later. His 1912 play,Pygmalion, tells a similar story.

Its flower girl, Eliza Doolittle, captures the idea directly. “The difference between a lady and a flower girl,” she says, “is not how she behaves, but how she’s treated.”

She stays a flower girl to Professor Higgins because that is how he views and treats her. Yet she could be a lady to anyone who treated her with that expectation instead.

### The Sociological Roots: Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

Sociologically, the Pygmalion effect is a manifestation of aself-fulfilling prophecy. Sociologist Robert K. Merton defined this idea.

Even a false belief can become true once people act on it. TheThomas theoremcame first.

It states that “if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.” Merton built directly on this.

Once people treat a belief as true, their actions can make its consequences real. This holds whether or not the belief itself was ever accurate.

The same loop shows up outside education, too. In one study, men believed from a photo that a woman on the phone was attractive.

They spoke to her more warmly as a result. She responded in kind, seeming exactly as sociable as they had expected (Snyder, Tanke, & Berscheid, 1977).

Expectation, behaviour, and outcome close into a loop. It works whether or not the original belief was ever true.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:The Pygmalion effect is a self-fulfilling prophecy: higher expectations from a teacher, manager, or coach raise a person’s actual performance.

- 1968 Study:Rosenthal and Jacobson randomly labelled 20% of pupils as “intellectual bloomers”; those pupils gained more IQ than their classmates by year’s end.

- Grade Effect:The gain was largest in the youngest pupils, who had the least prior contact with their teacher.

- Four Channels:Teachers transmit expectations through climate, input, output, and feedback.

- Golem Effect:Low expectations work the same way in reverse, lowering performance instead of raising it.

- Small But Real:A later meta-analysis of 18 experiments found the effect is genuine but modest, and depends on how early the expectation is planted.

- Beyond School:The same mechanism shows up in management, the military, sport coaching, and healthcare.
