The Strange Situation Experiment is a study by psychologist Mary Ainsworth that measures how infants respond to separations and reunions with their caregiver. It helps identify different attachment styles, like secure, avoidant, or anxious, based on how the child reacts when the caregiver leaves and returns.

### Key Takeaways

- Devised in the Late 1960s:Mary Ainsworth and Barbara Wittig designed the Strange Situation to observe how securely an infant is attached to a caregiver (Ainsworth & Wittig, 1969).

- Eight Short Episodes:Over roughly 20 minutes, the mother and a stranger enter and leave the room in a set sequence, creating brief separations and reunions for the infant.

- Three Attachment Patterns:How the infant reacts at reunion sorts them into secure, insecure-avoidant, or insecure-resistant (ambivalent) attachment; a fourth pattern, disorganized, was added later (Main & Solomon, 1990).

- Maternal Sensitivity Matters Most:Ainsworth’s caregiver-sensitivity hypothesis holds that a mother’s prompt, sensitive responsiveness to her infant’s signals predicts secure attachment better than any single other factor.

- Still Debated:The Strange Situation remains developmental psychology’s most-used attachment measure, but critics question whether a 20-minute lab session reflects real-world caregiving and whether it applies equally across cultures.

- Modern Evidence:A 2023 meta-analysis of over 20,000 Strange Situation sessions found secure attachment the most common pattern worldwide, though rates vary with socioeconomic risk and culture (Madigan et al., 2023).

### Background

The Strange Situation is a structured observational method developed by psychologist Mary Ainsworth to measure how securely an infant is attached to their caregiver.

Initially, Ainsworth observed infant behavior naturally within their homes.

In her early studies in Uganda (Africa), infants clearly demonstrated attachment behaviors. They used their mothers as a safe base for exploring and showed visible distress when separated or approached by strangers.

However, later studies conducted in Baltimore showed that American babies were less clearly demonstrating these behaviors, likely because their familiar home environments didn’t trigger attachment behaviors as intensely.

Recognizing this, Ainsworth developed a controlled, yet slightly unfamiliar scenario designed to reliably activate infants’ natural attachment behaviors.

This became known as the Strange Situation.

It created intensified versions of common situations, including brief separations from caregivers, interactions with a stranger, and reunions with caregivers, to vividly illustrate attachment styles.

The Strange Situation provides insight into a child’s expectations about their caregiver’s availability, essentially how secure they feel based on previous interactions.

It’s often described as a “laboratory microcosm”: a simplified environment that lets researchers clearly observe the balance infants strike. On one side sits their need for comfort and safety; on the other, their drive to explore.

### Strange Situation Procedure

Ainsworth and Barbara Wittig designed the strange situation to reveal how one- to two-year-olds use a caregiver as a secure base under mild stress (Ainsworth & Wittig, 1969).

Ainsworth and Bell (1970) then used the procedure to study the balance between attachment and exploration in around 100 middle-class American infants and their mothers.

The procedure was designed to be novel enough to spark exploration, but not so strange that it would trigger fear before the study began.

The lab room held a clear 9 x 9-foot floor space, divided into 16 squares for recording the infant’s location and movement.

Toys sat at one end; chairs for the mother and a stranger sat at the other. The mother and stranger were briefed on their roles beforehand, and the baby was placed centrally, free to move.

Over 20 minutes, the mother and a stranger entered and left the room in a set sequence. This recreated the mix of familiar and unfamiliar presence in a young child’s everyday life.

Ainsworth and Bell observed from behind a one-way mirror, so the infants never knew they were being watched.

The infant’s behavior was observed during eight pre-determined episodes, most lasting about three minutes each.

A caregiver who responds unpredictably, sometimes soothing the child’s distress and sometimes ignoring it, raises the odds of an insecure-ambivalent attachment. These children show heightened anxiety and mix dependence with resistance toward their caregiver.When a caregiver frequently neglects, ignores, or rejects the child’s emotional needs, the infant tends to develop an insecure-avoidant attachment instead. These infants become emotionally distant and self-reliant, having learned that signaling their needs rarely changes their caregiver’s behavior.Internal Working ModelsThese early attachment experiences profoundly influence a child’sinternal working models– the mental frameworks that guide their expectations and interactions in later relationships (Bowlby, 1969).Securely attachedchildren generally perceive themselves positively, as deserving of care and respect, and view others as supportive and reliable (Jacobsen & Hoffman, 1997).Avoidantly attachedchildren tend to internalize rejection, developing negative self-images and assuming they are unworthy of affection or attention (Larose & Bernier, 2001).Ambivalently attachedchildren frequently exaggerate their emotional responses and have inconsistent self-perceptions due to the unpredictability of their caregivers’ support (Kobak et al., 1993).Ultimately, Ainsworth’s findings provided essential empirical support for Bowlby’s attachment theory, highlighting the critical importance of sensitive and responsive caregiving.This research underscores how early interactions profoundly shape emotional health, relationship expectations, and social development throughout life.Theoretical Evaluation1. culture biasThe Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) has faced criticism for being culturally biased, often described as a culture-bound test.It was initially created by American-Canadian psychologist Mary Ainsworth, drawing from British psychiatrist John Bowlby’s attachment theory, reflecting predominantly Western norms and caregiving styles.Using such culturally specific approaches universally is known as an imposed etic, which can lead to misunderstandings and inaccurate assessments across diverse cultural groups.The cultural meanings attached to separation and reunion vary significantly worldwide, calling into question the ecological validity (real-world accuracy) of the SSP.Why is the Strange Situation Culturally Biased?Different Childhood Experiences:Childhood experiences vary widely across cultures, shaping how children react in situations like the SSP. What seems typical or “normal” in one culture might be unusual or stressful in another.Varied Caregiving Practices:Caregiving styles differ greatly around the world, directly influencing children’s reactions during separations and reunions. Practices considered appropriate and nurturing in one culture might appear insensitive or neglectful in another.Diverse Cultural Views on Emotional Expression:Cultures differ significantly in how they view emotional expression. Some encourage emotional openness and expressiveness, while others value emotional control and restraint, impacting how children show their feelings during tests like the SSP.Real-World Cross-Cultural Examples:Japan:Researcher Takahashi discovered the SSP didn’t effectively measure attachment in Japan due to traditional caregiving practices emphasizing constant closeness – such as co-sleeping, co-bathing, and always carrying the infant.Short separations during the SSP caused significant distress, sometimes resulting in securely attached Japanese infants being misclassified as ambivalent or resistant because of their intense reactions.This misclassification showed the SSP’s limited applicability to traditional Japanese caregiving contexts.Germany:Grossmann and colleagues found that in northern Germany, mothers often encouraged independence early in life. Many infants displayed behaviors classified by the SSP as “avoidant” attachment, such as minimal distress when separated and low-key reunions.However, these behaviors reflect culturally valued independence rather than insecure attachment. The SSP, therefore, misinterpreted culturally appropriate behaviors as problematic, exemplifying cultural bias.Uganda (Ganda Infants):Observations revealed culturally specific greeting behaviors among Ganda infants, such as hand-clapping, which differed from typical Western greetings like hugging and kissing.These differences illustrate how culturally influenced behaviors might be misunderstood or misclassified within the SSP, highlighting its limited cultural sensitivity.Beyond these individual studies, van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) pooled Strange Situation classifications from 32 samples across eight countries, the largest cross-cultural test of Ainsworth’s categories to date. Their analysis remains the founding evidence base for the debate over how universal the classifications really are.2. caregiver sensitivity theoryThis caregiver sensitivity theory is supported by research from De Wolff and van IJzendoorn (1997), who conducted a meta-analysis (a review) of research into attachment types.They found that there is a relatively weak correlation of 0.24 between parental sensitivity and attachment type – generally more sensitive parents had securely attached children.However, in evaluation, critics of this theory argue that the correlation between parental sensitivity and the child’s attachment type is only weak.Other factors may better explain why children develop different attachment types. Critics argue that maternal-sensitivity theory places too much emphasis on the mother alone.Focusing just on maternal sensitivity when explaining why children have different attachment types is, therefore, a reductionist approach.3. temperamentAn alternative theory proposed by Kagan (1984) suggests that the temperament of the child is actually what leads to the different attachment types.Children with different innate (inborn) temperaments will have different attachment types.Fox (1989) supported this theory. Babies with an “easy” temperament, meaning they eat and sleep regularly and accept new experiences, are likely to develop secure attachments.Babies with a ‘slow to warm up’ temperament (those who took a while to get used to new experiences) are likely to have insecure-avoidant attachments.Babies with a ‘Difficult’ temperament (those who eat and sleep irregularly and who reject new experiences) are likely to haveinsecure-ambivalent attachments.In conclusion, the most complete explanation of why children develop different attachment types would be an interactionist theory.This would argue that a child’s attachment type is a result of a combination of factors – both the child’s innate temperament and their parent’s sensitivity towards their needs.4. interactionist theoryBelsky and Rovine (1987) propose an interactionist theory to explain the different attachment types.They argue that the child’s attachment type is a result of both the child’s innate temperament and also how the parent responds to them (i.e., the parents’ sensitivity level).The child’s innate temperament may also influence how their parent responds to them, shaping the sensitivity the parent shows.To develop a secure attachment, a ‘difficult’ child would need a caregiver who is sensitive and patient for a secure attachment to develop.5. Meta-analysisMadigan et al. (2023) conducted the largest meta-analysis of the Strange Situation to date: 285 studies and over 20,000 infant-caregiver pairs. Globally, they estimated attachment as secure (51.6%), avoidant (14.7%), resistant (10.2%), and disorganized (23.5%), with no differences by child age, sex, or parent gender.Distributions did differ by other factors. Families facing socioeconomic risk showed higher rates of avoidant and disorganized attachment, and maltreated children had far higher rates of disorganized attachment (64%) than non-maltreated children (22%).Infants in foster or adoptive care showed less avoidant but more disorganized attachment than infants raised by biological parents.Avoidant attachment also declined over time across studies, perhaps reflecting changing parenting styles or measurement methods. Asia, Africa, and South America each showed distributions that departed from the North American norm.Madigan et al. treat the 51.6% global secure rate as strong evidence that secure attachment is most likely when a family faces few stressors and risks.They caution, however, that more research is needed on cultural differences and on how well the SSP performs across diverse groups. Socioeconomic disadvantage and trauma both disrupt secure attachment formation.Methodological Evaluation1. reliabilityThe strange situation classification has been found to have good reliability.  This means that it achieves consistent results.For example, a study conducted in Germany found that 78% of the children were classified in the same way at ages 1 and 6 years (Wartner et al., 1994).2. validityEcological ValidityThe Strange Situation is the most widely used method for assessing infant attachment (Melhuish, 1993), but Lamb et al. (1985) criticized it as highly artificial and lacking ecological validity.The child faces a strange environment, and the mother and stranger follow a fixed script of eight timed episodes that would rarely happen in real life.I have been quite disappointed that so many attachment researchers have gone on to do research with the Strange Situation rather than looking at what happens at the home or in other natural settings—like I said before, it marks a turning away from “field work,” and I don’t think it’s wise. (Ainsworth & Marvin, 1995, p. 12).The artificial setting may not activate the attachment system in every infant, so some children could be misclassified (Ziv & Hotam, 2015). Avoidant infants, for instance, may not feel genuine stress when separated in this unfamiliar room, and little evidence links their avoidant behavior to physical markers of stress.SSP classifications also show only modest links to expected correlates like maternal sensitivity.Population ValidityThe original study also lacks population validity: it used only American infants from middle-class families. Its findings describe how that specific group behaves and cannot be generalized to the wider population or toother cultures, whose caregiving expectations may differ.In Germany, for example, parents encourage independence early, so children there show less enthusiastic reunion behavior than the American norm the SSP was built on.Validity Across Relationships and OccasionsAinsworth herself noted a further limit: the Strange Situation identifies a child’s attachment to one caregiver, usually the mother, not a general attachment style. A child may show a different attachment pattern with their father or grandmother (Lamb, 1977).Attachment behavior can also shift across occasions. A securely attached child may look insecure if the mother becomes ill or family circumstances change, so a single Strange Situation session captures only one moment in a changing relationship.3. ethicsThe strange situation has also been criticized on ethical grounds.Because the child is put under stress (separation and stranger anxiety), the study has broken theethical guidelinesfor the protection of participants.However, in its defense, the separation episodes were curtailed prematurely if the child became too stressed.Also, according to Marrone (1998), although the Strange Situation has been criticized for being stressful, it simulates everyday experiences, as mothers leave their babies for brief periods in different settings and often with unfamiliar people such as babysitters.4. categorical measurementThe categorical approach to classification may be too reductive to fully capture the complexity of infant attachment patterns (Ziv & Hotam, 2015).Reducing attachment security to four rigid categories treats the SSP as a “weigh scale” reflecting a “true score” when attachment is likely more continuous and multidimensional.This oversimplification could hamper the refinement ofattachment theoryif inconsistencies are attributed to limitations of the theory rather than the measure.Infant attachment is complex, with individual differences likely being continuous rather than falling neatly into discrete categories.Who Was Mary Ainsworth?Mary Dinsmore Salter Ainsworth (1913–1999) was the American-Canadian developmental psychologist who turned attachment theory into an empirical science. She devised the Strange Situation and, through decades of naturalistic observation, identified the secure, avoidant, and resistant attachment patterns it measures.Early Life and TrainingAinsworth was born Mary Dinsmore Salter on 1 December 1913 in Glendale, Ohio, the eldest of three daughters in a bookish, achievement-minded family whose parents took it for granted that their daughters would attend university. In 1918 her father’s manufacturing firm transferred him to Toronto, and she grew up Canadian.At about fifteen she read William McDougall’sCharacter and the Conduct of Lifeand decided that psychology was what she wanted to do.She entered the University of Toronto’s honours psychology programme at sixteen, taking her BA in 1935, her MA in 1936, and her PhD in 1939.Her supervisor, William Blatz, taught “security theory”: the idea that a child who can rely on parents gains a base of familial security from which to explore, learn, and take risks.Her dissertation,An evaluation of adjustment based upon the concept of security(Salter, 1940), built appraisal scales for that same construct in young adults.In 1942 she left a junior teaching post at Toronto to join the Canadian Women’s Army Corps, serving four years in personnel selection and clinical assessment and reaching the rank of major.After the war she returned to Toronto as an assistant professor teaching personality assessment, becoming expert enough in projective testing to co-author a standard reference work on the Rorschach technique (Klopfer, Ainsworth, Klopfer, & Holt, 1954).From Bowlby’s Tavistock Team to UgandaIn late 1950, Ainsworth and her husband Leonard moved to London so he could complete his doctorate at University College. Looking for work, she answered a newspaper advertisement for a research post at the Tavistock Clinic, on a team led by John Bowlby.The team studied how early separation from a mother affects personality development. Taking the post was, as she later admitted, sheer chance, and it became one of psychology’s most consequential job applications.At the Tavistock she absorbed Bowlby’s emerging ethological thinking and, just as importantly, the disciplined naturalistic observation practiced by her colleague James Robertson, whose methods she took as a model for the rest of her career.She also co-authored the unit’s follow-up study of children separated from their mothers by early sanatorium stays (Bowlby, Ainsworth, Boston, & Rosenbluth, 1956).At the end of 1953 the Ainsworths moved to Kampala, Uganda, where Leonard had taken a post at the East African Institute of Social Research and Mary secured a research position of her own.Over nine months she visited 26 Ganda families with unweaned babies near Kampala, observing each mother and infant for two hours every fortnight in the family living room, working with an interpreter.It became the first empirical field study of infant attachment: she watched attachment emerge, described the baby’s use of the mother as a secure base for exploration, and distinguished securely attached, insecurely attached, and not-yet-attached infants.Publication was delayed for years; the monographInfancy in Ugandafinally appeared in 1967 (Ainsworth, 1967).The Baltimore Study and the Strange SituationIn 1955 the Ainsworths settled in Baltimore, and in 1956 Mary joined Johns Hopkins University, combining teaching with clinical diagnostic work; she became professor of developmental psychology in 1963.That year she launched the study that defines her career: the Baltimore project, a replication in depth of her Uganda fieldwork. Twenty-six families were recruited before their babies were born and visited at home for roughly four hours every three weeks across the whole first year.That amounted to about 72 hours of observation per family, with every feeding, cry, cuddle, and separation narrated into longhand records.To see how that year of home care shaped the infant’s behavior under stress, she and Barbara Wittig designed the Strange Situation (Ainsworth & Wittig, 1969).The reunion behaviors proved decisive, sorting the infants into secure, avoidant, and resistant patterns, and the home records showed that mothers of the secure infants had been reliably more sensitive, accepting, cooperative, and accessible across the first year.The full account of the procedure, its A/B/C classification system, and the caregiver-sensitivity findings appeared inPatterns of Attachment(Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters, & Wall, 1978), written with three of her students and often called the single most influential study in attachment research.Later Career and LegacyAinsworth moved to the University of Virginia in 1975 and was named Commonwealth Professor of Psychology in 1976, teaching until her retirement in 1984 and remaining active as professor emerita for another decade.Her later writing widened the lens beyond infancy: a synthesis of the Baltimore findings for a general audience in 1979, and a theoretical account of attachments beyond infancy to fathers, siblings, friends, and partners across the life course in 1989.The year after Bowlby’s death, she published a joint retrospective on their four-decade partnership (Ainsworth & Bowlby, 1991).Her honors included the American Psychological Association’s Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award (1989) and the American Psychological Foundation’s Gold Medal for Life Achievement (1998), alongside the Association’s G. Stanley Hall Award, earned in 1984.She died in Charlottesville, Virginia, on 21 March 1999, at age 85, following a stroke. ### Observational Scoring

In the Strange Situation procedure, trained observers closely monitored specific infant behaviors to understand their attachment patterns.

Observers recorded how infants explored, responded when separated from their caregivers, reacted to strangers, and behaved during reunions.

### Observational Procedure

- Observations were conducted discretely through a one-way mirror, ensuring infants and mothers behaved naturally.

- Typically, two observers narrated continuous, detailed accounts into a two-channel tape recorder, marking behaviors every 15 seconds, synchronized with a timer’s click.

- During early observations (first 14 infants), one observer spoke into the recorder while the other wrote notes by hand. Later, for the next 33 infants, only one observer (Bell) narrated continuously.

- Afterwards, these recordings were carefully transcribed, combined, and coded for detailed analysis.

### Categories for Behavior Scoring

Behavioral categories allow the researcher to tally observations into pre-arranged groupings. It also makes the observations replicable, so the results have greater reliability.

Essentially, the general behaviors observed are categorized based on their purpose and meaning in the context of attachment.

These four classes of behavior were scored for interaction with the mother in episodes 2, 3, 5, and 8, and for interaction with the stranger in episodes 3, 4, and 7.

### 1. Proximity and contact seeking:

Behaviors involve active efforts such as purposeful approaching, climbing, gesturing (like reaching or leaning), partial approaches, and directed cries.

The criteria include the child’s initiative, persistence, and effectiveness in gaining (or regaining) contact or proximity.

The score reflects the intensity and nature of the child’s efforts across different episodes.

### 2. Contact maintaining:

Behaviors relevant after the baby has made physical contact, either self-initiated or otherwise.

They encompass clinging, embracing, resisting release through intensified clinging or turning back and reaching if contact is lost, and vocal protestations.

### 3. Avoidance of proximity and contact:

Interaction-avoiding behaviors apply in situations that typically incite approach or interaction, such as an adult entering or trying to attract the baby’s attention.

The behaviors indicative of avoidance include increasing distance, turning away, averting gaze, hiding the face, or ignoring the person, especially when the person is attempting to engage the child’s attention.

Unlike the resistance variable, which is often associated with anger, avoidance may have a neutral tone or reflect apprehension.

It may be seen as a defensive behavior that conceals feelings, possibly including resentment.

The coding for this variable distinguishes between the child’s interactions with the mother and a stranger.

### 4. Resistance to contact and comfort:

Assesses the child’s resistant behavior towards someone who attempts to interact or come into proximity.

Contact- and interaction-resisting behaviors include angry, conflicting attempts to repel the adult, wriggling to get down if picked up, or rejecting toys used by the adult to interact.

Other indications could be angry screams, thrashing about, pouting, irritable fussing, or showing petulance.

These resistant behaviors may alternate with efforts to maintain contact with the person being rejected.

### 5. Search behavior:

During episodes 4, 6, and 7, search behaviors were recorded.

These included following the mother to the door, attempting to open it, banging on it, keeping the focus on the door or glancing towards it, approaching the mother’s empty chair, or observing it.

These behaviors indicated the infant’s active search or orientation towards the last seen location of the absent mother (usually the door) or a place associated with her in the unfamiliar setting (her chair).

Observers rated each behavior every 15 seconds, using a clear intensity scale from 1 (very weak or minimal) to 7 (strong or highly intense). Scores considered:

- Strength:How strongly or vigorously the baby showed a behavior.

- Frequency:How often the behavior occurred.

- Duration:How long the behavior lasted.

- Latency:How quickly or slowly the behavior began after a specific event.

- Type:Active behaviors (like reaching or crawling) scored higher than passive signals (like quiet vocalizations or facial expressions).

### Results [Attachment Styles]

Ainsworth and her colleagues (1978) identified three mainattachment styles, secure (type B), insecure avoidant (type A), and insecure ambivalent/resistant (type C).

She concluded that these attachment styles resulted from early interactions with the mother.

A fourth attachment style, known asdisorganized, was later identified (Main, & Solomon, 1990).

### B: Secure Attachment

Secure attachment was the most commonly observed attachment type in Mary Ainsworth’s original Strange Situation studies.

It describes children who have developed a strong sense of trust and comfort with their caregivers, typically due to caregivers consistently responding sensitively to their needs.

According to attachment theoristJohn Bowlby(1980), securely attached individuals hold an internal belief or expectation (representational model) that their attachment figures are “available, responsive, and helpful” (p. 242).

Similarly, Main and Cassidy (1988) noted that securely attached children confidently rely on their caregiver as a “safe base” from which to explore and return to for comfort during stressful situations.

### General Characteristics of Securely Attached Infants:

- Confident Exploration:They confidently explore their environment when the caregiver is present.

- Moderate Separation Distress:They experience moderate distress when separated from their caregiver.

- Moderate Stranger Anxiety:They typically show moderate anxiety around a stranger.

### A: Insecure Avoidant

Avoidant attachmentis a pattern observed in some infants, characterized by emotional distance, independence, and minimal visible reliance on caregivers.

Infants classified as having avoidant attachment typically showed little reaction to their caregiver’s departures or returns and displayed minimal difference in behavior toward strangers and caregivers.

- Independent Exploration:Avoidant infants often appear independent both physically and emotionally, exploring their environment without seeking reassurance from their caregiver.

- Minimal Separation Distress:They show minimal or no distress when separated from their caregiver; any distress shown usually comes from being left alone, not the caregiver’s absence specifically.

### C: Insecure Ambivalent / Resistant

Ambivalent (also called resistant) attachmentdescribes children who experience mixed feelings of dependency and anger toward their caregiver due to inconsistent emotional caregiving.

### General Behavioral Characteristics:

Children with ambivalent attachment typically exhibit behaviors characterized by anxiety, dependency, and conflicting emotions toward their caregivers. Specifically, these children:

- Clingy Exploration:They appear clingy and anxious in new or unfamiliar environments, showing reluctance or inability to explore freely.

- Intense Separation Anxiety:They become intensely distressed and visibly upset when separated from their caregiver.

- Stranger Anxiety:They display fear or anxiety when encountering unfamiliar people.

- Mixed Reunion Response:They show mixed emotional responses during reunions, initially seeking comfort but then resisting or rejecting the caregiver’s efforts to comfort them.

- Difficult to Soothe:They are generally difficult to soothe when upset, reflecting underlying insecurity about caregiver responsiveness.

### Example of Ambivalent Attachment Behavior:

When the mother leaves briefly, the child panics and becomes extremely distressed, crying loudly and showing clear anxiety.

Upon the mother’s return, the child initially seeks comfort but quickly becomes frustrated or even angry, pushing her away, resisting physical comfort, or continuing to fuss despite attempts to soothe them.

Ambivalent attachment typically emergesdue to caregivers who are inconsistent in responding to their child’s emotional needs.

Sometimes these caregivers may be responsive and comforting, while at other times, they might ignore or misinterpret their child’s signals.

Because the child never knows what response to expect, they become anxious and insecure, leading to the conflicting behaviors observed in the Strange Situation.

### Conclusion

A child’s sense of emotional security and trust in relationships develops significantly during their first year of life, shaped primarily by the caregiver’s responsiveness.

Ainsworth’s (1978) caregiver-sensitivity hypothesis holds that mothers who consistently and promptly respond to an infant’s cries, gestures, and facial expressions foster secure attachment. These infants come to trust that their caregiver will be accessible, responsive, and reliable even when physically apart (Stayton & Ainsworth, 1973).

Inconsistent or insensitive caregiving, by contrast, tends to produce insecure attachment. A caregiver who responds unpredictably, sometimes soothing the child’s distress and sometimes ignoring it, raises the odds of an insecure-ambivalent attachment. These children show heightened anxiety and mix dependence with resistance toward their caregiver.

When a caregiver frequently neglects, ignores, or rejects the child’s emotional needs, the infant tends to develop an insecure-avoidant attachment instead. These infants become emotionally distant and self-reliant, having learned that signaling their needs rarely changes their caregiver’s behavior.

### Internal Working Models

These early attachment experiences profoundly influence a child’sinternal working models– the mental frameworks that guide their expectations and interactions in later relationships (Bowlby, 1969).

- Securely attachedchildren generally perceive themselves positively, as deserving of care and respect, and view others as supportive and reliable (Jacobsen & Hoffman, 1997).

- Avoidantly attachedchildren tend to internalize rejection, developing negative self-images and assuming they are unworthy of affection or attention (Larose & Bernier, 2001).

- Ambivalently attachedchildren frequently exaggerate their emotional responses and have inconsistent self-perceptions due to the unpredictability of their caregivers’ support (Kobak et al., 1993).

Ultimately, Ainsworth’s findings provided essential empirical support for Bowlby’s attachment theory, highlighting the critical importance of sensitive and responsive caregiving.

This research underscores how early interactions profoundly shape emotional health, relationship expectations, and social development throughout life.

### 1. culture bias

The Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) has faced criticism for being culturally biased, often described as a culture-bound test.

It was initially created by American-Canadian psychologist Mary Ainsworth, drawing from British psychiatrist John Bowlby’s attachment theory, reflecting predominantly Western norms and caregiving styles.

Using such culturally specific approaches universally is known as an imposed etic, which can lead to misunderstandings and inaccurate assessments across diverse cultural groups.

The cultural meanings attached to separation and reunion vary significantly worldwide, calling into question the ecological validity (real-world accuracy) of the SSP.

- Different Childhood Experiences:Childhood experiences vary widely across cultures, shaping how children react in situations like the SSP. What seems typical or “normal” in one culture might be unusual or stressful in another.

- Varied Caregiving Practices:Caregiving styles differ greatly around the world, directly influencing children’s reactions during separations and reunions. Practices considered appropriate and nurturing in one culture might appear insensitive or neglectful in another.

- Diverse Cultural Views on Emotional Expression:Cultures differ significantly in how they view emotional expression. Some encourage emotional openness and expressiveness, while others value emotional control and restraint, impacting how children show their feelings during tests like the SSP.

### 2. caregiver sensitivity theory

This caregiver sensitivity theory is supported by research from De Wolff and van IJzendoorn (1997), who conducted a meta-analysis (a review) of research into attachment types.

They found that there is a relatively weak correlation of 0.24 between parental sensitivity and attachment type – generally more sensitive parents had securely attached children.

However, in evaluation, critics of this theory argue that the correlation between parental sensitivity and the child’s attachment type is only weak.

Other factors may better explain why children develop different attachment types. Critics argue that maternal-sensitivity theory places too much emphasis on the mother alone.

Focusing just on maternal sensitivity when explaining why children have different attachment types is, therefore, a reductionist approach.

### 3. temperament

An alternative theory proposed by Kagan (1984) suggests that the temperament of the child is actually what leads to the different attachment types.

Children with different innate (inborn) temperaments will have different attachment types.

Fox (1989) supported this theory. Babies with an “easy” temperament, meaning they eat and sleep regularly and accept new experiences, are likely to develop secure attachments.

Babies with a ‘slow to warm up’ temperament (those who took a while to get used to new experiences) are likely to have insecure-avoidant attachments.

Babies with a ‘Difficult’ temperament (those who eat and sleep irregularly and who reject new experiences) are likely to haveinsecure-ambivalent attachments.

In conclusion, the most complete explanation of why children develop different attachment types would be an interactionist theory.

This would argue that a child’s attachment type is a result of a combination of factors – both the child’s innate temperament and their parent’s sensitivity towards their needs.

### 4. interactionist theory

Belsky and Rovine (1987) propose an interactionist theory to explain the different attachment types.

They argue that the child’s attachment type is a result of both the child’s innate temperament and also how the parent responds to them (i.e., the parents’ sensitivity level).

The child’s innate temperament may also influence how their parent responds to them, shaping the sensitivity the parent shows.

To develop a secure attachment, a ‘difficult’ child would need a caregiver who is sensitive and patient for a secure attachment to develop.

### 5. Meta-analysis

Madigan et al. (2023) conducted the largest meta-analysis of the Strange Situation to date: 285 studies and over 20,000 infant-caregiver pairs. Globally, they estimated attachment as secure (51.6%), avoidant (14.7%), resistant (10.2%), and disorganized (23.5%), with no differences by child age, sex, or parent gender.

Distributions did differ by other factors. Families facing socioeconomic risk showed higher rates of avoidant and disorganized attachment, and maltreated children had far higher rates of disorganized attachment (64%) than non-maltreated children (22%).

Infants in foster or adoptive care showed less avoidant but more disorganized attachment than infants raised by biological parents.

Avoidant attachment also declined over time across studies, perhaps reflecting changing parenting styles or measurement methods. Asia, Africa, and South America each showed distributions that departed from the North American norm.

Madigan et al. treat the 51.6% global secure rate as strong evidence that secure attachment is most likely when a family faces few stressors and risks.

They caution, however, that more research is needed on cultural differences and on how well the SSP performs across diverse groups. Socioeconomic disadvantage and trauma both disrupt secure attachment formation.

### 1. reliability

The strange situation classification has been found to have good reliability.  This means that it achieves consistent results.

For example, a study conducted in Germany found that 78% of the children were classified in the same way at ages 1 and 6 years (Wartner et al., 1994).

### 2. validity

The Strange Situation is the most widely used method for assessing infant attachment (Melhuish, 1993), but Lamb et al. (1985) criticized it as highly artificial and lacking ecological validity.

The child faces a strange environment, and the mother and stranger follow a fixed script of eight timed episodes that would rarely happen in real life.

I have been quite disappointed that so many attachment researchers have gone on to do research with the Strange Situation rather than looking at what happens at the home or in other natural settings—like I said before, it marks a turning away from “field work,” and I don’t think it’s wise. (Ainsworth & Marvin, 1995, p. 12).

The artificial setting may not activate the attachment system in every infant, so some children could be misclassified (Ziv & Hotam, 2015). Avoidant infants, for instance, may not feel genuine stress when separated in this unfamiliar room, and little evidence links their avoidant behavior to physical markers of stress.

SSP classifications also show only modest links to expected correlates like maternal sensitivity.

The original study also lacks population validity: it used only American infants from middle-class families. Its findings describe how that specific group behaves and cannot be generalized to the wider population or toother cultures, whose caregiving expectations may differ.

In Germany, for example, parents encourage independence early, so children there show less enthusiastic reunion behavior than the American norm the SSP was built on.

Ainsworth herself noted a further limit: the Strange Situation identifies a child’s attachment to one caregiver, usually the mother, not a general attachment style. A child may show a different attachment pattern with their father or grandmother (Lamb, 1977).

Attachment behavior can also shift across occasions. A securely attached child may look insecure if the mother becomes ill or family circumstances change, so a single Strange Situation session captures only one moment in a changing relationship.

### 3. ethics

The strange situation has also been criticized on ethical grounds.

Because the child is put under stress (separation and stranger anxiety), the study has broken theethical guidelinesfor the protection of participants.

However, in its defense, the separation episodes were curtailed prematurely if the child became too stressed.

Also, according to Marrone (1998), although the Strange Situation has been criticized for being stressful, it simulates everyday experiences, as mothers leave their babies for brief periods in different settings and often with unfamiliar people such as babysitters.

### 4. categorical measurement

The categorical approach to classification may be too reductive to fully capture the complexity of infant attachment patterns (Ziv & Hotam, 2015).

Reducing attachment security to four rigid categories treats the SSP as a “weigh scale” reflecting a “true score” when attachment is likely more continuous and multidimensional.

This oversimplification could hamper the refinement ofattachment theoryif inconsistencies are attributed to limitations of the theory rather than the measure.

Infant attachment is complex, with individual differences likely being continuous rather than falling neatly into discrete categories.

### Who Was Mary Ainsworth?

Mary Dinsmore Salter Ainsworth (1913–1999) was the American-Canadian developmental psychologist who turned attachment theory into an empirical science. She devised the Strange Situation and, through decades of naturalistic observation, identified the secure, avoidant, and resistant attachment patterns it measures.

### Early Life and Training

Ainsworth was born Mary Dinsmore Salter on 1 December 1913 in Glendale, Ohio, the eldest of three daughters in a bookish, achievement-minded family whose parents took it for granted that their daughters would attend university. In 1918 her father’s manufacturing firm transferred him to Toronto, and she grew up Canadian.

At about fifteen she read William McDougall’sCharacter and the Conduct of Lifeand decided that psychology was what she wanted to do.

She entered the University of Toronto’s honours psychology programme at sixteen, taking her BA in 1935, her MA in 1936, and her PhD in 1939.

Her supervisor, William Blatz, taught “security theory”: the idea that a child who can rely on parents gains a base of familial security from which to explore, learn, and take risks.

Her dissertation,An evaluation of adjustment based upon the concept of security(Salter, 1940), built appraisal scales for that same construct in young adults.

In 1942 she left a junior teaching post at Toronto to join the Canadian Women’s Army Corps, serving four years in personnel selection and clinical assessment and reaching the rank of major.

After the war she returned to Toronto as an assistant professor teaching personality assessment, becoming expert enough in projective testing to co-author a standard reference work on the Rorschach technique (Klopfer, Ainsworth, Klopfer, & Holt, 1954).

### From Bowlby’s Tavistock Team to Uganda

In late 1950, Ainsworth and her husband Leonard moved to London so he could complete his doctorate at University College. Looking for work, she answered a newspaper advertisement for a research post at the Tavistock Clinic, on a team led by John Bowlby.

The team studied how early separation from a mother affects personality development. Taking the post was, as she later admitted, sheer chance, and it became one of psychology’s most consequential job applications.

At the Tavistock she absorbed Bowlby’s emerging ethological thinking and, just as importantly, the disciplined naturalistic observation practiced by her colleague James Robertson, whose methods she took as a model for the rest of her career.

She also co-authored the unit’s follow-up study of children separated from their mothers by early sanatorium stays (Bowlby, Ainsworth, Boston, & Rosenbluth, 1956).

At the end of 1953 the Ainsworths moved to Kampala, Uganda, where Leonard had taken a post at the East African Institute of Social Research and Mary secured a research position of her own.

Over nine months she visited 26 Ganda families with unweaned babies near Kampala, observing each mother and infant for two hours every fortnight in the family living room, working with an interpreter.

It became the first empirical field study of infant attachment: she watched attachment emerge, described the baby’s use of the mother as a secure base for exploration, and distinguished securely attached, insecurely attached, and not-yet-attached infants.

Publication was delayed for years; the monographInfancy in Ugandafinally appeared in 1967 (Ainsworth, 1967).

### The Baltimore Study and the Strange Situation

In 1955 the Ainsworths settled in Baltimore, and in 1956 Mary joined Johns Hopkins University, combining teaching with clinical diagnostic work; she became professor of developmental psychology in 1963.

That year she launched the study that defines her career: the Baltimore project, a replication in depth of her Uganda fieldwork. Twenty-six families were recruited before their babies were born and visited at home for roughly four hours every three weeks across the whole first year.

That amounted to about 72 hours of observation per family, with every feeding, cry, cuddle, and separation narrated into longhand records.

To see how that year of home care shaped the infant’s behavior under stress, she and Barbara Wittig designed the Strange Situation (Ainsworth & Wittig, 1969).

The reunion behaviors proved decisive, sorting the infants into secure, avoidant, and resistant patterns, and the home records showed that mothers of the secure infants had been reliably more sensitive, accepting, cooperative, and accessible across the first year.

The full account of the procedure, its A/B/C classification system, and the caregiver-sensitivity findings appeared inPatterns of Attachment(Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters, & Wall, 1978), written with three of her students and often called the single most influential study in attachment research.

### Later Career and Legacy

Ainsworth moved to the University of Virginia in 1975 and was named Commonwealth Professor of Psychology in 1976, teaching until her retirement in 1984 and remaining active as professor emerita for another decade.

Her later writing widened the lens beyond infancy: a synthesis of the Baltimore findings for a general audience in 1979, and a theoretical account of attachments beyond infancy to fathers, siblings, friends, and partners across the life course in 1989.

The year after Bowlby’s death, she published a joint retrospective on their four-decade partnership (Ainsworth & Bowlby, 1991).

Her honors included the American Psychological Association’s Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award (1989) and the American Psychological Foundation’s Gold Medal for Life Achievement (1998), alongside the Association’s G. Stanley Hall Award, earned in 1984.

She died in Charlottesville, Virginia, on 21 March 1999, at age 85, following a stroke. Her students, including Mary Main, went on to extend her classification system, adding the disorganized attachment category to the framework she built.
