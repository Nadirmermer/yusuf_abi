### Giriş

Sociology»Criminology

Deviance theory in sociology explores why individuals and groups violatesocial normsand the societal reactions to such violations. Deviance is defined as any behaviour, belief, or condition that deviates from a norm and elicits a negative reaction within a particular group. Sociologists generally consider deviance to be relative, meaning it is socially defined and can vary across cultures, time periods, and situations.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:Deviance is any behavior, belief, or characteristic that goes against social norms, ranging from minor infractions to serious crimes. It is defined by cultural context and can vary widely across societies.

- Types:Sociologists distinguish between formal deviance, which breaks laws, and informal deviance, which violates unwritten social rules. Both can provoke reactions from others or from institutions.

- Theories:Major sociological perspectives—such as Functionalism, Strain Theory, Labeling Theory, and Conflict Theory—offer different explanations for why deviance occurs and how society responds.

- Causes:Factors like social inequality, peer influence, cultural values, and structural opportunities can all contribute to deviant behavior. These influences often interact rather than act in isolation.

- Consequences:Deviance can lead to sanctions, stigma, or criminal penalties, but it can also challenge existing norms and spark social change. The same act may be judged differently depending on time and place.

### What is Deviant Behavior?

Deviance is a behavior, trait, or belief that departs from a social norm and generates a negative reaction in a particular group.

In other words, it is behavior that does not conform to the norms of a particular culture or society.

It includes those behaviors that attract negative responses and social controls. It also involves crimes committed in society.

What is considered acceptable or rude varies depending on the culture you are in. For example, eating with your left hand in Arab nations is considered rude.

Some behaviors are acceptable for certain age groups and some activities are illegal for some age groups.

For example, some people who engage in deviant behavior do so in order to challenge existing social norms and bring about change.

Additionally, deviance can also be a way for people to express themselves and their individuality.

### Formal Deviant Behavior

Formal deviant behavior is defined as behavior that violates formally enacted laws. This type of deviant behavior is often criminal in nature, and can result in punishments such as fines, imprisonment, or even death.

Examples of formal deviant behavior include but are not limited to: murder, robbery, assault, rape, and child molestation (Griffiths et al., 2012).

### Informal Deviant Behavior

Informal deviant behavior violates unwritten social norms rather than laws. It is usually seen as less serious than formal deviance and rarely brings legal punishment.

Instead, people who break these norms may be ridiculed or ostracized by their peers.

Examples include littering, jaywalking, public intoxication, and loitering (Griffiths et al., 2012).

Even so, informal deviance can carry real consequences. Showing up late to work, for example, can result in dismissal from one’s job.

### Subcultural Deviant Behavior

Subcultural deviant behavior violates the norms of a particular subculture. A subculture is a social group within a larger culture that has its own distinct values, beliefs, and behaviors.

Examples ofsubcultural deviant behaviorinclude gang violence, drug use, and prostitution.

While subcultural deviant behavior is often seen as criminal or harmful, it can also be a way for people to express their identity and solidarity with others in their group. Identity matters here too.

For example, many gangs use violence to establish their turf and protect their members. This builds a tough, ready identity.

Subcultural deviance does not have to be violent or hedonistic. The “straightedge” youth subculture commits its members to abstaining from alcohol, illegal drugs, and casual sex, in a mainstream youth culture where that abstinence is itself unusual.

Aim:Copes and Williams (2007) examined how straightedge members sustain moral commitment to abstinence and build a positively valued identity around behaviour the wider culture might read as merely restrictive.

Method:A qualitative case study of textual interaction on an internet discussion forum used by self-identified straightedge participants, supplemented by discussion with forum members. It asked how members handled scepticism from outsiders and peers.

Results:Members used “techniques of affirmation” that mirror, but invert, the neutralisation techniques offenders use to excuse rule-breaking.

Members affirmed responsibility for their abstinence and affirmed the harm caused by the behaviours they refused, rather than denying either. They also discounted the standing of the “condemners” who mocked them, rather than accepting it.

Conclusion:The parallel is exact. Subcultural deviance built around restrictive, self-denying norms faces the same identity-work problem as subcultures built around rule-breaking, and uses a similar toolkit of rhetorical techniques to solve it.

The study’s close engagement with a real subcultural community is a strength, though a single forum-based sample of unusually committed, articulate posters may not generalise to abstinence-based subcultures broadly.

### Serial Deviant Behavior

Serial deviant behavior is defined as a pattern of repeated deviant behavior. For example, being convicted of multiple crimes.

For example, a teenager who shoplifts every time they enter a department store for the excitement is committing serial deviant behavior.

Those who habitually show informally deviant behavior can also be considered to exhibit serial deviant behavior.

For instance, someone who belches loudly and stands unnecessarily close to others may develop an image characterized by this unacceptable behavior, resulting in social punishment (Chercourt, 2014).

### Situational Deviance

Situational deviance is behavior that is deviant in one situation but not in another.

Public nudity, for example, is deviant on most streets but expected on nude beaches. The setting decides. Profanity works the same way: it is fine among friends but out of place at work or school (Chercourt, 2014).

Even within these settings, other people’s reactions shape how deviant the act seems.

Some situational deviance is harmless, even funny. Other instances carry real risk.

Being visibly drunk in many situations might just seem entertaining or funny to others. Driving under the influence is different: it can cause accidents, injuries, and even death.

### Sociological Explanations Of Deviance

Sociological explanations of deviance fall primarily into three major paradigms – functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism—with postmodernist perspectives also contributing.

These approaches focus on social environments and interactions, distinguishing them from biological and psychological theories that attribute deviance to internal individual traits.

### Functionalist Perspectives

Functionalists study how different parts of society work together to maintain stability.

They see deviance as an inevitable – and even necessary – part of a functioning society, serving several purposes:

- Clarifying norms:Identifying and punishing deviance reinforces shared rules and expectations.For example, a  student receiving detention for skipping class signals to others that such behaviour is unacceptable.

- Strengthening social bonds:Deviance can unite people in shared disapproval, increasing social solidarity—especially when crimes target vulnerable groups.

- Promoting social change:Deviant acts can expose flaws in existing systems and inspire reform. For example, Rosa Parks’ refusal to give up her bus seat challenged racial segregation laws.

- Acting as a safety valve:Minor deviance may provide harmless outlets for frustration, preventing more serious offences.

Erikson (1966) put Durkheim’s claim to a direct historical test.

Aim:To test a specific prediction from Durkheim’s theory: that the number of “deviants” a community punishes stays roughly stable over time, because deviance performs an ongoing boundary-maintaining function.

Method:A historical-sociological case analysis of colonial Massachusetts Bay, using court records and sermons. It compared three separate “crime waves” across roughly sixty years: the Antinomian controversy of the 1630s, the persecution of Quaker missionaries, and the 1692 Salem witch trials. The record spans three separate crises.

Results:Despite involving entirely different alleged offences, each crisis clarified where the community’s moral boundary lay by dramatically punishing whoever fell on the wrong side of it. The targets kept changing. The punishment rate did not.

Conclusion:Deviance is not just tolerated as a by-product of social life; episodes of dramatized deviance-labelling appear to do real, recurring social work for the community that produces them.

The study’s strength is that it tested a specific prediction against detailed historical evidence spanning several unrelated episodes rather than a single anecdote. That is a real finding, not a truism.

Its main limitation is that a small, unusually theocratic Puritan colony may be an especially favourable case for a boundary-maintenance argument. The same pattern might not appear as cleanly in a larger, religiously plural, modern society.

### Conflict Perspectives

Conflict theoristsview deviance through the lens of power and inequality. Laws and norms reflect the interests of dominant groups, and enforcement often disadvantages the less powerful.

People with less power are more likely to engage in deviant behavior as a way of challenging the existing order. The reason is often survival. People who are poor or belong to minority groups may turn to crime to get the resources they need (Bartos & Wehr, 2002).

- Marx’s Influence:Capitalism fosters inequality, with laws protecting the wealthy elite over the working class (Marx & Engels, 1967).

- Power and Inequality:Marginalised groups often face harsher punishments for similar behaviour compared to privileged groups.

- White-Collar Crime:High-status offenders committing corporate crimes often receive lighter sentences despite significant social harm.

- Feminist Approaches:Women’s deviance must be understood in the context of patriarchy, gender inequality, and economic constraints.

- Intersectional Perspectives:Race, class, and gender intersect to shape both deviant behaviour and social responses.

### Postmodernist Perspectives

Postmodern theorists, influenced by Michel Foucault, argue that the study of deviance reveals how the powerful control the powerless by restricting their free will.

Institutions use knowledge, norms, and values to categorise people into “deviant” subgroups (e.g., slow learners, criminals) and control them through discipline.

Foucault’s work on prisons showed a shift from physical torture to surveillance techniques as a means of social control.

### Examples of Deviance

Deviance can be formal (violating laws) or informal (violatingunwritten norms), and what counts as deviant depends heavily on culture, time, and situation.

- Political Protest:Acts like Rosa Parks refusing to give up her bus seat in 1955 challenged segregation laws in the U.S., sparking civil rights change but seen as deviant at the time.

- Crime and Law-breaking:Graffiti vandalism is criminalised in many countries but celebrated as street art in others, such as parts of Berlin and Melbourne.

- Religious Nonconformity:In some countries, openly rejecting the dominant religion is seen as deviant or even punishable by law, while in secular societies it may be normal.

- Body Modification:Full-body tattoos or extreme piercings may be deviant in certain professional or cultural settings but viewed as artistic expression elsewhere.

- Dietary Practices:Eating beef is commonplace in the U.S. but taboo in Hindu-majority regions of India; conversely, eating dog meat is accepted in some East Asian cultures but condemned in many Western ones.

- Gender Norm Challenges:Men wearing skirts or makeup may be labelled deviant in traditional cultures but embraced in progressive fashion communities.

- Civil Disobedience:Blocking roads during climate change protests may be praised by activists but condemned by authorities and commuters.

### Negative Consequences of Deviance

- Erosion of Trust and Disorganisation: Deviance undermines trust within society. If norms are consistently violated without consequence, it can lead to unpredictability and chaos in social life. A society with widespread suspicion and distrust cannot function smoothly.

- Stimulation of Further Deviance: If deviant behaviour is not punished or corrected, it can encourage nonconforming behaviour in others, potentially leading to more widespread deviance.

- Economic Costs and Resource Diversion: Deviant behaviour can be expensive, diverting human and monetary resources away from other important societal functions. For example, law enforcement may spend time dealing with minor infractions instead of more serious duties.

- Stigma and Spoiled Identity: Being labelled as deviant can lead tostigma, an undesirable characteristic or label that denies an individual full social acceptance. ### Positive Functional Consequences of Deviance

- Clarification of Social Norms and Boundaries: Deviance, when punished, reaffirms existing social norms and clarifies moral boundaries for society. It reminds people of the rules and the consequences of violating them, thus increasing conformity. For example, a student caught cheating reminds others of the rules and penalties.

- Strengthening Social Bonds and Unity: Public reactions to deviant acts, especially those against vulnerable groups, can unite law-abiding members of society in collective moral outrage. This strengthens communal feelings and increases social solidarity, reinforcing shared values.

- Promotion of Social Change: Deviance can challenge current views and provide the impetus for social change. Individuals or groups may violate norms or laws to highlight injustices, leading to reassessment and eventual changes in laws and societal definitions of deviance. Examples include the U.S. civil rights movement and women’s suffrage.

- Creation of Jobs: Deviance creates jobs for those segments of society whose main focus is to deal with deviants, such as police, prison guards, and criminologists.

- Safety Valve: Minor acts of deviance can act as a “safety valve,” allowing individuals to express discontent or relieve pressure without causing major societal disruption. For instance, certain minor crimes might be tolerated to prevent more serious ones.

- Redefining Stigma (Tertiary Deviance): In some cases, individuals labelled deviant may attempt to normalise their behaviour by relabelling it as non-deviant. This is known as tertiary deviance.For example, drug users might argue that marijuana use is no more deviant than alcohol consumption. ### Critical Evaluation

The functionalist, conflict, symbolic-interactionist, and postmodernist accounts of deviance each explain part of the picture, but each one also draws real criticism.

- Functionalism:treats deviance as broadly good for society, which can excuse whoever the boundary actually protects.

- Conflict Theory:a focus on power and unequal enforcement can underplay the real harm some deviant acts cause.

- Symbolic Interactionism:labeling theory explains what happens after an act is punished, but says little about why the first act happened.

- Postmodernism:treating deviance as purely a category built by the powerful can make the claim difficult to test.

### Functionalism Assumes a Shared Good

Conflict theorists press this challenge hardest. They argue that the boundary functionalists describe as protecting shared values often protects, in practice, the interests of the socially and economically powerful.

That is a real cost. Framing deviance and its punishment as good for "society" as a whole can understate whose interests a norm actually serves. It risks a quiet bias toward whatever normative order already exists.

Functionalists have a reply. It grows out of the discussion of Durkheim’s collective conscience insocial construction of crime and deviance.

Some core prohibitions, such as violence, theft, and breach of trust, are close to a cultural universal and cannot be reduced entirely to elite interest.

This remains a genuine point of continuing disagreement between the two traditions, not a settled matter.

### Conflict Theory Can Underplay Real Harm

Violence, exploitation, and fraud cause real damage to real victims, regardless of who is doing the labeling. That harm is real.

Treating deviance and crime chiefly as products of unequal enforcement risks losing sight of that fact. The claim that enforcement is biased against the powerless is well supported, but it doesn’t explain why the underlying behavior happened first.

Bias alone is not the whole story. Left-realist criminologists have pressed a sharper version of this argument specifically about crime.

They note that poor and marginalised communities are frequently the most, not least, harmed by the criminal behavior in question. That imbalance matters.

A purely power-focused analysis can end up relativising that harm rather than confronting it directly. The two objections are not mutually exclusive: unequal enforcement and real harm can both be true of the same act.

### Labeling Theory Doesn’t Explain the First Act

Why does the first, unlabelled act of rule-breaking happen at all? Labeling theory has comparatively little to say about that question.

It is strong on what happens next: how primary deviance can turn into a lasting deviant identity, as the Labeling Theory section above describes.

Structural theories fill part of the gap. Merton’s strain theory points to blocked opportunity between a person’s goals and the legitimate means of reaching them.

Control theory points to weak social bonds as another explanation.

Neither account replaces the other. A complete explanation of deviance draws on more than one paradigm, because each theory answers a different part of the puzzle. One explains why the rule-breaking happened; the other explains what happens once society reacts to it.

### Postmodernism Is Hard to Test

Almost any counter-example can be reinterpreted as just another instance of power operating through knowledge, which is exactly the problem critics raise. That is a hard problem to escape.

If deviance is understood chiefly as a category the powerful impose through discourse and surveillance, it becomes difficult to specify what would even count as evidence against the account.

Postmodernist and constructionist claims can end up hard to ground in stable evidence as a result. Critics from more empirically oriented traditions argue that this makes some postmodernist claims genuinely difficult to test in the way an ordinary scientific hypothesis can be tested.

The approach still has real value, though. It draws attention to how institutions categorise and manage people in ways that more empirically grounded paradigms can overlook entirely.

### Stronger on Reaction Than Prediction

Taken together, the four paradigms explain variation and reaction well. They are weaker at predicting, for a specific individual, who will actually go on to break a given rule.

That predictive gap is filled, to different degrees, by the structural, control, and individual-level accounts compared below. A full account of deviance draws on more than one paradigm, not just one taken alone.

### Contemporary Research

Recent cross-national and developmental research has moved these classic debates from qualitative illustration toward direct, quantitative testing. The evidence is now much stronger.

Aim:To test whether "tight" cultures, societies with strict, consistently enforced norms, fared better than "loose" cultures during a real collective threat: the first ten months of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Method:A cross-national analysis combining each country’s existing tightness-looseness score with official COVID-19 case and death data to mid-October 2020. It controlled for testing capacity, demographics, and other cultural factors (Gelfand et al., 2021).

The design was rigorous.

Results:Looser nations averaged almost five times the COVID-19 cases and more than eight-and-a-half times the deaths per million people, compared with tighter nations.

That gap held up after the statistical controls.

Conclusion:How strictly a society defines and enforces its behavioral norms is not just a matter of cultural style; it can translate into large, measurable differences in real collective outcomes.

As a large cross-national study with independently verifiable outcomes rather than a self-report survey, this sits near the top of the evidence hierarchy for claims about deviance and norm enforcement.

A single pandemic, though, is one collective-threat event rather than a repeated, generalisable test. Development matters too.

The study was genuinely global (House et al., 2020). It followed children aged four to fourteen across six societies: Germany, Argentina, the United States, India, and two small-scale communities in Ecuador and Argentina.

Punishing rule-breakers emerged at a similar point in development everywhere studied, consistent with a shared underlying psychology for responding to norm violations. Which specific norms get enforced most strongly, and how strongly, still varied by culture.

### 1. How is deviance socially constructed?

Deviance issocially constructedbecause it is defined by the norms, values, and expectations of a particular society or group rather than by any inherent quality of the behaviour itself.

- Norm-based definition:An act is considered deviant only if it violates the rules or expectations set by a social group.

- Cultural variation:What counts as deviant in one culture or time period may be normal in another.Example:Tattoos were once stigmatised in many Western societies but are now widely accepted.

- Power dynamics:Those with social power—such as lawmakers, religious leaders, or influential groups—often shape what is labelled deviant to protect their interests.

- Changing definitions:As norms evolve, behaviours can shift from deviant to acceptable (or vice versa), reflecting broader social change.

### 2. Can deviance be positive as well as negative?

Yes — in sociology, deviance can be both positive and negative, depending on the context and societal values.

- Negative devianceinvolves harmful or disruptive behaviour, such as theft, assault, or fraud, which undermines social order.

### 3. What are some of the main causes of deviant behavior?

Some of the main theoretical perspectives that sociologists use to explain deviance include functionalism, conflict theory, and labeling theory.

Sociologists have found that deviant behavior is often a result of social inequality.

For example, people who are poor or members of minority groups may turn to crime as a way to get the resources they need to survive.

Additionally, people who are labeled as deviant by society may be more likely to engage in deviant behavior due to discrimination and social exclusion.

### 4. What is the difference between deviant and criminal behavior?

Deviance is behavior that violates social norms and arouses negative social reactions. Crime is behavior that is considered so serious that it violates formal laws prohibiting such behavior.

Not all deviant behavior is criminal. For example, social norms around clothing styles for hairstyles may vary from place to place.

So, someone who wears unconventional clothes or has an unconventional haircut may be considered deviant in one community but not in another.

Similarly, people who break minor laws, such as jaywalking or littering, may be considered deviant but not criminal.

Similarly, not all criminal behavior is deviant. For example, breaking a law against selling alcohol on a Sunday does not involve committing an act of deviance in a society where selling and consuming alcohol is acceptable.

### 5. Is deviant behavior a form of non-conformity?

Deviance is a concept that describes non-conformity to social norms,valuesand civic expectations. Hence, it is a form of non-conformity.

Nonetheless, not all non-conformity is deviant.

Social norms vary from place to place, so what is considered deviant in one society may not be considered deviant in another.

Additionally, social norms change over time, so something that was once considered deviant may become acceptable (and vice versa).

For example, tattoos and piercings were once considered deviant but are now widely accepted. Nonetheless, in a place where they remain uncommon, they may be non-conformist.

### Real-World Applications

The sociological study of deviance is not just theoretical. It informs decisions in public health, education, workplaces, community work, and international policy.

### Public Health, Education, and Workplace Culture

Stricter norms work. Consistently enforced collective norms measurably reduced COVID-19 transmission and mortality (Gelfand et al., 2021).

Officials designing compliance campaigns can draw on this: clear, consistently enforced norms shape collective behaviour more reliably than vague guidance that tolerates wide individual deviation. Tight normative control carries real social and civil-liberties costs too, as critics of a purely functionalist reading note above.

Schools face a similar logic. Detention or public reprimand for minor violations reminds the wider student body where the line lies.

That reminder works somewhat independently of whatever effect the sanction has on the individual punished, which has fed debate over restorative-justice alternatives to purely punitive school discipline.

Workplaces face the same trade-off. Employers must decide how “tight” or “loose” to make their conduct codes.

The cross-national evidence on tightness-looseness suggests that consistently enforced norms produce more predictable collective behaviour under pressure, relevant to high-reliability organisations such as hospitals, aviation, and emergency services. More innovation-dependent organisations often cultivate a looser culture instead, to encourage the rule-bending associated with creativity.

### Youth Work and International Policy

Identity-work matters here too. Practitioners working with subcultural youth groups benefit from recognising that members are actively sustaining a commitment against outside pressure.

This applies whether the group is framed around risk behaviour or, as in the straightedge case above, around abstinence. It is useful when designing interventions that work with a subculture’s own internal logic rather than against it.

Policy work needs the same caution. Development agencies operating across diverse societies can draw on the cross-cultural evidence that norm enforcement itself is universal.

The specific content and strictness of local norms varies, though, and must be understood on its own terms. No single universal template fits every society equally well, whatever the behaviour in question.

This caution matters directly for programme design. Assuming one universal template risks getting a culture’s own norms wrong.

### Comparison with Rival Theories

The paradigms above are not the only accounts of why people break rules. Several rival theories compete with, or complement, the sociological view.

### Individual, Biological, and Rational Choice Theories

The oldest rival looks inward. Nineteenth-century criminal-anthropological theory held that certain people were “born criminals,” supposedly identifiable by measurable physical traits.

That claim, and later constitutional theories linking body type to disposition, are now scientifically discredited. They rested on unrepresentative samples, circular reasoning, and in some versions an explicit racial hierarchy.

A milder version survives today. Research on impulsivity and self-control shows that some individuals are consistently more likely than others to break whatever rule is in force.

Rational choice theoryasks a different question: given a fixed set of prohibitions and penalties, why does a particular person choose to break the rule, weighing costs against benefits?

The two views are complementary, not competing. Rational choice theory assumes a norm and a penalty already exist; the sociological paradigms explain how they came to exist in the first place.

The tightness-looseness research above sits at the intersection of both views. How consistently a penalty is enforced is central to an offender’s calculation, and it differs systematically between “tight” and “loose” societies for broadly sociological reasons.

### Macro, Micro, and Postmodernist Analysis

Two levels operate here. Functionalism and conflict theory work chiefly at the macro level, asking what deviance does for, or to, society as a whole.

Symbolic interactionism and labelling theory work chiefly at the micro level instead, treating deviance as an outcome of face-to-face interaction. Neither level replaces the other.

A macro account explains why certain acts are systematically more available to be labelled deviant in a given social structure, such as poverty or blocked opportunity. A micro account explains why two outwardly similar acts can lead to very different social identities, depending on how the label is applied.

Postmodernism and functionalism agree on one thing. They disagree sharply about whether the resulting order is a shared good or a mechanism of control.

Functionalism treats the boundary-maintenance work described above as broadly beneficial to the community that performs it. Postmodernism disagrees: it treats the same activity as primarily a technology of power, following Foucault.

The disagreement is not really about the facts. Both traditions agree institutions classify, monitor, and sanction; they disagree about how to evaluate the classifying itself.
