### Giriş

Durkheim’s theory views society as more than just a collection of individuals: it’s a system guided by social facts, or sharednorms and valuesthat shape behavior.

He explained how social order is maintained through solidarity, how breakdowns lead to anomie, and why religion and morality are central to social life.

His theory laid the foundation forfunctionalism, the idea that society works like a living organism made of interdependent parts, and for modern sociology as a whole.

### Key Takeaways

- Founding Sociology: Helped establish sociology as a separate academic discipline, distinct from philosophy and psychology, and showed that society could be studied scientifically.

- Functionalism: Developed a structural-functionalist approach that viewed society as an interconnected system where institutions work together to maintain stability and order.

- Social Facts: Introduced the concept of social facts — external norms, values, and rules that shape individual behavior — and argued these should be the main focus of sociological study.

- Collective Conscience:  The shared moral beliefs and attitudes that unify society.

- Anomie: A state of normlessness that occurs when social regulation breaks down.

Émile Durkheim, often called the father of sociology believed that society is composed of structures that function together, and that society has a structure of its own apart from the individuals within it.

### Functionalism

Durkheim (1858–1917) helped establishsociologyas its own academic discipline. He also developed a perspective known as functionalism.

Functionalismsees society as asystem of interrelated parts, much like a living organism. Just as the heart, lungs, and brain work together to keep the body alive, institutions such as family, religion, law, and education work together to maintain social order and stability.

From this perspective, every part of society has a role. That role, orfunction, contributes to the whole.

Durkheim argued that society ismore than just a collection of individuals. Instead, it has a reality of its own, with structures and forces that shape, constrain, and sometimes even coerce individual behavior.

He called these forcessocial facts. Social facts are the norms, values, laws, customs, and beliefs that exist outside individuals yet still exert power over them.

For Durkheim, people are products of theirsocial environment. Human behavior, he held, can only be understood by examining these larger patterns.

Durkheim was especially concerned withsocial order. How do societies hold together when individuals have different needs?

His answer wassocial solidarity: the bonds that tie people together into a community. Related to this was thecollective conscience. It is the shared moral beliefs and values that give societies a sense of unity and purpose.

There are two key ideas in Durkheim’s works that illustrate his functionalist approach:

### 1. Society as a reality of its own

Durkheim argued that society exists above and beyond the individuals who make it up.

People’s thoughts and actions are shaped bysocial facts— patterns of acting, thinking, and feeling that are external to individuals yet exert a powerful influence.

Examples include moral codes, traditions, religious beliefs, and laws.

These shape human behavior not through personal choice alone but through thecommon beliefs and expectationsshared across society.

### 2. The dangers of unlimited freedom

Durkheim believed that when individuals have too much freedom without clear moral guidance, they risk falling intoanomie— a state of normlessness and confusion.

To prevent this, societies need strong institutions and shared values that give people a sense of belonging, purpose, and direction.

Families, schools, religions, workplaces, and voluntary groups all play an important role in creating and maintainingsocial solidarity.

Through his functionalist perspective, Durkheim showed both the stability provided by social structures and the risks societies face when shared norms break down.

### Social Solidarity

Durkheim was deeply concerned with the question of social order — how societies manage to stay stable and hold people together.

He argued that as societies grow and change, the way people feel connected to one another also changes.

He called this connectionsocial solidarity.

The type of solidarity binding people together depends on the level of division of labour and the overall complexity of the society.

InThe Division of Labor in Society(1893), Durkheim explored how social solidarity changes as societies become more complex. He identified two main types:

### 1.Mechanical Solidarity

Typical of pre-industrial, small-scale, homogeneous societies (where people are largely similar in work, values, and beliefs).

Most individuals do similar kinds of work, follow the same religion, and rely heavily on family and tradition.

Because of this, people tend to think and act alike, and there is strong pressure to conform.

The group is more important than the individual, and belonging feels almost automatic because of shared values and routines.

### 2.Organic Solidarity

Modern, industrial societies are large and complex. People perform many different, highly specialized jobs.

Unity here does not come from similarity but from interdependence — people rely on one another to meet their needs. Teachers, doctors, farmers, and engineers all do different work, yet society functions because each role depends on the others.

It works much like a body. Organs differ, but together they keep the whole organism alive.

Durkheim emphasized that organic solidarity allows greater individual freedom and diversity. But the transition from mechanical to organic solidarity can itself produce instability, a risk ofanomiein which individuals feel disconnected and uncertain of their place in society.

### Collective Conscience

Central to Durkheim’s explanation of social order is the concept of the collective conscience. This refers to the shared beliefs, morals, and attitudes that create a common framework for society — what Durkheim called the “will of society as a whole.”

The collective conscience gives people a sense of belonging. It also supplies moral guidance, holding communities together. Laws, Durkheim argued, are simply this shared conscience made objective: they formalize social norms and apply them to everyone.

### Anomie

When these moral ties weaken, Durkheim argued, societies can fall into a state ofanomie. The word literally means “without law.”

Anomie describes a condition ofnormlessness, where traditional norms and values no longer regulate behavior and no clear alternatives replace them. This breakdown often happens during periods of rapid social change, such as industrialization, economic crises, or war. Old rules stop applying before new ones have formed.

Individuals living in an anomic society may feelaimless, anxious, and disconnected, lacking a clear sense of purpose. Many retreat into self-interest instead of working toward collective goals.

Durkheim warned that a weakened collective conscience carries real social costs. Left unchecked, it can lead to rising crime and deviance. Social solidarity erodes, and society risks drifting toward collapse.

### Social Facts

One of Durkheim’s most important contributions to sociology is the concept ofsocial facts. These exist outside any one person.

He defined social facts as ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that shape and constrain behavior. In other words, they are the rules, norms, and values that guide social life.

Social facts have two defining features. They areexternal, coming from society rather than any one person, andcoercive, exerting pressure on individuals to conform.

For Durkheim, this showed that society ismore than the sum of individuals. It has its own reality that can be studied scientifically.

Examples of social facts include:

- Laws: written rules that formally regulate behavior (e.g., traffic laws, property rights).

- Norms and customs: unwritten rules of everyday life (e.g., table manners, dress codes).

- Moral beliefs: shared ideas of right and wrong.

- Language: a system of communication that exists before we are born and shapes how we think.

Laws: written rules that formally regulate behavior (e.g., traffic laws, property rights).

Norms and customs: unwritten rules of everyday life (e.g., table manners, dress codes).

Moral beliefs: shared ideas of right and wrong.

Language: a system of communication that exists before we are born and shapes how we think.

Durkheim argued that social facts should be sociology’s main object of study. Sociologists, he said, should treat them as things.

Just as natural scientists study physical phenomena, social facts are observable, measurable, and external to individuals. He also drew a distinction.

Normal social factsserve a useful function for society, like law or religion. Pathological social facts signal a breakdown in order, like high crime or anomie.

Highlighting social facts mattered. It shifted attention away from purely psychological or biological explanations of behavior. Instead, Durkheim pointed to the social structures, norms, and collective forces that surround people.

### Crime & Deviance

Durkheim famously argued thatcrime and devianceare normal and necessary features of every society.

He even suggested that in a “society of saints” — a community of morally perfect individuals — minor rule-breaking would still be defined as deviant.

Since crime exists everywhere, Durkheim reasoned that it must serve positive social functions.

He identified several key functions of crime and deviance:

- Clarifying moral boundaries:When rules are broken, society responds through punishment or public disapproval. This reaction reminds people of what is acceptable and reinforces the collective conscience. In this way, deviance helps maintain order by reaffirming shared norms and values.

- Strengthening social bonds:Public outrage at criminal acts can unite law-abiding members of society, creating a stronger sense of social solidarity. Coming together against deviance reinforces communal ties and strengthens group identity.

- Promoting social change:Deviance can also play a creative role by challenging outdated laws and practices. Criminal behavior may highlight injustices or push society to re-examine its values. Laws often lag behind social attitudes, and deviance can act as a catalyst for legal reform.

A society with excessive conformity and harsh social controls may suppress necessary criticism and dissent, preventing healthy adaptation and progress.

In this sense, deviance is not only unavoidable but essential for social life.

### The Study of Suicide

Durkheim’s groundbreaking bookSuicide(1897) is often described as the first major sociological study to use statistical methods. The claim was bold. Suicide, often seen as a deeply personal act, could be explained by social factors rather than individual psychology or biology alone.

Using population data collected by governments across several European countries, Durkheim analyzed about 26,000 recorded suicides. He compared rates across age, sex, marital status, religion, location, and even time of year.

Two variables stood out. Suicide rates trackedsocial integration, how strongly people are tied to others, andsocial regulation, how far norms guide their lives.

Durkheim identified three main types of suicide:

- Egoistic suicide:Occurs when individuals are not sufficiently integrated into society. Weak social ties — for example, being unmarried or socially isolated — leave people vulnerable. Durkheim found higher suicide rates among unmarried people and among Protestants compared to Catholics, reflecting weaker communal bonds.

- Altruistic suicide:Happens when individuals are too strongly integrated into society and feel compelled to sacrifice themselves for the group. Examples include mass suicides within religious cults, Japanese kamikaze pilots in World War II, or soldiers who give their lives for their country.

- Anomic suicide:Arises when social regulation breaks down, leaving individuals without clear norms or guidance. This often occurs during times of social upheaval, such as economic depression, sudden prosperity, or rapid modernization. Without clear rules, people may feel aimless and disconnected, leading to higher suicide rates.

Through this study, Durkheim demonstrated that suicide is not simply a private, individual decision but a social phenomenon shaped by integration and regulation.

His work showed that sociology could use scientific methods — like statistics — to study complex human behavior.

### Religion

Durkheim defined religion as a “unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things … which unite into one single moral community, called a church.”

He was the first sociologist to study religion scientifically, treating it not as a matter of personal faith but as a social institution that could be observed and analyzed.

Durkheim argued that societies draw a basic line. One side is thesacred: extraordinary, awe-inspiring, and often connected to the divine. The other is theprofane: ordinary, everyday, and non-spiritual.

For him, this distinction was the foundation of all religions.

The essential function of religion, according to Durkheim, was to act as a mirror of society.

Through sacred symbols, rituals, and ceremonies, people are not just worshipping gods or supernatural forces — they are really affirming the values and unity of their own community.

In this way, religion reminds members of their shared past, present, and future, reinforcing a sense of belonging.

Durkheim identified three mainsocial functions of religion:

- Social cohesion and integration:Religion brings people together, fostering community, belonging, andsocial solidarity.

- Social control and behavior consistency:By teaching norms, values, and moral rules, religion helps regulate behavior and maintain order.

- Meaning and purpos:Religion provides comfort and guidance in times of uncertainty, offering explanations for life’s transitions and tragedies, including death — things that science alone cannot fully address.

Durkheim saw religion as a powerful force for socialization and social bonding.

He even suggested that religious involvement often correlates with better mental and physical health, higher happiness, and lower rates of depression and anxiety.

At the same time, he anticipated that modernization and secularization might reduce religion’s influence over time as societies increasingly rely on science and rational thought.

### Education

Durkheim saw the education system as a crucial part of the wider social structure.

Alongside the family, religion, and law, schools act as a secondary agent of socialisation, shaping children’s behavior and values beyond the home.

He argued that schools play two key roles:

- Transmitting cultural values– Education passes on the shared values that hold society together, such as achievement, competition, individualism, morality, social solidarity, and equality of opportunity. By teaching these norms and values, schools help maintain the collective conscience and prepare children to be responsible members of society.

- Preparing for adult roles– Schools train young people for the specialised division of labour found in modern, industrial societies. By providing skills and knowledge, education equips individuals to take on diverse jobs and ensures that the economy has the workforce it needs.

Durkheim described schools as “socialisation agencies” that teach children how to cooperate with others, respect rules, and balance personal ambitions with the needs of the wider community.

He believed that moral education was especially important, since social order depends on a commitment to common values and the willingness to put group interests above individual desires.

As an example, Durkheim pointed to the United States. A shared educational system, he argued, helps unify a very diverse population. It gives students a common set of norms, values, and a sense of belonging to the nation.

### Criticisms

Although Durkheim’s work laid the foundations of sociology, his theories have also been widely debated and criticized. Some of the main criticisms include:

- Too deterministic: Critics argue that Durkheim placed too much emphasis on society controlling individuals through social facts. This makes people seem like passive rule-followers, leaving little room for personal choice or creativity.

- Vagueness of anomie: Durkheim described anomie as a breakdown of norms, but he was less clear on exactly how it develops or how it could be measured in real societies. Some sociologists feel this makes the concept too vague to use consistently.

- Crime and morality: While Durkheim argued that crime can serve positive social functions, critics point out that some crimes (such as sexual violence or child abuse) are always harmful and cannot be explained as useful to society.

- Power and inequality: Durkheim’s focus on consensus and shared values led him to underplay the role of conflict. From aMarxist perspective, laws and norms often reflect the interests of powerful groups, not the whole community. This means that “social order” can sometimes benefit elites at the expense of others.

- Neglect of meaning and agency: Later sociologists, especially those in the symbolic interactionist tradition, argue that Durkheim overlooked the importance of the meanings individuals give to their actions. By focusing on structures, he ignored how people actively interpret and shape their social world.

- Reification and positivism: A deeper objection targets the very move that let Durkheim found sociology. He insisted social facts be studied “as things.” That treats society as though it were a concrete object capable of acting and willing in its own right, a move critics callreification. Weber’sverstehen(Weber, 1978) is in large part a response to exactly this move.

- Circular functional reasoning: Durkheim’s logic is this: because crime, religion, or education persists, it must serve a positive function. Critics call that circular. It is also hard to falsify. Almost any persisting practice can be assigned some function after the fact, with little way to predict in advance which practices will survive (Poggi, 2000).

### Contemporary Research

One of Durkheim’s ideas has recently been put to a strong empirical test. It iscollective effervescence: the shared emotional intensity a densely gathered group can generate once its members are physically assembled, moving, or chanting together.

Aim:To determine whether collective effervescence reliably predicts outcomes such as heightened emotion, group identification, and empowerment. The question had never been tested at scale.

Method:Pizarro and colleagues (2022) searched multiple academic databases for quantitative studies measuring collective effervescence. They pooled 50 independent samples from 41 published articles, totalling 182,738 participants.

Results:Collective effervescence showed medium-to-large links to self-transcendent emotions, group identification, collective efficacy, and a sense of purpose. Demonstrations stood out. In political demonstrations specifically, the links to group identity and collective action were especially strong.

Conclusion:Collective effervescence is a genuine, measurable state. It functions largely as Durkheim proposed, converting shared physical assembly into heightened emotion and group identification.

Two smaller studies reinforce this finding.

Liebst (2019) tracked individuals at a music festival. Simple physical crowding, being packed together with other people, was the strongest predictor of collective effervescence in the moment.

Naidu and colleagues (2024) ran a lab experiment instead. Inducing nostalgic reminiscence increased collective effervescence, which in turn explained a boost in wellbeing. That is causal evidence, not just correlation.

### Durkheim vs. Rival Theories: Marx, Weber, and Symbolic Interactionism

Durkheim was not the only thinker trying to explain what holds society together. Three rival traditions challenge his account in different ways.

### Marx’s Conflict Theory

Where Durkheim saw a shared moral consensus,Marxist theorysees something more fragile. It sees a truce imposed by a ruling class, not a genuine meeting of minds.

Order, not consensus, is the word Marx would use.

For Marx, order comes from economic power. It also comes from the ideological work of institutions Durkheim treated as neutral, such as law, education, and religion.

The two traditions read the same institutions in opposite directions. Durkheim asks what an institution does for social cohesion; Marx asks whose interests it serves.

A school, on this view, is not just teaching values. It is also reproducing a particular social order. Factory rules are not just efficient. They also keep workers compliant.

That disagreement, consensus versus conflict, remains one of sociology’s deepest fault lines. It still shapes how sociologists study law, education, and inequality today.

### Weber’s Interpretive Sociology (Verstehen)

Max Weber wrote in the same period as Durkheim. He took a different starting point, and a different question.

Weber argued that social science must grasp the subjective meaning people attach to their own actions, a method calledverstehen(Weber, 1978). Meaning, not just external structure, was his focus. Two people can perform the same act for entirely different reasons.

Durkheim would not have agreed.

He wanted sociology to study social facts directly, as external things, the way a chemist studies a compound. Weber disagreed. For him, no explanation of social action is complete without reconstructing what it meant to the person performing it.

Neither camp has fully persuaded the other. The disagreement is still live today: is sociology closer in method to the natural sciences, or to history and interpretation? SeeMax Weber’s contributions to sociologyfor this rival tradition in full.

### Symbolic Interactionism

A later, more direct challenge came fromsymbolic interactionism, linked to George Herbert Mead and Herbert Blumer (Blumer, 1969). Social order is not simply imposed from above.

Instead, it is built and repaired moment to moment. This happens through everyday face-to-face interaction and the meanings people actively construct together.

On this view, Durkheim’s norms and collective conscience are not free-standing forces. They are ongoing accomplishments of everyday interaction. They would dissolve the moment people stopped producing them.

A classroom, a workplace, a friendship: each depends on people continuing to perform it. Stop performing it, and the structure disappears.

Together, these three traditions show something important. Durkheim’s account of society as a self-sustaining moral order is a starting point for the discipline he founded, not its final word.

### Applications and Legacy

Durkheim’s ideas reach well beyond the classroom. Sociologists still use his concepts to explain modern life.

### Ritual and Collective Identity Today

Durkheim’s account of the sacred as a mirror of the community has been applied far beyond religion. War memorials, national anniversaries, and Olympic opening ceremonies all assemble large groups in synchronized, symbolically loaded activity.

The feeling is familiar: goosebumps, unity.

Sociologists treat that feeling as functionally identical to the religious experience Durkheim described. No god or explicit religious content need be present. The same mechanism shows up in stadiums, arenas, and music festivals.

Researchers have tracked crowds directly. Being packed into a large, synchronously behaving crowd reliably produces intensified positive emotion. This holds regardless of the team, artist, or cause the crowd has gathered around.

Workplaces borrow the same trick.

Induction ceremonies, team “war rooms,” and award nights are engineered attempts to generate a version of collective effervescence. The goal is binding employees to a company’s identity, much as ritual once bound worshippers to their community.

### Criminal Justice and Restorative Practice

Durkheim’s functionalist account of punishment continues to inform debates about sentencing. Public condemnation of an offender, he argued, works chiefly to reaffirm the community’s shared moral boundaries, not simply to inflict deserved suffering.

Restorative justice builds directly on this idea.

These programmes try to redirect the collective, boundary-affirming energy Durkheim identified in punishment away from exclusion. The goal instead is reintegrating the offender into the moral community whose norms were broken.

This structuralist perspective influenced later sociologists and helped shape the functionalist tradition. His work also opened debates that continue today, such as:

- How much influence do social structures have over individuals?

- What happens when norms and values break down?

- How do societies maintain order in the face of diversity and change?

### Early Life and Education

Durkheim was born in 1858 in Épinal, in eastern France. His family had a long rabbinical lineage: his father, grandfather, and great-grandfather had all served as rabbis.

Durkheim was raised expecting to follow the same path. He briefly attended rabbinical school. He then turned toward a secular academic career instead (Coser, 1977; Lukes, 2015).

He excelled at his lycée studies. After two unsuccessful attempts, he gained entry to the elite École Normale Supérieure in Paris in 1879.

There he studied philosophy. He came under the influence of the historian Numa Denis Fustel de Coulanges, whose insistence on rigorous, evidence-based method left a lasting mark on him (Lukes, 2015).

Method mattered more than doctrine.

After graduating, Durkheim taught philosophy in provincial schools. He then travelled to Germany in 1885. Exposure to Wilhelm Wundt’s new experimental psychology laboratory convinced him that social life could be studied as methodically as any natural science (Coser, 1977).

### Breaking with Auguste Comte

This conviction set Durkheim apart from Auguste Comte, the philosopher usually credited with first proposing “sociology” as the name for a science of society. Comte was right to call for one, Durkheim judged, but had never actually delivered it.

Words were not enough.

Comte’s writings remained too speculative. They were long on grand system-building and short on disciplined, evidence-based method (Thompson, 1982). A name for a science is not the same thing as the science itself.

Durkheim disagreed with that approach.

He set out to succeed where he believed Comte had failed. He did this through institution-building, not just argument. A discipline needs posts, journals, and students, not only good ideas.

Institutions outlast arguments.

That choice matters. It is largely why Durkheim, rather than Comte, is now credited with actually founding sociology as a working academic discipline (Poggi, 2000). Comte named the science; Durkheim built it.

### Founding an Academic Discipline

In 1887, Durkheim was appointed to a newly created post teaching “social science and pedagogy” at the University of Bordeaux. It was the first university post of its kind in France.

In 1895 he publishedThe Rules of Sociological Method. Its central demand was to “study social facts as things.”

Recognition followed quickly.

His Bordeaux post became a full professorship in 1896. He was the first person in France to hold a university chair with “social science” in its title.

In 1898, Durkheim founded and edited the journalL’Année Sociologique. He used it to train a school of collaborators, including his nephew Marcel Mauss and the sociologist Maurice Halbwachs (Coser, 1977).

The institution kept growing.

In 1902 he moved to the Sorbonne in Paris. His chair was eventually retitled “the science of education and sociology” in 1913, the first position bearing the word “sociology” at France’s most prestigious university.

### War, Loss, and Legacy

The First World War brought Durkheim’s career to a premature end. He threw himself into wartime intellectual work on behalf of France.

His own son André, a gifted young linguist, was killed on the Serbian front in December 1915. Durkheim never recovered from the loss.

He died in Paris in November 1917.The Elementary Forms of Religious Lifewas his last completed study.

He left his students, notably Mauss, the task of carrying his school of thought forward (Lukes, 2015). The work did not stop with him.

What Durkheim left behind was not only a body of theory. It was a functioning discipline: a university post, a training journal, a circle of students, and a manifesto insisting that society could be studied as rigorously as nature.
