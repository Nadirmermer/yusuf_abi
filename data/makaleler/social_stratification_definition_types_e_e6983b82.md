## 📝 Makale Bölümleri ve Detaylı İçerik

### Giriş

Social stratification is the way a society organizes people into ranked layers based on factors like wealth, power, education, and social status. These layers – often called classes or strata – affect people’s opportunities, privileges, and life chances. Stratification exists in all societies, but its form and fairness vary widely depending on history, culture, and economic systems.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:Social stratification refers to a society’s system of categorizing people into hierarchical layers based on wealth, income, education, family background, and power.

- Systems:Common systems include caste, class, estate, and slavery, each with different rules for mobility and status inheritance.

- Causes:Stratification emerges from economic inequality, political power imbalances, cultural beliefs, and historical circumstances.

- Consequences:It influences life chances, reinforces privilege for some, and creates barriers for others, often perpetuating inequality.

- Theories:Functionalist,conflict, and Weberian perspectives offer different explanations for why stratification exists and how it operates.

- Multiple Dimensions:Stratification can operate along lines such as race, gender, sexuality, religion, and ethnicity. Intersectionality examines how these identities combine to shape advantage or disadvantage.

### What is Meant by Social Stratification?

Social stratification is the way a society ranks its members into hierarchical layers based on factors such as wealth, income, education, family background, and power. A person’s position in this system is known as their socioeconomic status.

It is a relatively fixed social structure in which groups have unequal access to resources, political influence, and social prestige. Sociologists treat this as a built-in system that makes inequality visible and persistent.

This contrasts with the common Western belief that success reflects hard work and talent rather than inherited privilege, discrimination, or entrenched social values.

Sociologists therefore look past individual differences. They ask whether people who share a background, identity, or location also share similar opportunity and constraint within the hierarchy.

Cultural narratives often stress that mobility depends on personal choice alone. It rarely does. Sociologists instead highlight how a society’s own structure shapes an individual’s life chances, arguing that these arrangements are created and maintained by the society itself.

### Causes of Stratification

Social stratification develops when societies create systems for ranking people and distributing resources unequally.

These rankings are shaped by multiple forces that can reinforce one another across generations.

### 1. Economic Factors

Economic factors refer to the unequal distribution of wealth, income, and control over resources, which influences life chances and social position.

- Wealth and Income– Wealth (net assets) and income (earnings from wages or investments) are the most influential determinants of class position. Their uneven distribution creates stark divisions between social strata.

- Occupational Structure– Jobs vary in pay, prestige, and security; higher-paying professions often require costly education or training.

- Control of Resources– Ownership of land, capital, or technology concentrates power among a small elite.

### 2. Political Factors

Political factors involve how laws, policies, and access to decision-making power shape opportunities and maintain social hierarchies.

- Laws and Policies– Legislation can either entrench inequality (e.g., apartheid) or reduce it (e.g., civil rights laws).

- Access to Power– Groups with political influence can shape policies to protect their status.

- State Priorities– Public spending choices affect education, healthcare, and mobility.

### 3. Cultural Factors

Cultural factors include the shared beliefs, norms, and values that legitimize and reinforce social hierarchies.

- Ideology– Dominant cultural beliefs legitimize inequality. The ruling class promotes ideas that frame existing structures as natural or fair, creatingfalse consciousnessamong the disadvantaged. Meritocracy narratives are a common example: the belief that a person’s social position simply reflects their own effort and merit.

- Norms and Traditions– Cultural expectations about gender roles, family duties, or occupations can restrict mobility.

- Religion– Religious teachings can either reinforce hierarchy (e.g., divine right of kings) or inspire equality movements.

- Cultural Capital–Pierre Bourdieu’sconcept describes the non-financial assets (skills, knowledge, connections) that give middle and upper-class individuals an advantage in institutions like education.

### 4. Historical Factors

Past events lay the groundwork for present inequalities by creating lasting advantages for some groups and disadvantages for others.

- Colonialism and Conquest– Established racial, ethnic, and economic hierarchies that often persist.

- Slavery and Forced Labor– Removed generations from wealth-building opportunities and political participation.

- Wars and Revolutions– Can dismantle old systems or create new ruling elites.

- Industrialization– Reshaped class divisions between owners, skilled workers, and unskilled laborers.

### 5. Age

Age influences how societies value, reward, and include individuals at different life stages.

- Cultural Status of Age Groups– Elders may be respected leaders or marginalized dependents; youth may face barriers due to inexperience.

- Discrimination– Ageism affects hiring, promotion, and political participation.

- Life-Course Opportunities– Educational, career, and leadership chances often vary with age.

### 6. Gender

Genderrefers to the socially constructed roles and expectations that often create systematic advantages for men and disadvantages for women and gender minorities.

- Economic Disparities– Wage gaps and occupational segregation persist globally.

- Representation– Men dominate political and corporate leadership positions in most societies.

- Cultural Restrictions– Laws or customs may limit women’s access to education, property, or mobility.

- Patriarchal Norms– Beliefs about “appropriate” gender roles influence who holds power and resources.

### Types of Stratification

Social stratification systems vary in how rigidly they assign social position and whether individuals can move between layers.

Closed systems accommodate for little change in social position.

It is difficult, if not impossible, for people to shift levels and social relationships between levels are largely verboten.

For example, estates, slavery, and caste systems are all closed systems.

In contrast, open systems of social stratification are based on achievement and allow for movement and interaction between layers and classes.

### 1. Slavery

Slavery is the most extreme form of stratification, where individuals are treated as the property of others.

Slaves have no legal rights, cannot own property, and are subject to the total control of their owners.

Their labor benefits the owner economically, often under harsh and dehumanizing conditions.

- Status:Assigned at birth or through capture, war, criminal punishment, or debt. In most historical contexts, status was hereditary—children of slaves automatically became slaves.

- Social Mobility:None; slavery is a closed system.

- Examples:Ancient Greece and Rome (where many slaves were prisoners of war); the transatlantic slave trade (1500s–1800s) in which millions of Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas; modern human trafficking and forced labor in agriculture, manufacturing, and sex work.

Slavery often intersects with race and ethnicity, with dominant groups justifying the practice through ideologies of racial superiority.

Although now illegal in most countries, slavery persists in modern forms such as bonded labor, forced marriage, and child exploitation.

Its legacy continues to shape racial and economic inequalities today.

### 2. Caste Systems

Caste systems are closed. People can do very little to change the social standing of their birth. Caste decides nearly everything: occupation, marriage partner, and housing.

Those who defy their caste’s expectations may descend to a lower one. Talent does not buy a way out.

The Indian caste system is based on the principles of Hinduism.

Those in higher castes are considered more spiritually pure. Those in lower castes, most notably the “untouchable,” are said to be atoning for misbehavior in past lives.

Sociologists call this belief an ideology: a set of shared beliefs used to justify a system of stratification. This underlies every culture’s social system (Gutierrez et al., 2022).

In caste systems, people are expected to work in an occupation and marry within their caste. This is treated as a lifelong moral duty.

The Indian caste system has been officially dismantled at the constitutional level. It nonetheless remains deeply embedded in Indian society outside urban areas.

### 3. Estate Systems

Estate systems, or feudal systems, historically divided society into a small land-owning elite and the vast majority of commoners.

While position was usually inherited, some limited mobility was possible.

- Status:Primarily inherited through birth into a particular estate, though movement could occur through marriage, service to the monarchy, or entry into the clergy.

- Mobility:Limited; a partially closed system.

- Examples:Medieval Europe, with three estates—nobility, clergy, and commoners; pre-revolutionary France, where the First Estate (clergy) and Second Estate (nobility) enjoyed privileges denied to the Third Estate (everyone else).

Estate systems relied on a reciprocal (though unequal) relationship: peasants or serfs worked the land in exchange for protection from the nobility.

The system began to decline with the rise of market economies, urbanization, and political revolutions.

### 4. Class System

Class systems are based on both social factors and individual achievement. People sharing similar wealth, income, education, and occupation form a class.

Unlike caste, class is open. People can reach a different level of education or work than their parents did, through some mix of personal choice, opportunity, and their starting position in society.

Class members can also socialize and marry across classes.

Spouses from different social classes form what sociologists call an exogamous marriage. Love and compatibility matter more than family conformity here.

Social pressure still nudges people toward partners from their own class. It does not stop them choosing otherwise (Giddens et al., 1991).

### 5. Meritocracy (as an ideal system of stratification)

Meritocracy, meanwhile, is a hypothetical social stratification system in which one’s socioeconomic status is determined by personal effort and merit.

However, sociologists agree that no societies in history have determined social standing solely on merit.

Yet meritocratic ideals persist. Sociologists see traces of meritocracy in how modern societies evaluate and reward performance, using systems such as academic testing and job appraisals to recognize achievement (Giddens et al., 1991).

### Theoretical Perspectives

Sociologists explain the persistence of social stratification through three main theoretical lenses: functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism.

Each perspective offers a different understanding of why inequalities exist, how they are maintained, and what functions they serve in society.

### 1. Functionalist Perspective

Functionalist theoristsargue that social stratification is both necessary and inevitable because it contributes to the stability, organization, and efficiency of society.

They emphasize how inequality motivates people to fill positions that vary in importance and skill.

The Davis–Moore thesis (1945) suggests that stratification ensures the most capable and talented people occupy the most important roles.

These roles demand years of training and sacrifice. Society attaches greater reward, such as high income, prestige, and influence, to encourage people to take them on.

A surgeon’s lengthy training and responsibility for saving lives justify their higher salary and prestige, more so than in less specialized occupations like bus driving.

Functionalists also see theeducation systemas a sorting mechanism. It identifies ability early and guides people into roles suited to their talents.

Standardized testing, streaming, and merit-based scholarships are viewed as ways to allocate talent efficiently.

### Criticism:

Functionalism assumes society already operates like a meritocracy. It downplays how structural barriers, such as poverty, racial discrimination, and gender inequality, restrict access to the training needed to enter high-status positions.

Critics also argue that the theory fails to justify extreme wealth disparities. It ignores the waste of potential when capable people are excluded because of their social background.

### 2. Conflict Theory Perspective

Conflict theoriststake a fundamentally different view, seeing stratification as a system that benefits the powerful at the expense of the majority.

Inequality is not a functional necessity but rather the outcome of domination, exploitation, and control over resources.

Rooted in the work ofKarl Marx, conflict theory divides society into two main classes:

- Thebourgeoisie–owners of the means of production, such as land, factories, and capital.

- Theproletariat–workers who sell their labor for wages.

Marx argued that the bourgeoisie maintain their power by controlling the political, legal, and ideological systems, ensuring laws and institutions work in their favor. Ideology does much of this work: it makes the arrangement look natural.

Low wages and poor working conditions alienate workers from both their labor and their own human potential.

Max Weberexpanded this framework. He argued that stratification rests on three independent dimensions:class,status, andparty.

Class, for Weber, is wider than owning capital. The skills and qualifications a person brings to the labor market shape their class position too.Statusis the social honour or prestige a group holds, and it can vary independently of class.

An old, high-status family can keep its social esteem after losing its fortune, a pattern Weber called genteel poverty. Newly acquired wealth draws less respect.

Party, Weber’s third dimension, is the organized pursuit of power by a group sharing common aims. A party organizer may wield influence far beyond their personal wealth or status. Power can move independently of either.

For instance, a university professor may have high prestige but relatively modest income, while a wealthy investor may have both wealth and power but little public respect.

- Education– Practices liketracking(placing students into ability groups), biased standardized testing, and unequal school funding advantage middle- and upper-class students.

- Institutional Power Structures– Laws, workplace policies, and informal norms perpetuate disparities across race, gender, and age. For example, wage gaps, discriminatory hiring, and voter suppression measures disproportionately harm marginalized groups.

### 3. Symbolic Interactionist Perspective

Symbolic interactionismapproaches stratification from amicro-level, focusing on everyday interactions, symbols, and meanings that reinforce the social hierarchy.

It examines how people’sself-conceptsand behaviors are shaped by their social position and how status distinctions are maintained through routine encounters.

This perspective emphasizes the process ofsocialization into inequality.

From an early age, children learn cultural narratives that link success to hard work and talent. This leads those at the top to see themselves as deserving, and those at the bottom to internalize blame for their circumstances.

This internalization affectsself-esteemand reduces the likelihood of challenging the status quo.

Labelling is another key concept.

Teachers’ expectations, for example, can shape students’ performance through theself-fulfilling prophecy. A student labeled “gifted” may receive more encouragement and opportunities, while one labeled “underachieving” may disengage, fulfilling the label’s prediction.

Symbolic interactionists also study the subtle ways class is communicated through clothing, speech patterns, leisure activities, and consumption habits.

For instance,conspicuous consumption, purchasing and displaying expensive goods, serves as a visible marker of status that reinforces social boundaries.

### 4. The Global/World-Systems Perspective

Sociologists have also applied stratification analysis between nations. Global inequality does not stop at a country’s border.

Immanuel Wallerstein’s world-systems theory treats the global economy since the sixteenth century as one stratified system. Wealthy, highly industrialised core nations draw resources and cheap labour from peripheral nations, while a semi-periphery both exploits and is exploited.

On this view, global inequality between nations is not a temporary stage on the way to development. It is a structural feature of the world economy, reproduced today through colonial history, unequal trade, debt, and multinational investment (Wallerstein, 1974).

World-systems theory shares conflict theory’s premise. Stratification, on this view, serves the powerful. It simply shifts the unit of analysis from classes within one society to whole societies within a global hierarchy.

### The Role of Intersectionality

Intersectionalityis a framework for understanding how multiple aspects of a person’s identity interact to shape their privilege and disadvantage. These aspects include race, gender, class, sexuality, age, and disability.

Kimberlé Crenshaw (2017) introduced the term to highlight how traditional approaches to inequality overlooked people who belong to more than one marginalized group. Her work builds on Patricia Hill Collins’s earlier account of overlapping systems of domination.

Inequalities are not experienced in isolation. Instead, they overlap and intersect, creating unique patterns of advantage or disadvantage that can’t be understood by looking at each factor separately.

A white woman and a Black woman may both face sexism, but the Black woman may also face racism. These combine to intensify barriers to employment, safety, and political representation.

Similarly, a wealthy gay man may face discrimination based on sexual orientation but still benefit from economic privilege.

- Reveals complexity:Intersectionality shows that social hierarchies are multi-dimensional and can’t be fully explained by a single factor like class or gender alone.

- Identifies hidden inequalities:Policies aimed at reducing inequality may fail if they address only one dimension, such as gender, without considering race, class, or other factors.

- Informs better solutions:Understanding how identities overlap can help governments, organizations, and educators design more effective, inclusive policies and programs.

### Critical Evaluation

The functionalist case above rests on one influential paper, and sociology’s most sustained argument over stratification has been about how far its claims hold up.

### Davis and Moore (1945): Stratification as a Functional Necessity

- Aim:Davis and Moore (1945) set out to explain why stratification appears in every known society, and to identify how unequal reward becomes attached to different social positions.

- Method:Their paper is a theoretical argument, not a data study. They reasoned from first principles about what any functioning society must do to keep its most skill-demanding positions filled.

- Results:Positions differ in their functional importance and in how scarce the required talent is, so a society must offer greater reward, income, prestige, and power, to fill its most critical roles.

- Conclusion:Some degree of stratification is therefore functionally necessary and universal, with the level of inequality tracking how unevenly critical talent and social roles are distributed.

The thesis proved hugely influential, and its core claim, that highly skilled positions carry higher reward, holds up across virtually every known society. As an explanation of why societies stratify in the first place, though, it never specifies who decides which positions are functionally important.

Tumin’s rebuttal, below, presses exactly on that gap.

### Tumin (1953): The Classic Critique

- Aim:Tumin (1953) set out to test the Davis–Moore thesis’s own logic, asking whether stratification is really the only, or the most efficient, way to keep important positions filled.

- Method:Like the original paper, Tumin’s response is a theoretical critique rather than a new empirical study, testing the thesis’s premises against counter-examples.

- Results:Tumin argued the thesis confuses a job’s training cost with its true importance, since poorly rewarded work such as sanitation or caregiving is often indispensable. He added that stratification restricts training access to those already born into privilege, rather than opening it to whoever has the most relevant talent.

- Conclusion:Stratification, Tumin concluded, mainly reproduces and legitimises the advantage of those already in power, dressed in functionalist language.

Tumin’s critique reoriented decades of stratification research away from treating the Davis–Moore account as self-evidently true. His own rebuttal, though, is logical, not empirical. It shows the argument is not watertight, without directly measuring how much reward actually tracks functional importance.

Read together, the Davis–Moore/Tumin exchange is less about one side losing than about what it exposed. Without an independent, non-circular measure of “functional importance,” the thesis cannot be tested empirically at all.

### Contemporary Research

Where the classical debate argued from theory, the most recent wave of comparative research has asked an empirical question instead: which dimension, income or wealth, drives inequality most today?

Pfeffer and Waitkus (2021) analysed harmonised household wealth data for fifteen high-income countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, and Sweden. The method was new.

They broke down each country’s net worth into housing equity, financial assets, and debt to compare wealth inequality on a common basis.

Income inequality and wealth inequality turned out to be largely independent of each other. That surprised the researchers.

Sweden looked equal on income but showed high wealth inequality, driven mainly by unequal housing wealth, while the United States combined high inequality on both measures.

Housing equity, not financial assets or income, best explained why some countries had far more unequal wealth distributions than others. That one factor stood out.

Zucman’s (2019) review of global wealth data found a similar pattern of rising concentration at the very top. The richest one percent’s share of wealth has grown sharply in the United States, and to a lesser degree across China and Europe, since the 1980s.

It differs from the housing story above.

This rise is driven mainly by financial and business assets at the top.

Together, the two studies show that Weber’s insistence on more than one axis of resource ownership still holds up against the best contemporary evidence on wealth.

### What is social stratification?

Social stratification refers to the way in which society is organized into layers or strata, based on various factors like wealth, occupation, education level, race, or gender.

It’s essentially a kind of social hierarchy where individuals and groups are classified on the basis of esteemed social values and the unequal distribution of resources and power.

### What is the main purpose of social stratification?

Ensures Roles are Filled by the Competent:Stratification means that positions are given to those who have the ability and skill to execute the duties of the job. People in higher strata often have higher education and skills.

Maintains Social Order:By establishing a hierarchy and clear societal roles, stratification can contribute to overall societal stability and order.
