Psychology»Social Psychology

Conformity, or majority influence, is when a person changes their attitudes, beliefs, or behaviour to match a group or a perceived social norm.

This change happens in response to real or imagined group pressure, even without any direct request to comply.

Humans are highly social creatures, and conformity lubricates social interactions, helping societies and groups function smoothly and predictably.

Conformity is a type of social influence involving a change in belief or behavior to fit in with a group.

### Types of Conformity

Herbert Kelman (1958) identified three levels of conformity that differ in depth and duration.

Motivation determines which level occurs.

This distinction matters: it helps psychologists predict how long a behaviour change will last.

### 1. Compliance(or group acceptance)

Compliance represents the most shallow level of conformity.

It involves a person publicly agreeing with the group while privately maintaining their original dissenting views.

The primary motivation is the desire to gain specific rewards or avoid social punishment.

- Behavioral Duration:This change is strictly temporary. The behavior ceases immediately once the group pressure is removed.

- Psychological Mechanism:It is driven by normative social influence: the pressure to be liked by others.

- Empirical Link:This was the primary type of conformity observed in theAsch (1951) line studies. Participants knew the correct answer but gave the wrong one to avoid looking foolish.

### 2. Identification(or group membership)

Identification occurs when an individual adopts the behaviors of a group to establish a satisfying relationship.

The person values their membership in the group and wants to be associated with its identity.

While this involves both public and private acceptance, it is often tied to the specific social role.

- Behavioral Duration:This change generally lasts only as long as the person remains a member of that group.

- Psychological Mechanism:The individual adopts the group’s norms to bolster their own self-image.

- Empirical Link:TheStanford Prison Experiment(Zimbardo, 1971) serves as a classic example. Participants adopted the aggressive or submissive traits associated with their roles as “guards” or “prisoners.”

### 3. Internalization(genuine acceptance of group norms)

Internalisation is the deepest level of conformity.

It occurs when the group’s ideas and actions are intrinsically rewarding and consistent with the individual’s own values.

The person accepts the group’s position both publicly and privately because they believe it is correct.

- Behavioral Duration:This change is permanent. The new beliefs become a part of the individual’s core belief system even after the group is gone.

- Psychological Mechanism:It is driven by informational social influence: the need to be accurate.

- Empirical Link:Sherif (1935) demonstrated this in his autokinetic study. Participants continued to use the group-established norm even when tested alone later.

### Ingratiational Conformity

Leon Mann (1969) later identified a fourth type known as ingratiational conformity.

This is motivated by a proactive desire to gain favor or impress others rather than a reactive fear of rejection.

- Motivation:The individual seeks social rewards or “status points” through agreement.

- Distinction:It differs from standard compliance because group pressure is not the primary catalyst. The individual chooses to conform as a strategic social maneuver.

### Why Do People Conform?

Deutsch and Gerard (1955) identifiedtwo reasons why people conform:

### 1. Normative Social Influence (NSI)

Normative social influence occurs when an individual conforms to satisfy the human requirement for social acceptance.

This motivation stems from the fear of being marginalized or rejected by the collective.

Because social groups often punish deviance, individuals align their behavior with group norms to remain “in favor.”

- Conformity Type:This process typically leads tocompliance; it is a superficial change where the individual adopts the group’s actions while privately disagreeing.

- Psychological Trigger:NSI is strongest when the group is important to the person or when the group size increases.

- Empirical Evidence:The Asch (1951) line studies provide the clearest example of NSI. Participants gave incorrect answers they knew were wrong simply to avoid the discomfort of standing out.

### 2. Informational Social Influence (ISI)

Informational social influence is driven by the desire to be accurate and the need for cognitive certainty.

In situations where the “correct” response is unclear, people look to others as a valid source of information.

This behavior is based on the assumption that the group possesses better knowledge or more experience than the individual.

- Conformity Type:This process usually results ininternalisation; it is a deep and permanent shift where the individual truly believes the group’s perspective.

- Psychological Trigger:ISI occurs most frequently in ambiguous situations, during a crisis, or when an expert is present.

- Empirical Evidence:The Sherif (1935) autokinetic study demonstrates ISI. Participants lacked a physical frame of reference and used the group’s estimates to create a stable reality.

### Referent Informational Influence

Beyond the dual-process model, Social Identity Theory introduces the concept of referent informational influence.

This perspective suggests that people conform because they categorize themselves as members of a specific “in-group.”

The individual views the group’s norms as a defining feature of their own identity.

- Motivation:Conformity here is not about fearing rejection or seeking truth. Instead, it is about maintaining a sense of self that is tied to the group’s values.

- Distinction:Individuals conform to “their” group even if no one is watching, as the group norm has become a personal standard.

### Factors Affecting Conformity

The propensity to conform is not fixed but fluctuates based on environmental and personal conditions.

Psychologists categorize these influences into situational variables, which relate to the external environment, and dispositional characteristics, which relate to internal personality traits.

These factors interact to determine the intensity of the pressure felt by the individual.

### Situational Factors

Situational factors represent the external dynamics of a group setting. These variables often dictate the level of normative or informational pressure applied to a person.

### Key Takeaways

- Three Levels:Kelman’s (1958) framework separates compliance, identification, and internalisation by how deeply and how long the change lasts.

- Two Reasons:People conform for normative reasons, to be liked, or informational reasons, to be right, and the two often work together.

- Group Size:Conformity rises sharply up to about three people, then levels off; adding more members barely raises it further.

- Breaking Unanimity:A single dissenter, even one who is also wrong, cuts conformity dramatically by removing the fear of being the lone holdout.

- Ecological Validity:Asch’s line task was low-stakes and artificial, so real conformity over values or relationships may look quite different.

- Not Fixed:Conformity rates vary by culture, era, and privacy, showing it is shaped by context rather than a fixed personal trait.

### Group Size

Conformity rates generally rise as the number of people in the majority increases.

Research suggests that a majority of three to five people creates a peak level of normative pressure.

Beyond this point, adding more members results in diminishing returns; the presence of ten people does not exert significantly more pressure than five.

Asch’s own data put numbers on this pattern. With a single confederate giving the wrong answer, conformity was only about 3%. Two confederates raised it to roughly 13%. Three or more brought it to about 32%, the study’s overall average, after which extra members added little.

### Unanimity and Social Support

Complete consensus within a group creates immense pressure to comply.

However, a single dissenter, someone who breaks the group’s unanimous front, dramatically reduces conformity.

The dissenter does not even need to be right.

Even if they give a different wrong answer, their presence still validates the right to disagree and frees the individual from the fear of being the sole deviant.

Asch (1956) found this was the single most powerful variable of all. Breaking unanimity with one dissenting confederate cut conformity from about 32% to roughly 5%.

The drop was nearly 80%.

Allen and Levine (1968) later showed a similar pattern. Even a dissenter wearing thick-rimmed glasses, implying poor eyesight, still cut conformity substantially, from about 97% to 64% in their version of the task.

### Task Difficulty and Ambiguity

As a task increases in difficulty, the correct answer becomes less obvious.

Individuals then lose faith in their own perceptions and look to the group for clarity. This shift represents a transition from normative influence to informational influence.

The group becomes the expert.

When uncertainty is high, the group is viewed as a necessary tool for accuracy.

Asch demonstrated this directly: he made the comparison lines more similar in length, so the correct answer was harder to see, and conformity rose. Participants facing real ambiguity increasingly treated the group’s answer as a genuine source of information rather than something to publicly go along with.

### Anonymity

The fear of public ridicule is a primary driver of conformity.

When individuals provide their responses privately or anonymously, conformity rates drop significantly.

Removing the “public eye” eliminates the threat of social rejection; this allows the individual to act according to their private beliefs.

Asch’s own written-response variation confirms this. When participants recorded their judgement privately instead of announcing it aloud, conformity dropped to about 12.5%. The pattern replicated elsewhere. Deutsch and Gerard (1955) found the same result using apparatus that screened participants from one another, producing lower conformity than face-to-face testing.

### Dispositional Characteristics

Dispositional factors refer to the internal traits and psychological makeup of the individual. These characteristics determine how a person perceives and processes group pressure.

### Personality and Locus of Control

Individuals with low self-esteem or high anxiety conform more frequently to seek validation.

Conversely, those with an internal locus of control, the belief that one is responsible for their own life events, show greater resistance.

High intelligence and expertise also buffer against pressure by increasing self-confidence in one’s own judgment.

### Cultural Context

Culture plays a defining role in social behavior.

Collectivistic cultures, such as those in East Asia, value social harmony and cooperation over individual expression.

In these societies, conformity is often viewed as a positive trait that strengthens group bonds.

Individualistic cultures, like the USA, prioritize personal autonomy, leading to lower average conformity rates.

### Gender and Socialization

Some studies indicate that females may conform more than males in public settings.

This trend is often attributed togender socialization, the process of learning societal roles that prioritize interpersonal harmony.

However, these differences often disappear when the task is gender-neutral or when participants feel expert in the subject matter.

### Mood and Emotional State

The emotional state of an individual can alter their susceptibility to influence.

People are generally more amenable to group pressure when they are in a positive mood.

Similarly, the relief experienced after a fearful event makes a person more likely to comply with requests or group norms.

### Asch (1951): The Line Judgment Experiment

Solomon Aschdesigned the most famous conformity experiment to test whether a unanimous majority could make someone give an answer they knew was wrong.

- Aim:To investigate whether group pressure could make a participant conform to an obviously incorrect answer, using a task with an unambiguous correct response.

- Procedure:Asch seated 50 male undergraduates among seven confederates who gave scripted answers to a simple line-matching task. On 12 of 18 trials, the confederates unanimously gave the same wrong answer before the real participant responded.

- Findings:Participants conformed to the wrong majority answer on about a third of the critical trials, and roughly 75% conformed at least once. In a control condition without group pressure, participants made errors on fewer than 1% of trials.

- Conclusions:A unanimous but clearly wrong majority can lead people to give an answer they privately know is incorrect. Later interviews showed most conformed to avoid standing out, not because they were genuinely persuaded, showing normative rather than informational influence.

### Jenness (1932): Ambiguity and Conformity

Arthur Jenness conducted the first systematic study of conformity using an ambiguous task. He sought to understand how social interaction influences individual judgment when the “correct” answer is unclear.

- Aim:To investigate whether individual estimates of a quantity would converge toward a group mean in an uncertain situation.

- Procedure:Participants first estimated the number of beans in a glass bottle alone. The group then discussed the total, agreed a collective estimate, and each gave a second individual guess to see if it had shifted.

- Findings:Almost all participants changed their original guesses to align more closely with the group’s consensus.

- Conclusions:Social influence significantly impacts individual judgment. When reality is unclear, people rely on the collective opinion of others to establish a sense of accuracy.

### Sherif (1935) Autokinetic Effect Experiment

Muzafer Sherif conducted a pivotal laboratory experiment to explore how group norms emerge in unstable environments. He utilized a visual illusion to create an objective sense of uncertainty.

- Aim:Sherif intended to demonstrate that individuals conform to collective standards when placed in an ambiguous situation.

- Procedure:Participants viewed a stationary point of light in a darkened room, where the autokinetic illusion made it appear to move. They then judged the light’s movement again in groups of three, pairing two participants with similar estimates against one whose initial estimate differed sharply.

- Findings:Group estimates converged toward a central mean over repeated trials, and the outlier participant shifted their judgment to match the other two. Each group eventually established its own unique, consistent “norm” for the perceived distance.

- Conclusions:When situational cues are unclear, individuals look to others for guidance to resolve their own doubt. This experiment remains the foundational evidence for informational conformity: the need for certainty drives group consensus.

### Critical Evaluation of Conformity Research

Asch’s tightly controlled design has clear strengths. Because he manipulated only the presence of a unanimous wrong majority, Asch could isolate group pressure as the actual cause of conformity. The paradigm also proved highly replicable, becoming a template for decades of further research across cultures and group sizes.

The paradigm also has real limitations. Before looking at each in detail, here is a summary of the main criticisms:

- Ecological Validity:Judging line lengths among strangers is a trivial, artificial task, so real-world conformity over values or ethics may look very different.

- Sample Bias:All 50 original participants were American male college students, and later cross-cultural research shows conformity rates vary by culture and gender.

- A Product of Its Era:A 1980s replication with British students found almost no conformity, suggesting Asch’s high rates reflected 1950s America rather than a timeless law.

- Ethical Concerns:Participants were deceived about the study’s true purpose and could not give fully informed consent, and some showed measurable physical signs of stress.

### Ecological Validity

Psychologists treat Asch’s roughly one-in-three conformity rate as a laboratory finding rather than a fixed constant, because of what the task itself was actually testing.

A line-judgement task carries no real stakes. Getting it wrong costs nothing, unlike everyday disagreements over values, politics, or relationships.

Because the task was so trivial, some participants may have judged that resisting a harmless disagreement was not worth the social friction. Real pressure to conform on a moral or political question could look very different, especially where reputation or relationships are genuinely at stake.

Asch himself only ever measured this narrow, low-stakes case. Group pressure can clearly distort judgement, though not exactly how strongly it will do so on a real, consequential issue.

Psychologists describe this concern with a specific term: poor mundane realism, meaning the task does not resemble a real-life situation closely enough for its results to generalise with confidence.

### Sample Bias: Culture and Gender

Bond and Smith’s (1996) meta-analysis of studies using Asch’s line-judgement task found conformity rates are not fixed. They run higher in collectivist cultures, which prize group harmony, than in individualist ones like the United States.

Rates vary by gender too.

They also vary by the era in which a study was run. This matters because Asch’s original sample was narrow: 50 American male undergraduates from one university in the 1950s.

One sample cannot speak for everyone.

A finding drawn from one demographic cannot be assumed to hold for women, older adults, or people from more collectivist societies without direct testing. The lesson from Bond and Smith’s cross-cultural evidence is not that Asch was wrong, but that conformity itself is shaped by culture.

### A Product of Its Era

Perrin and Spencer (1980) repeated Asch’s exact procedure with British engineering, mathematics, and chemistry students. They found conformity on just one of 396 critical trials, a tiny fraction of Asch’s original rate.

They argued the original high rates reflected the conformist culture of 1950s McCarthy-era America rather than a timeless feature of human behaviour. Their science-student sample may also have been unusually independent in perceptual judgement, which complicates the comparison.

Tellingly, Perrin and Spencer later re-ran the same task using young offenders on probation, with their own probation officers acting as the confederates.

Conformity climbed back toward Asch’s original levels.

This suggests the low 1980 figure was not simply proof that conformity had disappeared from human nature. It re-emerges once the majority holds genuine authority over the participant.

### Ethical Concerns

Back and colleagues (1963) recorded raised autonomic arousal in Asch-type situations, physical evidence of genuine psychological conflict rather than simple play-acting.

That conflict existed because participants were deceived. They were told they were taking a simple vision test and had no idea the other “participants” were confederates working from a script. So they could not give fully informed consent.

Asch did debrief every participant.

Most reported being glad they had taken part, but formal ethics codes did not yet exist in the 1950s, and Asch himself did not treat the findings lightly.

As Asch wrote in 1955, it was “a matter of concern” that group pressure could lead “reasonably intelligent and well-meaning young people” to “call white black.” By modern BPS and APA standards, the study would not be approved today.

### Contemporary Research

More recent work has extended Asch’s paradigm using tools he never had access to.

Berns and colleagues (2005) scanned participants’ brains with fMRI while they completed an Asch-style group task involving mental rotation.

Conforming changed the brain.

When participants conformed to a wrong group judgement, activity changed in brain regions that handle perception. This fits Asch’s own report that a few participants genuinely came to see the majority’s answer as correct.

Resisting looked different.

Staying independent instead activated regions linked to emotional discomfort and conflict monitoring, giving a biological basis to the tension Asch’s independent participants described in their interviews.

Researchers have also applied Asch’s logic to digital environments, studying how visible cues such as vote counts, “likes”, and comment consensus shape people’s expressed opinions online.

The old pattern persists online.

Conformity effects continue in these computer-mediated settings much as they did around Asch’s table. Anonymity online reduces this pressure in much the same way Asch’s own private-response variation did. The perceived size and expertise of the online majority also shifts how strongly people conform.

### Examples of Conformity

- Fashion, Trends, and Fads:People often copy the clothing, hairstyles, and music tastes of those around them, wearing a hat to a wedding, say, to avoid standing out.

- Social Media Challenges:The 2014 “Ice Bucket Challenge” saw millions dump freezing water on themselves and donate to ALS research after friends and celebrities did the same.

- Consumer Behavior and the “Bandwagon Effect”:Advertisers exploit conformity to drive sales: people buy a product, like a phone brand, because peers recommend it and owning it feels like acceptance.

- Health and Lifestyle Choices:Teenagers often start smoking or drinking because peers who already do so make the behavior feel like a social norm.

- Social Roles and Environments:Social norms shape behavior in context, such as college students sitting quietly and taking notes because that is the expected student role.

- Ambiguous Situations:When unsure how to act, such as which cutlery to use at a fancy restaurant, people watch others and copy them.
