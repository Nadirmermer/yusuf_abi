Somatotype and constitutional psychology is a theory developed by William Sheldon that links body shape to personality traits. He proposed three main body types: ectomorphs, who are slim and introverted; mesomorphs, who are muscular and assertive; and endomorphs, who are rounder and sociable.

While historically influential, the theory is now considered outdated and overly deterministic, but it helped spark early interest in howbiologymight shape behavior.

### What is constitutional psychology?

Constitutional psychology is an approach that tries to explain personality and behaviour in terms of a person’s biological or physical makeup.

In other words, it’s the study of how the body’s structure and inherited traits might shape how someone thinks, feels, and acts.

### Key ideas include:

- Heredity matters:People’s natural (genetic or constitutional) differences were thought to make some individuals stronger, more capable, or more “fit” than others.

- Organic focus:Instead of looking at thoughts or emotions directly, researchers likeCesare Lombrosolooked for clues in the body — measuring skulls, faces, and other features.

- Personality and biology:Later researchers, including Sheldon, continued this idea by trying to match body types with personality traits.

- Clinical observation:The approach influenced psychiatry and psychology, leading to attempts to classify people based on physical and mental characteristics.

### W. H. Sheldon’s Three Somatotypes

William Sheldon’s Theory explored whether a person’s body type (or physical build) could be linked to their personality and behaviour, especially criminal or delinquent behaviour.

This idea formed part of an older scientific approach known as the constitutional school in psychology,  to explain behaviour through biological or physical factors rather than social ones.

Its most influential figure was the German psychiatrist Ernst Kretschmer.

He linked three body builds to different mental-health risks: a tall, thin asthenic type, a muscular athletic type, and a short, rounded pyknic type (Kretschmer, 1925).

Kretschmer found pyknic patients were more often diagnosed with manic-depressive illness, and asthenic or athletic patients with schizophrenia. Sheldon adapted this German psychiatric typology into a general theory of everyday personality and criminal tendency, a far bigger claim than Kretschmer ever made.

### The Core of Sheldon’s Theory

Sheldon believed that people could be grouped into three main body types, which he called somatotypes.

He argued that each body type was connected to certain temperamental traits and even to tendencies toward deviant or criminal behaviour:

- Ectomorphs:slim and fragile; thought to be quiet, anxious, and restrained.

- Mesomorphs:muscular and athletic; thought to be energetic, assertive, and more likely to take risks or get into trouble.

- Endomorphs:soft and round; thought to be sociable, relaxed, and comfort-seeking.

Sheldon created a three-digit rating system to score each body type, with each of the three dimensions rated on a seven-point scale.

The rating combined body measurements, such as height, weight, skinfold thickness and body circumference, with visual judgment of a photograph.

For example, someone rated 4–6–3 would show moderate endomorphy, high mesomorphy and low ectomorphy.

### 3. Endomorph(Viscerotonic Temperament)

Endomorphs are characterized by increased fat storage, a wide waist, and a large bone structure.

In everyday language, endomorphs would usually be described as heavy-set or overweight. They have a smooth, round body, small shoulders, and shorter limbs.

### Physical Characteristics of Endomorphs

An endomorph is someone whose body is naturally soft, rounded, and fuller.

Typical physical features:

- Rounder, softer body shape

- Higher levels of body fat

- Wider waist

- Fat stored around the stomach, hips, and thighs

- Curvier appearance (less muscular or slim)

Sheldon linked this body type to the endoderm — the inner layer of the embryo that forms internal organs like the stomach and intestines.

Endomorphs (also known as viscerotonic) were seen as relaxed, sociable, tolerant, comfort-loving, peaceful, good-humored, and in need of affection.

### Critical Evaluation

William H. Sheldon’s theory of Constitutional Psychology and his system of somatotypes (Endomorph, Mesomorph, and Ectomorph) have faced strong and lasting criticism.

Although Sheldon aimed to create a scientific link between body type and personality, his ideas have since been discredited in modern psychology.

Critics have raised concerns about his methods, scientific validity, and ideological bias, as well as the ethical implications of linking physical appearance to personality and worth.

### 1. Scientific and Methodological Problems

Sheldon claimed to measure body types scientifically, but his system relied heavily on visual judgment and personal interpretation.

- Lack of consistency:Results could vary widely depending on who made the assessment.

- Measurement errors:Physical data like body fat or muscle size were often taken inconsistently.

- Subjective scoring:Somatotype ratings (1–7 for each type) depended too much on personal opinion rather than objective data.

One of his former assistants, Barbara Honeyman Heath, accused Sheldon of manipulating data to fit his own ideas.

She alleged that he altered or edited photographs used inAtlas of Mento match predetermined body-type categories — raising serious doubts about the honesty of his research.

Modern psychologists argue that Sheldon’s systemreduces complex human behaviourto something far too simple.

- Too deterministic:It suggests that people’s personalities are fixed by their body type, leaving little room for personal growth or environmental influence.

- Lack of evidence:Later research found no reliable proof that body type predicts personality traits.

- Ignoring other factors:Personality is now understood to result from a mix of genetics, environment, learning, and culture, not just physique.

Although Sheldon’s ideas about personality are now scientifically invalid, his system still holds historical significance. Its influence spans research methods, sports science, and, more troublingly, the biases his stereotypes left behind.

### 5. Contemporary Research

No modern researcher defends Sheldon’s central claim that body type causes or predicts criminal behaviour. The theory is dead. But contemporary research asks a related question: how far has the stereotype it created outlived the science behind it?

A 2008 study re-tested Sheldon’s own hypothesis using a modern offender sample. It swapped his subjective photo ratings for a standard modern measure, body mass index (Maddan, Walker, & Miller, 2008). The update was overdue.

Working with a US state prison population, the study found a modest link between higher body mass and criminal involvement.

The researchers concluded this reflected ordinary social effects of size, such as looking more threatening or being harder to restrain, not any fixed biological cause. The link survives. It is just far weaker, and no longer deterministic.

The strongest evidence comes from real policing records, not a lab experiment.

- Aim:To test whether officers’ perceptions of a stopped person’s body size, independent of race, predict a frisk, a search, or the use of force (Milner, George, & Allison, 2016).

- Method:The researchers analysed over three million NYPD stop records from 2006–2013, coding each person’s estimated height, weight, race, and whether the stop escalated.

- Results:Tall, heavy Black and Hispanic men faced the greatest risk of being frisked or searched. Tall, heavy suspects of any race faced a higher risk of police force.

- Conclusion:Perceived body size acts as an independent risk factor for adverse police treatment that adds to, rather than replaces, racial disparity.

This is one of the largest studies of its kind, drawing on several million real stops rather than a survey. Its main limitation is that officers’ size estimates are a perception, not a verified measurement. This caveat matters.

That is arguably the point: a perceived muscular build, exactly the mesomorphic type Sheldon linked to crime, measurably raises the risk of harsher treatment.Implicit-bias trainingalready used in policing could reasonably extend to this same body-size cue.

A body-type bias also shows up ineyewitness testimony. In one study, participants watched a mock crime and then tried to identify the culprit from a photo lineup.

They were significantly more likely to wrongly pick a muscular foil over an overweight or average-build one. Build has no bearing on guilt (Shaw & Wafler, 2016).

A mesomorphic build raises perceived guilt at the moment of identification, not only at the moment of arrest.

Read together, these two studies triangulate on the same conclusion from two different stages of the justice system: the police stop and the courtroom identification that may follow it. Sheldon’s biology was wrong. His stereotype still does damage.

### Sheldon’s Study:Varieties of Delinquent Youth (1949)

William H. Sheldon’sVarieties of Delinquent Youth: An Introduction to Constitutional Psychiatry(1949) was the third book in his Human Constitution Series.

It was his most ambitious — and most controversial — attempt to apply his somatotype theory (body type classification) to human behaviour, especially to crime and delinquency.

### 1. Core Idea: The Muscular “Criminal Type”

The book’s main claim was that body type predicts a tendency toward criminal behaviour.

Sheldon focused particularly on the mesomorphic body type — muscular, strong, and athletic — and argued that this physique was more likely to be associated with delinquency and aggression.

- Sheldon compared photographs of criminals and non-criminals, concluding that muscular builds (mesomorphs) were most common among delinquents.

- He connected this body type to what he called the Somatotonic temperament — energetic, assertive, and dominant.

- He also placed the mesomorph in a psychiatric framework, linking it to paranoid schizophrenia and suggesting that “heroism and paranoia” could arise from the same temperament.

### 2. The “Heroic Delinquent” and the “Temperamental Superman”

Although Sheldon described the mesomorphic type as prone to delinquency, he also idealised certain muscular individuals as “biological heroes.”

- He wrote about a “disturbing relationship between delinquency and heroism,” arguing that some delinquents showed admirable courage and defiance.

- These “heroic types” were, in his words, “splendid physical specimens” who had been misunderstood by society and “forced underground into prisons and mental hospitals.”

Sheldon believed that human worth and morality were biologically determined.

He argued that modern society was in decline because it allowed “bad reproduction to drive out good.”

- “Irresponsible reproduction” (promiscuous breeding) as the highest form of delinquency.

- Modern medicine and democracy were “keeping the weak alive indiscriminately,” leading to biological and moral degeneration.

- His ultimate goal was a program he called “biological humanics” — a eugenic vision where obeying God meant obeying biological laws of selective breeding.

### The Posture-Photograph Programme

Sheldon’s ratings depended on a huge, standardised photographic archive. Building it had real, documented consequences for tens of thousands of ordinary people, not just for the pages of his books. The practice shows that his constitutional psychology was never purely academic.

### A Routine “Posture” Assessment

From the 1940s into the early 1970s, several elite American universities and preparatory schools required incoming students, men and women alike, to be measured and photographed.

Students were typically photographed unclothed or in minimal clothing, posed against a calibrated grid backdrop. Privacy was minimal. The stated purpose was a routine “posture” assessment, meant to spot and correct poor spinal alignment.

In practice, the resulting images and measurements fed constitutional-typing research, including Sheldon’s own, into the supposed links between physique and personality, intelligence, or criminal propensity. Few students questioned it.

Most students were simply told this was a health requirement, not a research study. They had no real sense of taking part in someone else’s science. Meaningful,informed consent, in anything like the modern sense, was largely absent.

### A Documented Account of Institutional Harm

This practice’s long institutional life shows how a scientifically weak, ideologically loaded theory can still leave a durable mark on ordinary lives, well outside the laboratory.

A physician who was photographed as a schoolboy later published a first-hand account of the programme (Sklar, 2019). He described vivid, lasting embarrassment among his own cohort.

Years later, at a school reunion, former classmates still joked uneasily about the images’ potential for blackmail.

Sklar argues the episode is an early, unusually stark example of “shame culture” in institutional life. It combined surveillance and humiliation, justified by supposedly scientific goals. That cost was never disclosed to those who bore it, nor weighed against the research’s dubious scientific value.

The harm was not confined to Sheldon’s books. This same eugenic worldview, discussed in the Critical Evaluation above, here shaped a real research practice. Its data-collection infrastructure imposed a genuine, well-documented cost on a generation of students who never chose to take part.

### The Gluecks’ Studies on Physique and Delinquency

The husband-and-wife research team Sheldon Glueck and Eleanor Glueck are best known for their large-scale studies of juvenile delinquency, which became milestones in criminological research.

Building on William H. Sheldon’s ideas about body type (somatotyping), they explored whether physical build, especially muscularity (mesomorphy), might be linked to criminal behaviour.

Their main works —Unraveling Juvenile Delinquency(1950) andPhysique and Delinquency(1956) — reflected the positivist and multidimensional tradition in criminology.

### 1. Context and Research Design

The Gluecks aimed to better understand the causes of crime by using a rigorous, data-driven approach that included both biological and social variables.

While they were influenced by Sheldon’s ideas, they were far more scientific and cautious in their conclusions.

- Instead, they proposed that biological traits (like strength or energy) may influence how individuals interact with their environment.

Unlike Sheldon, the Gluecks saw physique as one factor among many.

They wrote that biological features “set the context for social forces”, rather than determine behaviour outright.This made their theory more balanced and less deterministic than Sheldon’s biological approach.

### 3. Evaluation and Criticism

Despite their cautious and data-rich approach, the Gluecks’ inclusion of body type still drew sharp criticism — especially from sociologists who rejected biological explanations of crime.

The most prominent critic was Edwin H. Sutherland, a leading sociological criminologist best known for hisDifferential Association Theory(which argued that crime is learned through social interaction).

- Sutherland’s stance:Crime should be explained purely through social factors, not biology or individual traits.

- He viewed the Gluecks’ work — which included physique, intelligence, and personality — as athreat to the sociological focusof criminology.

- Sutherland accused them of using“scanty data”and called their conclusions“methodologically unsound” and “invalid.”

- As a result, many sociologistsdismissed the Gluecks’ researchas belonging to the same tradition as Sheldon’s and other “biological determinists,” even though the Gluecks themselvescriticised Sheldon’s methodsfor being unscientific and biased.
