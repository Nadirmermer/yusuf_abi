### Giriş

Psychology»Social Psychology

Sociology and psychology are closely related disciplines that complement each other in understanding human behavior and social phenomena.

Psychology studies the mind of an individual to understand human behavior and social and emotional reactions. In contrast, sociology looks beyond individuals and studies society, social behavior, and social institutions.

Sociology provides a broader perspective, examining how social structures, cultural norms, and societal factors shape individuals and groups. It looks at institutions and interaction, not just the person.

Psychology instead focuses on the individual: internal processes, cognition, emotion, and motivation. It asks how personal experience and traits feed back into social life.

Both fields study human behavior. They meet directly insocial psychology. This field explores how individual thought and social influence interact, including group dynamics, social cognition, and identity formation.

### Differences Between Psychology and Sociology

- Focus:Psychology is the study of the emotions and behavior of an individual, whereas sociologists look beyond the individual to examine societal institutions and groups of people.

- Licensing:Psychologists typically need to pass a test to become licensed, while sociologists have no licensing requirements.

- Diagnosis:Psychologists can diagnose and treat mental health disorders whereas sociologists cannot.

- Training:Psychologists must understand basic medical science, such as the biological processes of the brain, whereas sociologists must have a strong understanding of social theory and public policy.

- Careers:Sociologists tend to work in social work, social justice, and social services while psychologists are usually better equipped to work in human resources, health clinics, or counseling.

### Similarities Between Psychology and Sociology

- Shared Science:Both psychology and sociology are social sciences that involve the study of human thought processes and behaviors.

- Shared Goal:Both fields are concerned with improving people’s lives and bettering society.

- Shared Skills:Both professions utilize communication, interpersonal, analytical, research, listening, observational, and problem-solving skills.

- Career Versatility:Both psychology and sociology degrees are versatile and can lead to a wide range of career opportunities.

### Levels of Analysis: The Individual and the Social

Psychology and sociology study the same subject matter: human behavior. What separates them is the level at which each discipline looks for its explanation.

Neither level is more correct than the other.

Sociology typically works at the macro level, explaining patterns across whole populations and institutions. Psychology typically works at the level of the individual: a person’s cognition, emotion, and personal history.

### Explaining the Self: Two Different Starting Points

Sociologists and psychologists have each tried to explain how a person develops a stable sense of self, starting from opposite ends.

EriksonandPiagetlocated the answer inside the individual, in stage-based psychological development.

Sociologist Charles Cooley took the opposite view.

His concept of thelooking-glass self(Cooley, 1902) held that a person’s self-understanding is built from how they believe other people see them, not from internal maturation alone.

Mead (1934) took this further.

A self only becomes possible once a child learns to take another person’s perspective, and eventually society’s. This is the seed of what sociologists callsymbolic interactionism, meaning built through everyday interaction.

Neither account is wrong. Each answers a different question about the same thing.

### A Genetic Layer Behind a “Social” Outcome

Criminologists have long treated a teenager’s delinquent friendships as a purely environmental outcome, shaped by neighborhood and family supervision. One study tested whether genetics plays a role too.

- Aim:Yun, Cheong, and Walsh (2011) tested whether genetic factors are really involved in delinquent peer-group affiliation, using a stricter measure of peer delinquency than earlier studies had used.

- Method:The researchers reanalyzed sibling data from a large U.S. adolescent health survey, replacing the original study’s reliance on a teenager’s own report of their friends’ behavior with a peer-reported measure instead.

- Results:Even with this more independent measure, the genetic association with delinquent peer affiliation held up, closely replicating the earlier finding.

- Conclusion:Some of what looks like a purely social process has a real dispositional component, operating through traits that make some teenagers more likely to select into delinquent peer groups.

- Evaluation:This is one modest study, not a landmark finding. Genetic-design studies like this carry their own statistical assumptions. Still, it cleanly shows that the individual and the social can both be true explanations of the same case.

This does not mean peer networks and family environment do not matter. It means an individual-level layer of explanation survives alongside them, rather than being explained away by them, in the very same case.

### What is Psychology?

According to the American Psychological Association,psychologyis the scientific study of the mind and behavior of individual people.

Psychologists aim to understand the mental processes behind the behavior of individuals, or groups.

They study the mind, how it works, and how it affects behavior. Psychologists focus on social and emotional factors like anger, intelligence, aging, anxiety, depression, and stress.

### What You Study in Psychology

Psychologists look into the cognitive and emotional processes of individuals and try to understand how they influence human behavior.

Coursework in psychology might include topics such as human development, psychopathology, statistics, mental illness, and personality.

Psychologists are involved in research such as cognition, attention, emotion, brain functioning, motivation, and perception.

Psychology splits into several specific subfields. Students can study behavioral neuroscience, clinical psychology, cognitive psychology, developmental psychology, social psychology, quantitative psychology, and abnormal psychology.

### Career Paths in Psychology

Similarly, there is a plethora of potential careers that someone with a psychology degree can pursue.

Psychologists can work in outpatient facilities or offices, either as an individual practitioner in a private practice or as part of a group practice.

They can also work in hospitals, health clinics, schools and universities, sports teams, rehabilitation centers, or for large corporations as organizational psychologists.

Many psychologists take a therapeutic role. They practice psychotherapy in clinical, counseling, or school settings. Other psychologists conduct scientific research in academic settings on topics relating to mental processes and behavior.

### What is Sociology?

Sociologyis a social science that studies human social relationships and institutions. Sociology looks past individual differences to examine groups of people – families, nations, companies, communities, etc.

Sociologists strive to understand how human action and consciousness are shaped by surrounding cultural and social structures.

Sociologists are concerned with people’s reactions to certain physical characteristics and how these reactions affect individuals in society.

Sociology’s subject matter ranges widely. Topics include the causes and consequences of love, racial and gender identity, family conflict, aging, poverty, crime, and religious faith. Other topics include population growth and migration, war and peace, and economic development.

### What You Study in Sociology

Sociologists try to answer questions about current social issues and learn about the ways diverse communities interact with each other.

Coursework in sociology might focus on globalization, race and ethnicity, diversity and inequality, methods of research, and social problems.

Theresearch methodssociologists use are varied but include conducting large-scale surveys, interpreting historical documents, analyzing census data, studying video-taped interactions, interviewing participants of groups, or conducting laboratory experiments.

Students learn to think critically about human social life and know how to help others understand the way the social world works.

### Career Paths in Sociology

Careers in sociology are essentially limitless. Sociologists can be employed by research institutes, criminal justice systems, public health and welfare organizations, private businesses, law firms, international agencies, medical centers, educational institutions, advertising firms, and more.

They often work as social researchers, case workers, paralegals, public relations workers, administrators, community organizers, public policy researchers, or data analysts.

According to the American Sociological Association, about a quarter of those with a bachelor’s degree in sociology work in social services, as either counselors or psychologists.

### Critical Evaluation

Each discipline’s greatest strength creates a matching blind spot. Comparing the two side by side shows what each one offers, and where it needs the other’s perspective.

### Psychology’s Blind Spot: WEIRD Samples and the Replication Crisis

The controlled experiment gives psychology real precision: it can isolate one mechanism, manipulate it, and measure the effect. But its evidence base is narrow.

Much of psychology’s research has drawn on undergraduate students at wealthy, Western universities. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) named theseWEIRD samples: Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic.

They showed these samples are often poor proxies for humanity in general, from visual perception to moral reasoning.

This pattern compounds a second problem. A large, pre-registered attempt to replicate 100 studies from three leading psychology journals reproduced the original result in fewer than half of cases (Open Science Collaboration, 2015).

Most of the failed replications involved effects that were statistically weaker or more surprising in the first place.

Sociology’s comparative, cross-national tradition is one of the more natural correctives to both problems.

### Sociology’s Blind Spot: Explaining Away the Individual

Sociology’s mirror-image risk works the other way. A structural explanation can overreach.

It can treat a person’s position in society as though it fully determines their outcome, understating real differences in temperament, motivation, and biology between people. Durkheim’s own term for psychology’s mirror-image error was psychologism; this reverse error is sometimes labeled sociologism.

Random assignment is the sticking point.

Sociology has also historically found controlled causal tests harder to run. It is difficult, for practical and ethical reasons, to randomly assign whole communities to different levels of inequality the way a psychologist can randomly assign individuals to a lab condition.

This gap has narrowed.

Field and survey experiments have grown within sociology itself, including audit studies that test for discrimination under controlled, randomized conditions in real institutions.

### Contemporary Research

Real-world problems increasingly need both levels of explanation working together, especially where behavior has to shift at population scale, quickly.

- Aim:Van Bavel and colleagues (2020) set out to synthesize evidence-based insight from across the psychological and behavioral sciences that could inform government and public-health responses to the COVID-19 pandemic.

- Method:A team of more than 40 researchers reviewed the existing research literature across six domains, including threat perception, science communication, and the social and cultural context of behavior, condensing each into concrete recommendations for policymakers.

- Results:Compliance with public-health measures was shaped less by individual risk perception alone than by social norms, and by pre-existing inequality, culture, and political polarization.

- Conclusion:Effective pandemic policy could not rely on individual-level psychological insight alone, nor on structural policy levers alone; it needed both working together.

The review’s authors argued that although COVID-19 is caused by a virus, its societal impact is produced through social processes. Understanding a population’s inequalities, they concluded, matters as much as understanding any individual’s psychology.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:Psychology studies the mind and behavior of the individual; sociology studies society, social structures, and institutions.

- Level of Analysis:Psychology explains behavior at the individual level; sociology explains it at the level of groups, institutions, and whole populations.

- Overlap:The two fields meet directly in social psychology, which studies how individual thought and social influence interact.

- Careers:Psychologists must pass a licensing exam and can diagnose mental health disorders; sociologists face no licensing requirement and cannot.

- Modern Research:A 2020 review found that effective COVID-19 policy needed both individual psychology and social context, such as norms and inequality, working together.

- Evaluation:Each discipline has a blind spot: psychology’s evidence base has leaned on narrow, WEIRD samples, while sociology can understate individual variation.

### What is the difference between ethnomethodology and sociology?

Sociology encompasses broad studies of social interactions and structures, including the symbolic interactionism framework that focuses on interpersonal symbols and meanings.

Ethnomethodology, a subset of sociology, specifically examines the daily methods people use to perceive and construct social reality, diverging from broader sociological analyses.
