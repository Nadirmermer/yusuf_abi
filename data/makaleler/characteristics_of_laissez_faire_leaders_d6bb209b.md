Laissez-faire leadership is aleadership stylein which the leader gives group members minimal (if any) guidance.

In this leadership style, group members assume full responsibility for the group. They must “determine goals, make decisions, and resolve problems on their own” (Sharma & Singh, 2013).

The leader steps back. “Laissez-faire” means “leave alone” in French.

The leader takes a minimal role in the group’s inner functions.

Kurt Lewin identified the style as the opposite ofautocratic leadership, in which one leader holds decision-making power with little input from the group (Lewin, 1939).

He did not favor it himself.

It tends to work best at senior levels of an organization, where leaders appoint other experienced leaders and let them solve problems independently (Lewin, 1939).

This style requires group members with certain characteristics. Like any leadership style, it has advantages and disadvantages.

### Key Takeaways

- Hands-Off Style:Laissez-faire leaders give group members full responsibility for goals, decisions, and problem-solving, and stay out of day-to-day involvement.

- Needs Skilled Teams:It works best when members are highly skilled, experienced, and trustworthy, and should be avoided when they are not (Sharma & Singh, 2013).

- Key Benefits:It can build personal responsibility, encourage creativity, and support faster decision-making and hands-on learning.

- Key Risks:Common downsides include role ambiguity, low accountability, and a perceived lack of leadership.

- Modern Evidence:Recent systematic reviews link excessive autonomy to lower satisfaction and productivity, and higher burnout, especially in education settings.

### “Hands-Off Style” Leadership

A hands-off approach is perhaps the fundamental characteristic of laissez-faire leadership, as the “laissez-faire leadership style is also known as the “hands-off” style” (Sharma & Singh, 2013).

In this leadership style, the leader is absent from many of the group’s functions and leaves decisions in the hands of group members.

This suits people who work well with little direction.

It can create problems for people who need more guidance to complete tasks effectively.

### Provide Training and Support

Because the leader of a laissez-faire group stays out of decision-making, they must make sure group members are well prepared for their responsibilities.

A variety of training is usually needed before members can take on such large responsibilities.

The leader must ensure materials and support are available, since they may not be accessible once the decision-making process is underway.

### Trust is Given to Employees

Employees are given so much responsibility in laissez-faire leadership that a great deal of trust is placed in them to keep the group running smoothly.

A laissez-faire leader needs group members they can trust, so the group can progress without the leader’s direct input.

Without that trust, staying uninvolved becomes difficult, and the style breaks down.

Placing trust in group members can raise their confidence and make them more likely to work efficiently and effectively.

### Employees Make Decisions

Making decisions is part of group members’ responsibilities in laissez-faire leadership (Sharma & Singh, 2013). Employees come together to make the best decisions for the group’s future development.

The process should be collaborative.

All group members participate in some way, though how responsibilities are divided differs from group to group.

### Encourages Personal Growth

One advantage of laissez-faire leadership is that, by providing group members with more responsibility within the group, it encourages their own personal growth.

Being trusted with more tasks, and completing them, can boost anyone’s confidence in their own abilities and skills.

This creates a positive cycle.

Confident people tend to do better work, and doing good work raises their confidence even further.

### Builds Personal Responsibility

Laissez-faire leadership also creates personal responsibility, since group members are trusted to set goals and solve problems on their own.

Motivated employees working autonomously can adjust direction and overcome obstacles more quickly than if they had to wait for a leader’s approval, supporting faster course corrections.

This autonomy also supports retention (Chaudhry & Javed, 2012).

A research lab of skilled scientists shows this in action.

The lab leader offers an opinion only when asked, while each scientist solves problems independently and owns their own research.

### Encourages Creativity

The laissez-faire leadership style includes many people in the decision-making process and other important group functions. This brings many different perspectives and ideas, which encourages creativity (Malec, 2021).

Importantly, “such a focus on personal growth and a general trust in the capabilities of individuals creates room for innovation and to be creative without the fear of failure, or rather the fear of the consequences of failure.”

Removing an authority figure to overlook the decision-making process within a group gives people opportunities to make mistakes, which promotes creativity.

### Learning and Development

Another advantage of laissez-faire leadership is that “it facilitates learning and development opportunities…because of its hands-off approach, employees have the chance to learn on their own” (Pathak, 2022).

Direct experience often teaches better than instruction.

A laissez-faire leadership style provides this opportunity for hands-on learning.

### Faster Decision-Making

A laissez-faire leadership style can lead to faster decision-making, because group members do not have to pass ideas by the leader (Pathak, 2022).

Fewer steps mean quicker choices.

This allows a smoother, speedier process, where more decisions get made and the group can progress faster overall.

### Lack of Role Clarity

One disadvantage of laissez-faire leadership is a lack of role clarity, since no leader is involved in assigning tasks.

Without a proper assignment of tasks, it may be unclear to members exactly what they are responsible for.

Additionally, without a leader involved along the way, there is no one available to clarify responsibilities if they become unclear.

### Isolation

Group members may even feel like the group leader does not care about the group in this leadership style (Malec, 2021).

With the leader so removed from the group dynamic, it may seem as though they are not invested. The opposite is likely true.

Choosing this style is itself a sign of investment.

Leaders who choose laissez-faire believe it is the best way for the group to function.

Implementing a whole new style of leadership demonstrates real commitment to the group’s success.

Naturally, a group’s designated leader is normally its authority figure.

For a group to adopt laissez-faire leadership, that leader (or the group’s founder) has to deliberately choose to step back and implement this style.

Interestingly, a 2012 study found that “laissez-faire style is not an important style that boosts the motivation level of workers as compared to other leadership styles” (Chaudhry & Javed, 2012).

This highlights the potential lack of motivation that laissez-faire leadership may cause among group members.

### Low Accountability

Without a clear authority figure or hierarchy, group members in a laissez-faire group are not held accountable by anyone.

Their only real accountability is to the group’s success.

If members are not invested in that success, they have no accountability at all.

### Avoids True Leadership

With the low involvement of the leader in a laissez-faire leadership style, there is a lack of any true leadership, which can be a disadvantage for many groups.

Leadership can be a strong factor in productivity and in moving a group forward.

With all members at the same level and no one in charge, accountability may be low.

Little progress may follow.

### Contemporary Research

Recent research shows laissez-faire leadership carries real costs, not just for group members but for leaders too.

A 2024 systematic review of 64 studies on laissez-faire leadership in education found the style is generally linked to lower employee satisfaction, productivity, and institutional effectiveness.

Excessive autonomy has downsides.

It has been linked to employee absenteeism, increased unethical behavior, workplace incivility, and burnout, even though it can foster creativity in highly skilled, motivated teams.

A 2025 study focusing on leaders themselves found they also suffer from their own passiveness, reporting lower well-being and reduced effectiveness when they fail to meet role expectations.

### When Should Laissez-Faire Leadership Be Used?

Laissez-faire leadership should be used when group members “are highly skilled, experienced, educated, and trustworthy” (Sharma & Singh, 2013).

That does not guarantee success, though.

Even skilled groups may find laissez-faire leadership is not always the best option.

When implementing this style, it is important to hire people who want to work this way.

Forcing people into an environment they are uncomfortable with rarely works well (Malec, 2021).

People respond differently to laissez-faire leadership, as they do to any leadership style.

### When to Avoid Using Laissez-Faire Leadership

If group members do not have the proper qualities for laissez-faire leadership as described above, it would be extremely difficult for effective decisions to be made for the group without a designated leader, and therefore this leadership style should be avoided (Sharma & Singh, 2013).

Laissez-faire leadership should also be avoided when group members are not skilled at setting and maintaining deadlines or managing projects.

It should also be avoided when group decisions require precision and attention to detail.

Small details can be overlooked when multiple people are deciding.

This compounds the lack of role clarity common to this style. If no part of the decision-making process is assigned to a specific member, some details are bound to be missed.

Laissez-faire leadership should also be avoided when high productivity is required.

Group productivity depends on many people working efficiently together.

That is hard to guarantee if members are unaware of their responsibilities, unmotivated, or dealing with other issues within the group.

### Who created laissez-faire leadership?

Kurt Lewin, a social psychologist, is often said to be the creator of laissez-faire leadership (STU, 2014).  Lewin “identified laissez-faire leadership as the opposite ofautocratic leadership” and, notably, “did not subscribe to it as his preferred leadership method”.

### What is the difference between laissez-faire and autocratic leadership?

The difference between laissez-faire leadership and autocratic leadership is that “autocratic leadership favors direct control and oversight in the work environment”, while laissez-faire leadership takes the opposite approach (Malec, 2021).

### Who are some examples of laissez-faire leaders?

Some examples of laissez-faire leaders include Herbert Hoover (former president of the United States) and Warren Buffet (Malec, 2021).  Some important characteristics of a laissez-faire leader are that they are good at delegating tasks, building an effective and balanced group, and genuinely trusting their group members to run the group well.

### How can laissez-faire leadership be applied to education?

Laissez-faire leadership can be applied to education by providing a hands-off approach to learning (Sharma & Singh, 2013).  In a laissez-faire educational setting, teachers may not provide their students with much direction, allowing them to take charge of their own education.

This may be beneficial for students who learn at a different pace than in a typical learning environment, whether they move faster or slower.

### What are the qualities that define a leader who uses laissez-faire?

A leader who uses a laissez-faire style tends to be hands-off, giving their team autonomy to make decisions and complete tasks independently. They trust their team members, encourage innovation and creativity, and provide minimal guidance or intervention.

This leadership style promotes self-direction, fosters a sense of ownership among team members, and often requires proficient and self-motivated individuals.
