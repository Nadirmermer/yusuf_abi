Cardinal traits are rare, overarching characteristics that dominate an individual’s personality and life, such as Mother Teresa’s altruism. They come from Gordon Allport’strait theory of personality. Such traits are so pervasive that they define a person’s identity and actions.

### Key Takeaways

- Cardinal Traits:A rare trait so dominant it becomes part of someone’s public identity, like Mother Teresa’s altruism.

- Central Traits:The 5 to 10 core characteristics most people have that shape, but do not fully determine, behavior.

- Secondary Traits:Narrower traits that only appear in specific situations, such as becoming short-tempered under pressure.

- Rarity:Most people do not have a single Cardinal Trait; a mix of Central Traits is far more common.

- Testability:Allport never specified how to measure a trait’s pervasiveness, so classifying a trait as cardinal is usually done only in hindsight.

- Modern Research:Studies show people’s behavior varies a lot moment-to-moment, which questions whether any one trait truly dominates everything a person does.

### What Are Cardinal Traits?

Psychologist Gordon Allport developed Trait Theory to explain howpersonality(the relatively stable, distinctive characteristics that make someone who they are) is organized. He split individual traits into cardinal, central, and secondary types.

Cardinal Traits dominate a person’s whole life.

Allport was one of the first psychologists to study traits systematically. He argued that Cardinal Traits shape and direct a person’s behavior above all else. Central Traits, by contrast, are present to some extent in everyone. Secondary Traits emerge only in particular situations.

Together, these three levels give a fuller picture of personality than a single score could.

Cardinal Traits are the most dominant traits a person can have, but they are also the rarest. Very few people are defined by one theme that shapes their entire life.

Such a trait becomes almost synonymous with the person themselves. It typically develops later in life and colors nearly every aspect of their purpose, behavior, and attitude.

Most people do not have a Cardinal Trait. They instead have a mix of Central Traits, though famous historical figures are often defined by a single Cardinal Trait.

There are also examples of cardinal traits in literature and myth. Don Juan was so renowned for his romantic exploits that his name became a synonym for heartbreaker and profligate.

Ebenezer Scrooge represented the cardinal trait of greediness. Narcissus, of Greek mythology, was so enamored with his reflection that his name became the root of the term narcissism or extreme self-obsession.

### What Are the Differences Between Cardinal, Central, and Secondary Traits?

Central traits affect but do not determine a person’s behavior. Secondary traits are also present in all individuals and can influence behavior, but secondary traits are strongly dependent upon immediate context, so they are not apparent in all situations.

Secondary traits tend to show themselves in particular situations. Again, for example, you might generally be pretty easygoing, but you might become short-tempered when under much pressure.

Alternatively, an ordinarily calm person might become very anxious when faced with speaking in public.

### How Do Cardinal and Central Traits Differ?

Cardinal Traits are rare but strongly determine a person’s behavior. Central Traits, by contrast, are present in everyone to varying degrees and are far more common.

Think of the words you would use to describe your overall character, such as anxious, honest, friendly, or generous. Psychologists would classify these as your central traits.

Allport suggested that most individuals have about 5 to 10 central traits. He also theorized that the majority of people have many of these traits to a certain degree.

### Critical Evaluation of Allport’s Trait Theory

Allport’s cardinal, central, and secondary traits are still widely taught, but psychologists have long debated how scientific the framework really is. The theory has clear strengths and equally clear limits.

### Strengths of the Approach

Allport’s typology captures something questionnaires often miss: how a single, dominant characteristic can define a whole life and public identity. This descriptive richness anticipated modern person-centered and narrative approaches to personality.

They see a person as more than test scores.

Allport was also one of the first psychologists to study personality vocabulary systematically. That work laid the groundwork for later trait models.

He insisted that a central trait is the handful of qualities a friend would mention in a reference letter, not an abstract statistical dimension. That emphasis on real judgments about real people is one reason the typology still appears in textbooks nearly a century later.

Allport was not opposed to measurement itself. His point was narrower: a purely nomothetic, scores-only account renders a whole person in an oversimplified way.

### The Problem of Testability

Cardinal, central, and secondary traits are defined only by how pervasive they are. Allport never specified how to measure that pervasiveness.

There is no test for where a central trait ends and a cardinal trait begins. The boundary is fuzzy.

This makes the classification hard to apply except after the fact. A historian can label Mother Teresa’s altruism a cardinal trait once her life is complete.

But the theory cannot predict in advance which of a living person’s traits, if any, will become cardinal.

The theory also explains structure better than development. It assumes a biological basis shaped by experience. It does not specify the mechanism behind either claim.

Later researchers responded to exactly this problem. Eysenck and Cattell replaced Allport’s judgment calls with factor analysis, a statistical method for defining traits from measurable patterns in data rather than a reader’s impression.

### What Modern Research Suggests

More recent trait research complicates the cardinal-trait idea. Fleeson (2001) tracked participants’ moment-to-moment behavior for one to three weeks.

He found enormous variation: people were sometimes far more extraverted than the typical extravert, and sometimes far less.

Yet each person’s average behavior stayed remarkably stable over time.

This suggests personality works more like a pattern of tendencies than one trait permanently dominating everything a person does. That is a more modest claim than a single trait directing almost all behavior.

The picture is more nuanced. It does not deny that some people are more consistent than others. But it treats “cardinal” as a matter of degree, not an all-or-nothing label.

In other words, cardinal traits may be a useful shorthand more than a literal description of how anyone truly behaves.

### Is extraversion a cardinal trait?

No, extraversion is one of the “big five” broad categories of personality traits. Extraversion relates to focusing attention outward, onto other people and the environment.

An individual high in extraversion might be sociable and outgoing. This is often spelled “extroversion.”

Each of the “big five” categories is a spectrum. It’s not a fixed type.

For example, extraversion runs between extreme introversion and extreme extraversion. Most people fall somewhere in between.

### Is kindness a cardinal trait?

Kindness can be a cardinal trait. The thing to make clear here is that a cardinal trait is a pervasive characteristic that becomes the person’s identity.

If you dedicate your whole life to helping others, volunteering your time, making donations, and treating people with endless compassion, you can earn the cardinal trait of kindness.

Behavior involves an interaction between a person’s fundamental personality and situational components. The situation that an individual finds themselves in plays a significant role in how the person reacts.

However, in most cases, people offer responses consistent with their underlying personality traits.

### Does everyone have a cardinal trait?

Like mentioned in this article, a cardinal trait is one that dominates your entire personality and, therefore, your life. Hence, we say that cardinal traits are not very common.

Only a few select people have personalities dominated by a single trait. Instead, our personalities typically consist of multiple traits.

### How many central traits does a person typically have?

There is no specific number of central traits that a person typically has. Central traits refer to fundamental characteristics that heavily influence a person’s behavior and personality.

Individuals may possess a range of central traits, which can vary significantly based on individuals’ unique experiences, upbringing, and personal values. Some people may display several central traits, while others may exhibit fewer but more dominant ones.
