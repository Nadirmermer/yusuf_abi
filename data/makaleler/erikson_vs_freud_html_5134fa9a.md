Psychology»Freudian Psychology

Sigmund Freud’spsychosexual theory focuses on unconscious sexual drives shaping personality through five childhood stages, with development largely complete by adolescence.

Erik Eriksonexpanded this into eight psychosocial stages spanning the entire lifespan, emphasizing identity formation through social relationships rather than sexual impulses.

### Key Takeaways

- Psychosexual vs Psychosocial:Freud’s five stages centre on sexual drives and end by adolescence; Erikson’s eight centre on social relationships and last a lifetime.

- Training:Erikson trained under Anna Freud in Vienna, not under Sigmund Freud himself, before developing his own theory.

- Ego vs Id:Freud centred his theory on the id and unconscious conflict; Erikson stressed the ego’s healthy, adaptive striving.

- Health vs Pathology:Freud focused mainly on how development goes wrong; Erikson gave equal weight to positive outcomes, like hope and wisdom.

- Determinism:Freud saw early childhood as largely decisive; Erikson was more optimistic, seeing room for change at any age.

### 1. Focus of Development

Freud’s (1905, 1923) developmental focus ispsychosexual: internal drives and how their repression or fulfillment in childhood stages shape one’s later behavior.

Freud’s model centers on howbiological instinctual drives, especially sexual and aggressive urges, shape early development.

He believed that personality is largely formed in early childhood, as children seek pleasure through different erogenous zones at each stage.

Unconscious drives(thelibido) are the engine of development in Freud’s view, and managing these instinctual impulses is crucial for healthy growth​.

Childhood experiences and conflicts are thus pivotal.

Freud (1905) famously argued that even infants are driven by sexual impulses (polymorphous perversity) and that these early unconscious desires influence adult personality​.

Erikson(1958, 1963) proposed a theory ofpsychosocial developmentthat encompasses the entire lifespan, with each stage marked by a specific psychosocial crisis that needs resolution.

Erikson trained in Vienna underAnna Freud, not under Sigmund Freud himself, before re-envisioning development as a psychosocial process. He emphasizedsocial interaction and cultural contextrather than just instinctual sexual drives.

Each of his stages centers on a psychosocial crisis: a turning point that can resolve in a healthier or a less healthy direction for the person’s personality. The shift in focus is deliberate.

He maintained that while inner drives exist,social relationships and challengesare the primary forces in development​.

Erikson’s theory spans the entire lifespan, not just childhood.

He proposed that people continue to develop their identity and personality through ongoing social challenges at every age, from infancy to old age.

Thus, the focus is on how the ego develops through navigating social conflicts (e.g. building trust, finding role models, forming intimate bonds) in a cultural context.

In Erikson’s view, development is anongoing, lifelong journeyshaped by our interactions with others and our environment, not just by childhood instincts​.

### 2. Stages of Development

Freud (1905) outlinedfive psychosexual stages(oral, anal, phallic, latency, and genital) that occur during childhood and adolescence, arguing that early experiences shape adult personality.

Freud outlinedfivestages of development, each tied to anerogenous zoneand an internal conflict.

- Oral(infancy, pleasure from feeding)

- Anal(toddlerhood, toilet training)

- Phallic(early childhood, curiosity about genitalia and Oedipal feelings)

- Latency(middle childhood, sexual impulses dormant)

- Genital(adolescence, maturation of sexual interests) stages​

Progressing through each stage successfully (i.e. with appropriate resolution of the conflict) was thought to be essential for a healthy personality.

If a conflict is not properly resolved, for example if a child’s needs are over- or under-gratified, Freud suggested the person could becomefixatedat that stage. This could lead to lasting traits orneurosesin adulthood.

For instance, an adult who is excessively dependent or likes oral stimulation (smoking, overeating) might exhibit an oral fixation stemming from issues in the Oral stage​.

Freud’s stages are sequential and mostly confined to childhood and adolescence; by theGenital stage(teen years), he believed the personality structure is largely set.

Erikson (1958, 1963) proposedeight psychosocial stagesspanning from infancy to old age, with each stage presenting a unique developmental task or crisis that influences identity and relationships.

The stages are commonly known by opposing outcomes: for example:

- Trust vs. Mistrust(0–18 months): consistent, reliable care builds the virtue of hope.

- Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt(18 months–3 years): encouraged independence builds the virtue of will.

- Initiative vs. Guilt(3–5 years): the freedom to plan and lead play builds the virtue of purpose.

- Industry vs. Inferiority(5–12 years): mastering the “work” of school builds the virtue of competence.

- Identity vs. Role Confusion(12–18 years): exploring values and roles builds the virtue of fidelity.

- Intimacy vs. Isolation(18–40 years): forming close, committed relationships builds the virtue of love.

- Generativity vs. Stagnation(40–65 years): guiding the next generation builds the virtue of care.

- Ego Integrity vs. Despair(65+ years): looking back with acceptance builds the virtue of wisdom.

At each stage, the person faces a social or emotional challenge (such as developing trust in caregivers, or forming a stable identity during the teen years).

Each stage works the same way.

Successful resolution of each stage’s conflict strengthens the ego and cultivates a psychological virtue, such as hope, will, purpose, fidelity, love, care, or wisdom.

For example, resolving the first crisis of trust vs. mistrust leads to the virtue ofhope. Resolving the identity crisis in adolescence, by contrast, leads tofidelity: the ability to live true to oneself.

The same pattern repeats at every stage.

Unlike Freud’s stages, which end in adolescence, Erikson’s stages ensure that development is a lifelong process.

Even in adulthood, we continue to confront new developmental tasks, such as finding meaningful relationships or keeping a sense of purpose in mid-life. Our personality keeps being refined through these experiences.

### 3. Role of Society and Culture

Freud (1923) primarily focused on biological drives and individual psychology, with less emphasis on social and cultural factors.

### Freud: Society as a Backdrop

Freud’s theory gives relatively little attention to broader social or cultural influences. It is primarily anintrapsychictheory: one focused on the individual’s inner world of instincts and family dynamics.

Development for Freud centres on the family. This includes theOedipus complexand the internalization of moral rules through the superego. He did not emphasize cultural or social differences in shaping the stages.

Freud viewed his stages as universal human drives.

Familial interactions, such as how parents toilet train a child, matter. But larger societal factors were not central to his model. Society mainly enters the theory as a source of repression and moral rules the child’s psyche must accommodate, not as a shaper of development itself.

Erikson (1958, 1963) incorporated social influences into his theory, recognizing the impact of societal expectations and cultural norms on development.

### Erikson: Culture as a Driving Force

Erikson’s theory is explicitly contextual. Culture, society, and historical period all shape development, in his view.

Each psychosocial stage is influenced by social institutions and cultural expectations. How trust is fostered in infancy, or identity is formed in adolescence, can vary by culture.

Erikson had a genuinely global perspective. He studied child-rearing in different societies, including Sioux and Yurok Native American communities, during his own fieldwork. He wove those cross-cultural insights directly into his theory.

In his psychosocial model, the outcome of each crisis can be affected by how supportive the social environment is.

The surrounding environment matters greatly.

Social roles and cultural normsare built directly into the stages. Generativity in midlife is a good example: it involves contributing to society and guiding the next generation, which assumes a cultural context for what counts as a valued contribution.

Overall, Erikson placed much heavier emphasis on the link between the individual and society. Development, for him, reflects both personal growth and social influence.

### 4. The Unconscious Mind

Freud (1915) focused on the unconscious as a reservoir of repressed desires and conflicts stemming from early childhood experiences.

### Freud: The Power of the Unconscious

A cornerstone of Freud’s theory is the prominence of theunconscious mind. Much of the mind, in his view, lies hidden. Freud believed these hidden desires, memories, and conflicts still shape a person’s feelings and behaviour, even though they are not consciously aware of them.

In development, Freud held that the most critical conflicts, such as infantile sexual wishes or aggressive impulses toward a parent, are repressed into the unconscious. These repressed contents keep shaping personality and can surface indirectly, for instance as anxieties,dreams, or psychosomatic symptoms.

He likened the mind to an iceberg. Only a small part is visible. The vast majority of mental processes lie hidden below the surface, in the unconscious.

Thus, in Freud’s stages, a child might resolve a conflict only unconsciously. A boy in the Phallic stage, for example, represses his Oedipal desire for his mother out of fear of his father, or castration anxiety. This repression then unconsciously shapes his superego and gender identity.

Erikson (1958, 1963) emphasizedego awareness and social understandingover Freud’s emphasis on unconscious instinctual urges, marking a move toward a more conscious and rational view of human development.

### Erikson: The Conscious, Adaptive Ego

Erikson emphasized ego awareness and social understanding over Freud’s focus on unconscious instinctual urges. This marked a move toward a more conscious, rational view of development.

Erikson was influenced by Freud and acknowledged the unconscious. But he shifted his focus toward the conscious ego and social experience in guiding development.

He did not delve deeply into repression or id-versus-superego conflicts. In fact, he was fairly indifferent to purely unconscious processes as an explanation for development.

Instead, Erikson argued that humans are largely aware of their goals and motivations as they move through life. The ego, he believed, plays a constructive, organizing role in actively shaping personality.

The ego, he believed, gains real strengths this way. Hope, will, purpose, and fidelity are examples, each won by consciously facing and resolving a stage’s crisis.

In Erikson’s theory, people actively engage in self-reflection and choice. During the Identity vs. Role Confusion stage, for example, adolescents consciously explore different roles and ideas to forge their identity.

Unconscious influences are not denied here. But in Erikson’s model they are not the main drivers of development; ego identity and personal meaning, built through conscious integration of experience, matter most.

### 5. View of Human Nature

Freud (1915) presented a more deterministic view of human nature, with behavior driven by unconscious instincts and early childhood experiences.

### Freud: A Deterministic View

Freud’s theory can be described asdeterministic. Our paths, on this view, are largely fixed by factors outside our control, above all our early childhood experiences and innate drives.

He believed personality is essentially formed in the first few years of life. Later development, on this view, is fundamentally constrained by those early events.

Little, on this account, is left to chance.

If something goes wrong in a childhood stage, such as a fixation or an unresolved conflict, Freud believed the person would carry those issues into adulthood. There is limited room to change course.

There is a pessimistic undertone here.

Adult neuroses and character flaws are treated as direct products of early-life events and unconscious conflict, implying that personality is more or less set by the end of adolescence.

Freud also viewed human nature as driven by instinctual urges, sex and aggression, that society must tame. On this picture, human beings are governed above all by primal urges and childhood dynamics.

Erikson (1958, 1963) offered a more optimistic view, emphasizing the potential for growth and change throughout life, with individuals actively shaping their identities through resolving psychosocial crises.

### Erikson: Growth and Change Are Always Possible

Erikson’s stance on determinism is more moderate. He acknowledged early experience and the unconscious, but also believed in personal agency and cultural influence —positioning his theory between Freud’s strict determinism and a pure free-will perspective.

His overall view of human nature is optimistic and growth-oriented.

He saw people as capable of continual development and adaptation across their entire lives, with each new stage bringing fresh opportunities to build strength, even after earlier difficulties.

This implies real human resilience.

A troubled childhood need not doom a person: a supportive relationship in young adulthood, or a sense of accomplishment in mid-life, can still alter their trajectory. The ego, he added, could harness free will and social resources to grow.

Human nature, for Erikson, involves more than resolving conflict.

It also means finding meaning and contributing to society, which lends a more hopeful tone. Someone who struggled in youth, for example, could still achieve ego integrity in old age by reflecting on life and finding acceptance.

Erikson saw adults as still adaptive. People remain capable of new insight at every phase of life, he argued, by extending the stages well into adulthood. This suggests a dynamic human nature shaped by cumulative experience, rather than Freud’s relatively static endpoint in adolescence.

### Critical Evaluation

Comparing the two theories also means weighing them as scientific accounts of development, not just as competing descriptions.

### Strengths of Each Approach

Both theories have left a lasting mark on developmental psychology, but for different reasons.

- Lifespan Scope:Erikson was among the first to argue development continues across the whole of life, unlike Freud’s stages, which are essentially complete by adolescence.

- Social Grounding:By foregrounding relationships and culture rather than internal drives, Erikson’s theory applies more readily to real institutions and relationships than Freud’s.

- Testable Offshoots:Erikson’s theory inspired further research, most notably Marcia’s (1966) identity statuses, which turned the vague idea of an identity crisis into something measurable.

Together, these strengths help explain why Erikson’s model is now the more widely taught account of lifespan development, despite building directly on Freud’s own theory. It still frames much current research on identity and adult development.

### Limitations of Both Theories

Both theories also carry several well-known criticisms.

- Weak Evidence:Both rest mainly on clinical case studies and psychobiography rather than controlled research, which makes them hard to test rigorously.

- Vague Mechanisms:Erikson’s theory describes each crisis richly but rarely explains exactly how a person moves from one stage to the next.

- Cultural Bias:Both were developed largely from Western, middle-class observation; in collectivist cultures, the timing and even the sequence of the crises may differ (Ochse & Plug, 1986).

- Gender Bias:Placing identity before intimacy reflects a male, individualistic model; Gilligan (1982) argues that for many women the two develop together instead.

Taken together, these limitations are why both theories are best treated as influential frameworks for thinking about development, rather than as settled, fully tested science.

### Contemporary Research

Modern longitudinal research keeps testing Erikson’s account of identity. Fadjukoff, Pulkkinen, and Kokko (2016) tracked identity formation from age 27 to 50, and Meeus et al. (2012) followed identity trajectories across five waves of adolescence.

Both extend Erikson’s account with modern methods, and both find that identity development continues well into adulthood. The strongest test of how identity status actually changes, though, comes from a meta-analysis by Kroger, Martinussen, and Marcia (2010).

That meta-analysis is summarised below.

- Aim:To test Erikson’s claim that identity status moves in a predictable direction across adolescence and young adulthood, using Marcia’s (1966) four identity statuses (achievement, moratorium, foreclosure, diffusion).

- Method:The researchers pooled 124 studies published between 1966 and 2005, combining longitudinal data (tracking the same people over time) with cross-sectional data (comparing different age groups at a single point in time).

- Results:On average, 36% of participants moved toward a more mature identity status over time, against 15% who regressed and 49% who stayed the same. A substantial share of samples had still not reached identity achievement by young adulthood.

- Conclusion:The evidence broadly supports Erikson’s claim that identity moves, on average, toward achievement across this period. But the movement is neither universal nor complete: close to half of people show no status change at all.

This meta-analysis is arguably the strongest empirical test of Erikson’s fifth stage available. It turns his clinical idea of an identity crisis into measurable categories, and it pools decades of independent samples rather than resting on any one study.

But it mostly combines correlational and observational designs. So it can describe the pattern of identity change without fully explaining why some people move toward achievement while others remain foreclosed or diffuse.
