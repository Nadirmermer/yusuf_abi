### History and Overview

All humans have, within themselves, both a light and a dark side. People vary in how consistently they show light or dark patterns of thought, feeling, and behavior. The light triad names three related traits that “light” people exhibit more often (Kaufman, 2019).

The three related traits are (Kaufman, 2019):

- Faith in humanity: an overall tendency to see the best in people and believe that most people are basically good

- Humanism: a belief in the inherent dignity and worth of other humans

- Kantianism: treating people as ends unto themselves rather than unwitting pawns, drawn from Kant’s categorical imperative

The light triad of personality has been studied extensively by psychologists, and there is now a great deal of evidence to support the existence of this group of traits.

Furthermore, research has shown that the light triad of personality is related to a number of important outcomes, including overall mental and physical health, relationship satisfaction, and life satisfaction.

### The Psychology of the Light Triad

Kaufman, Yaden, Hyde, and Tsukayama (2019) ran the founding study that defined and named the light triad. It was the first study of its kind.

Aim:to redress an imbalance in personality research, in which “dark,” socially-aversive traits had been studied intensively while positive, growth-oriented traits were comparatively neglected. The researchers set out to test whether a benevolent orientation is genuinely distinct from simply being the opposite of a malevolent one.

Method:the researchers built the 12-item Light Triad Scale (LTS-12), measuring Kantianism, humanism, and faith in humanity. The scale was brief by design. They gave it to four demographically diverse samples totalling 1,518 participants, alongside measures of the dark triad, the Big Five, and wellbeing.

The results were striking.

Results:the light and dark triads were negatively but only moderately correlated, showing they are not simply opposite ends of one scale. The sample was unusually large for the field.

High light-triad scorers showed a distinctive profile of curiosity, humility, secure attachment, and compassion. The light triad predicted life satisfaction over and above other personality measures.

Conclusion:This was a first step, not a final answer. Kaufman and colleagues (2019) concluded that the light triad is a reliably measurable, distinct orientation towards other people, not merely the absence of the dark triad. It adds predictive value for wellbeing over and above existing personality measures.

The takeaway is simple.

All human beings have both positive and negative personality traits. What differs between people is how patterns of thought, behavior, and dark emotion interplay with light emotion (Kaufman, 2019). Life circumstances shape this balance too.

Dark triad traits are linked to greater childhood unpredictability, aggression, utilitarian moral judgment, selfishness, power, money, and immature defense styles (Malik et al., 2020). Light triad traits show the opposite pattern.

There is also a gender difference between the two triads. Those with high levels ofdark triad traitstend to be younger and more often male.

They are more motivated by power, sex, achievement, and affiliation, and show self-enhancement values and immature defense styles. Conspicuous consumption and selfishness follow the same pattern. They also see creative work and religious immortality as routes to transcending death.

Overall, the dark triad is negatively correlated with life satisfaction, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and self-transcendent values (Malik et al., 2020). It is also linked to lower compassion and empathy. Dark-triad scorers are less likely to believe that humans, including themselves, are good.

### Curiosity and the light and dark triads

The light and dark triads are both associated with curiosity, but of different kinds. The difference lies in intent. The light triad favors stretching curiosity, while the dark triad favors embracing and depriving curiosity.

Stretching curiosity means exploring and taking risks, even when failure is possible. It drives growth.

Embracing curiosity, by contrast, means engaging in taboo or dangerous activities that can lead to harm or reckless behavior. Both types can appear in either triad (Malik et al., 2020).

### Examples of Light Triad Traits

Several historical figures are widely cited as embodying all three light triad traits: Kantianism, faith in humanity, and humanism.

- The Dalai Lama:a lifelong advocate for empathy, compassion, religious tolerance, interfaith dialogue, and gender equality (Cooke, 2020).

- Mahatma Gandhi:led India’s independence movement through nonviolent resistance, religious tolerance, and support for women’s rights.

- Nelson Mandela:promoted racial reconciliation and women’s rights after 27 years in prison as South Africa’s first Black president.

### The Dalai Lama

The Dalai Lama is a well-known example. He has repeatedly emphasized empathy and compassion in human life. He has also written extensively on the need for religious tolerance and worked to promote interfaith dialogue between different religions.

In addition, the Dalai Lama has been a consistent advocate for gender equality. He has spoken out against violence against women (Cooke, 2020).

These themes map onto the light triad’s core traits: humanism’s concern for others’ dignity, and faith in humanity’s trust in people’s basic goodness. His consistent refusal to treat people as instruments for his own ends echoes Kantianism specifically, the trait named for exactly that principle.

Kaufman and colleagues (2019) name him, alongside Gandhi and Mandela, as a widely-cited human exemplar of the light triad.

### Mahatma Gandhi

Gandhi was a political leader in India who fought for the country’s independence from British rule. He is best known for his philosophy of nonviolent resistance, which he used to great effect leading protests against British policy.

Gandhi was also a committed advocate for religious tolerance and worked to promote Hindu-Muslim unity. He supported women’s rights too. He spoke out against the practice of child marriage.

Kaufman and colleagues (2019), and popular treatments of their work, cite him too, alongside the Dalai Lama and Mandela, as a widely-recognized real-world exemplar of the light triad’s benevolent orientation.

His nonviolent resistance also reflects faith in humanity: a strategy that only works if one believes an opponent can be reached through moral appeal rather than force.

### Nelson Mandela

Nelson Mandela was a political leader in South Africa. He fought against the country’s system of apartheid for decades. After 27 years in prison, he was released and went on to lead the country as its first Black president.

During his time in office, Mandela worked to promote racial reconciliation and forgiveness. He also spoke out against gender violence. He worked to promote women’s rights too, and his work bridging apartheid embodied faith in humanity as well as humanism (Cooke, 2020).

Kaufman and colleagues (2019) also name him, alongside Gandhi and the Dalai Lama, as a widely-cited human exemplar of the disposition.

His choice to pursue reconciliation rather than retribution after 27 years of imprisonment is a striking real-world instance of humanism: valuing former opponents’ dignity even when reprisal was available.

### The Light Triad Rating Scale

Kaufman, Yaden, Hyde, and Tsukayama’s light-dark triad scale looks for the traits that sit opposite the dark triad in people who score high on darker emotions (2019). It measures three traits: Kantianism, humanism, and faith in humanity.

Kantianism, a reference to Kant’s categorical imperative (Pogge, 1998), is the ability to see people as ends in themselves rather than as a means to an end. Humanism is the ability to value other humans of all social statuses and apply ethics to everyday life.

Faith in humanity is different again. Those who score highest on it tend to believe that people are inherently good.

Kaufman et al. evaluated these traits with twelve questions rated from “totally disagree” to “totally agree.” They found that high scorers on the light triad also tend to score high on:

- Intellectual curiosity

- Attachment to others

- Tolerance towards others

- A low need to exert power over others

- Agreeability

Kaufman et al. (2019) also profiled high scorers more broadly. Most had stable childhoods. They also reported high spirituality, life satisfaction, compassion, empathy, and open-mindedness.

They tended to be older and more often female than those with strong dark triad traits (Kaufman et al., 2019). Higher scorers also reported more satisfaction with their relationships, competence, autonomy, and romantic passion. Secure attachment was common too.

Motivation matters too.

The light triad, in general, tracks a motivation towards intimacy and self-transcendent values rather than status or self-interest. Status matters less to them. Curiosity, perspective, zest, love, kindness, teamwork, forgiveness, and gratitude are among the character strengths linked to it.

Mature defense styles and optimistic beliefs about the self, the world, and one’s future round out the profile. High scorers also report greater self-esteem, authenticity, and a stronger sense of self than others (Kaufman et al., 2019).

### The Dark Side of The Light Triad

Scholars generally agree the light triad has no serious downsides. It does trade away a few of the dark triad’s advantages.

- Achievement drive:negatively correlated with achievement and self-enhancement motives, and uncorrelated with bravery and assertiveness (Kaufman et al., 2019).

- Interpersonal guilt:associated with greater guilt, which can limit ambition even as it helps repair relationships.

Both trade-offs stem from the same root. The light triad’s low drive for self-enhancement may act as a barrier to achieving challenging goals andfully self-actualizing(Kaufman et al., 2019).

Guilt cuts both ways too. It helps repair damaged relationships, but may also hold someone back for fear of succeeding while others remain less successful.

The pattern reverses for darker traits. Narcissism, by contrast, tends to predict success in leadership positions. Machiavellianism and psychopathy are linked to lower anxiety, guilt, and depression. Dark triad traits, finally, are also associated with being more risk-taking and adventurous (Kapoor, 2015).

### Critical Evaluation

The light triad’s biggest challenge is not whether it exists, but whether it says anything new. Two established trait models already cover similar ground, and later research has had to test the construct directly against them.

### Is the Light Triad More Than Relabeled Agreeableness?

TheBig Five’sagreeableness, and the HEXACO model’s honesty-humility, are the light triad’s closest neighbours. Ashton and Lee (2007) added honesty-humility, a factor capturing sincerity, fairness, and a reluctance to exploit others, to the five-factor structure.

The overlap is strong. Muris, Merckelbach, Otgaar, and Meijer (2017) showed meta-analytically that the dark triad maps onto the low pole of exactly this dimension, and of agreeableness.

Kaufman and colleagues (2019) confirmed the light triad maps strongly onto its high pole. A correlation this strong invites an obvious question.

If a “new” trait sits almost on top of an established one, why keep both? Lukić and Živanović (2021) tested this directly.

They modelled the light triad against both the Dark Tetrad and a combined HEXACO-plus-Disintegration model of basic personality in a large Serbian sample. Neither model absorbed the light triad completely.

A meaningful share of its reliable variance survived even the broader personality model. The light triad still added incremental prediction of social worldview and susceptibility to a militant-extremist mindset once those traits were controlled. Agreeableness and honesty-humility, then, are close relatives of the light triad, not aliases for it.

The overlap remains real, though. It is the construct’s central, still-open vulnerability.

### Contemporary Research

Independent replications since 2019 now converge on the same three-facet structure the founding study reported, well beyond its original Western, online samples. Pechorro, Baptista, Bonfá-Araujo, Nunes, and DeLisi (2024) translated the Light Triad Scale into Portuguese and tested it in 242 Portuguese community and at-risk-of-delinquency adults.

The three-facet structure fit adequately, reliability was good, and the scale predicted justice-involvement outcomes such as arrests and prison sentences. Landa-Blanco, Espinoza-Rivera, Caballero-Juárez, and Mejía-Sánchez (2025) extended this further.

Surveying 715 adults in Honduras, they found all three facets independently predicted flourishing, together explaining around a quarter of its variance.

Applied findings have grown too. All of it, though, remains cross-sectional and self-report. Malik, Shahzad, Waheed, and Yousaf (2020) found that light triad traits buffered the link between abusive supervision and malevolent creativity among junior doctors in Pakistan.

Curtis (2023) found that Kantianism specifically predicted lower academic misconduct among 390 students, an effect explained by lower feelings of entitlement. In relationships, the picture is more mixed.

Huţanu and Holman (2026), surveying 303 Romanian adults in committed relationships, found that faith in humanity predicted less extradyadic infatuation, but humanism, paradoxically, predicted more of it.

None of these designs can show cause and effect. But taken together, they are consistent, theoretically coherent evidence that the light triad’s benefits travel across culture, context, and life domain.

### An Expert’s View

Kaufman led the founding team. As he explains in a 2021 interview on the Stanford Psychology Podcast, personality research spent two decades mapping the dark triad’s “everyday psychopaths” while leaving ordinary, non-saintly goodness largely unstudied.

Kaufman argues that “everyday saints” are more common than most people assume. These are people whose habitual stance toward others is trusting, dignifying, and non-exploitative.

He also underlines a point from the evidence above. Because the light and dark triads are only moderately correlated, most people carry some mixture of both rather than being purely one or the other.

The three light-triad traits, in his framing, are not a rare virtue reserved for moral exemplars. They are continuous dimensions on which everyone can be placed, from the least to the most saintly.
