Psychology»Learning Theories

Social cognitive theory is a psychological theory that emphasizes how people learn by observing others, a process known asobservational learning. It posits that behavior is shaped by a dynamic and reciprocal interaction between an individual’s personal factors (like beliefs and self-efficacy), their behavior, and their environment. Key concepts include self-efficacy, observational learning, and the idea that individuals have agency and can self-regulate their actions.

### Reciprocal Determinism

Reciprocal determinism describes how cognitive processes (personal factors), behaviour, and the environment all interact with each other.

Each factor simultaneously influences, and is influenced by, the other two.

The theoretical foundation forreciprocal determinismistriadic reciprocality, Bandura’s model of human behaviour as acontinuous, mutual interactionamong three elements:

- Environmental Influences(Context): The external surroundings, social context, and stimuli (rewarding or punishing) where the behavior occurs.

- Personal Factors(Cognition): Internal characteristics like beliefs, expectations (especially outcome expectancies, beliefs about what a given action will lead to), and personality.

- Behavior: The specific actions or conduct performed by the individual.

The Dynamic InteractionIndividuals are active agents, not just passive recipients of their environment.Rather than being a one-way path (e.g., environment → behavior), reciprocal determinism emphasizes continuous, bidirectional feedback loops among these three elements.Not only does the environment shape an individual’s behavior and internal processes (e.g., thoughts, emotions, beliefs), but individuals also influence and reshape their environment through their behavior.They interpret their surroundings and make choices that, in turn, adjust their behavior and modify the environment itself.Central Role of Cognition:Personal factors, such asself-efficacy(one’s confidence in their own abilities), are crucial. High self-efficacy influences which behaviors a person chooses to attempt (often learned throughobservational learning), and how successfully they perform them.The Continuous Loop:Your beliefs influence your actions, your actions alter your environment, and your environment, in turn, modifies your beliefs and future behavior.These three factors don’t operate equally at all times. Their relative weight shifts with the situation, and can change over time too.Free Will and DeterminismReciprocal determinism is also Bandura’s answer to the free will–determinism debate. Rather than picking a side, he argued that the environment is a potential force, not a fixed one.Related ArticlesAlbert Bandura’s Social Learning TheoryObservational Learning In PsychologyBehaviorism in PsychologyZone of Proximal DevelopmentVygotsky’s Theory of Cognitive DevelopmentOperant Conditioning: What It Is, How It Works, and ExamplesJerome Bruner Theory of Cognitive DevelopmentConstructivism Learning Theory & Philosophy of EducationThat is because behaviour helps create the very conditions that then act back on it (Bandura, 1978). This is soft determinism: fully caused, but partly self-caused.Bandura summed this up in a single phrase: people are “both products and producers of their environments” (Bandura, 1978).Example 1. Bungee JumpingA helpful example illustrating the simultaneous influence of these three factors is considering whether a person decides to bungee jump at a festival:Behavior (Bungee Jumping):The actual decision and action of jumping.Cognitive Factors:These influence the behaviour, perhaps including personal beliefs and values (e.g., “I am adventurous”) and past experiences with similar challenging behaviours (e.g., prior success climbing mountains).Context:This refers to the specific environment and the reward structure for the behaviour (e.g., the thrilling atmosphere of the festival, the encouragement of friends, or the feeling of accomplishment associated with the jump).According to reciprocal determinism, the interaction is continuous. The festivecontextmight activate the cognitive factor of “adventurous beliefs,” which increases the likelihood of thebehavior(jumping).Completing the jump (thebehavior) then strongly reinforces the cognitive factor (“I am capable”), potentially altering the individual’s futurecontextchoices toward more thrilling environments.Example 2. High School Basketball StudentPersonal Factors: A student believes they are good at basketball (high self-efficacy).Behavior: Because they’re confident, they practice regularly and volunteer for extra drills.Environment: Coaches notice their dedication and give them more playing time. Teammates give positive feedback.Cycle of Influence: The student’s confidence (personal) drives more practice (behavior), which earns praise from peers and coaches (environment). That praise, in turn, raises the student’s self-efficacy further, and they practice even more diligently.Example 3. Office Presentation AnxietyPersonal Factors: An employee feels nervous about speaking in front of groups (low self-efficacy for public speaking).Behavior: Because of their anxiety, they avoid volunteering for presentations or may deliver them with shaky confidence when forced.Environment: Coworkers and supervisors observe this hesitance, and the employee is rarely asked to present important information.Cycle of Influence: The environment (lack of opportunities to present) inadvertently reinforces the employee’s fear. Limited practice opportunities mean the individual’s anxiety (personal factor) never improves, which then continues to shape their avoidance behavior.Example 4. Healthy Eating at HomePersonal Factors: A parent values good nutrition and believes they can prepare healthy meals (high self-efficacy in cooking).Behavior: They spend time planning meals, shopping for fresh ingredients, and cooking balanced dinners.Environment: The family’s kitchen is stocked with healthy foods, and family members offer positive feedback about the meals. Children may begin to request more nutritious options at school or with friends.Cycle of Influence: The positive feedback from the environment (family enjoyment, requests for more healthy dishes) further encourages the parent to keep cooking healthy meals, reinforcing their confidence and interest in healthy eating.Human AgencyWhat most separates social cognitive theory from behaviourism is human agency: the idea that people intentionally shape their own lives, rather than reacting to events around them (Bandura, 1989).You Might Also LikeAlbert Bandura’s Social Learning TheoryObservational Learning In PsychologyZone of Proximal DevelopmentVygotsky’s Theory of Cognitive DevelopmentOperant Conditioning: What It Is, How It Works, and ExamplesBehaviorism in PsychologyEarlier behaviourist models treated the person as little more than a stimulus-response machine, with no real say in what happened to them.Four Properties of AgencyBandura (2001) identified four properties that make a person an agent rather than a passive reactor:Intentionality:Forming intentions, proactive plans and strategies for bringing something about, rather than simply expecting an outcome to happen.Forethought:Anticipating likely consequences and visualising future courses of action, so an imagined future guides and motivates present behaviour, not just the past.Self-Reactiveness:Motivating and executing a plan once made, monitoring and adjusting one’s own conduct against personal standards through self-influence.Self-Reflectiveness:Reflecting on the soundness of one’s own thoughts and actions, especially on one’s own self-efficacy, which Bandura saw as the most distinctly human part of agency.Because people can forethink and reflect, they can also learn vicariously.Watching what happens to someone else can teach a lesson without ever performing the behaviour themselves.Three Modes of AgencyBandura (2001) also distinguished three ways agency is exercised. Most people blend all three in daily life.Personal Agency:Bringing one’s own influence directly to bear on one’s own functioning and environment, rather than acting through others.Proxy Agency:Acting through someone else who has resources, expertise, or power a person lacks, such as lobbying an official or relying on an expert.Collective Agency:A group acting together on a shared belief in its collective efficacy, its joint capability to organise and carry out a shared goal.Collective efficacy is not just the sum of a group’s individual beliefs. It is an emergent property instead.How well a group coordinates predicts what it attempts, how much effort it sustains, and how well it withstands setbacks (Bandura, 2000).This is the same active picture that runs through reciprocal determinism.People actively select and shape the environments that act back on them, which is why individuals are, in Bandura’s phrase, active agents rather than passive recipients of their environment.Observational LearningBandura emphasized that people often learn new behaviors by watching others rather than by direct experience alone.Observational learningunderscores the importance of role models, whether they be parents, teachers, peers, or media figures, in transmitting knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors.This concept became especially famous through Bandura’s Bobo doll experiments, in which children imitated aggressive behaviors displayed by adult models.Origins: The Bobo Doll ExperimentsSocial cognitive theory traces its origins to a series of well-known studies on observational learning: Bandura and colleagues’Bobo Doll experiments.In these experiments, researchers exposed young, preschool-aged children to a live adult model acting violently toward a large, inflatable doll (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961). A follow-up study two years later repeated the demonstration using a filmed model instead.The setup was deliberately simple.This aggressive behavior included verbal insults and physical violence, such as slapping and punching.After being exposed to this model, the children were placed in a room where they were given the same inflatable Bobo doll. Children who watched the aggressive model were far more likely to imitate the specific acts of aggression they had seen (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961).The effect was strong and consistent.In a separate follow-up study, Bandura (1965) showed children a film in which the aggressive model was rewarded, punished, or received no consequence.The punished group changed how they acted.They imitated significantly less aggression than the other two groups, showing that vicarious consequences shape performance, not just learning.This experiment introduced the concept of observational learning to humans.Bandura’s ideas stood in stark contrast to those of previous behaviorists, such as B.F. Skinner. According to Skinner (1950), learning can only be achieved through individual action.Bandura disagreed.He claimed that people and animals can also learn by watching and imitating the models they encounter in their environment, enabling them to acquire information more quickly.The Role of Cognitive ProcessesBandura identified four cognitive processes that must occur for observational learning to take place: attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation (Bandura, 1986).These processes show that learning is not automatic. It depends on the learner’s capacity and incentive.The theory extends social learning with cognitive processes, such as conceptions, judgment, and motivation. These shape an individual’s behavior and their environment in turn.AttentionThe individual needs to pay attention to the behavior and its consequences and form a mental representation of the behavior. Some of the things that influence attention involve characteristics of the model.This means that the model must be salient or noticeable. If the model is attractive, prestigious, or appears to be particularly competent, you will pay more attention.And if the model seems more like yourself, you pay more attention.One notable exception is the televised model. A person on screen can hold your attention even though you have no real relationship with them at all (Bandura, 2003).RetentionStoring the observed behavior in LTM where it can stay for a long period of time. Imitation is not always immediate. This process is often mediated by symbols. Symbols are “anything that stands for something else” (Bandura, 1998).They can be words, pictures, or even gestures. For symbols to be effective, they must be related to the behavior being learned and must be understood by the observer.Motor ReproductionThe individual must be able (have the ability and skills) to physically reproduce the observed behavior. This means that the behavior must be within their capability. If it is not, they will not be able to learn it (Bandura, 1998).Self-efficacy gates this stage. People rarely attempt a behaviour they expect to fail at, so low confidence can block reproduction even when attention and retention are perfectly intact.MotivationThe observer must be motivated to perform the behavior. This motivation can come from a variety of sources, such as a desire to achieve a goal or avoid punishment.Bandura (1977) proposed that motivation has three main components: expectancy, value, and affective reaction. Expectancy is the belief that one can successfully perform the behavior. This is self-efficacy in miniature.Value is simply the importance of the goal the behavior is meant to achieve. Affective reaction is the emotions tied to the behavior itself.Positive emotions make a behavior more likely to be learned than negative ones. Reinforcement and punishment both shape motivation this way.Individuals must expect to receive the same positive reinforcement (vicarious reinforcement) for imitating the observed behavior that they have seen the model receiving.Imitation is more likely to occur if the model (the person who performs the behavior) is positively reinforced. This is called vicarious reinforcement.Imitation is also more likely if we identify with the model. We see them as sharing some characteristics with us, i.e., similar age, gender, and social status, as we identify with them.Reinforcement & ExpectationsReinforcementReinforcement refers to the outcomes or consequences that follow a behavior, such as rewards, approval, or success, and how they affect future behavior.Bandura argued that reinforcement doesn’t just strengthen behavior directly (as in Skinner’s behaviorism).Instead, it works indirectly by influencing what peopleexpectto happen if they act a certain way.Seeing someone else rewarded for a behavior (called vicarious reinforcement) can motivate an observer to imitate that behavior. Conversely, seeing punishment can discourage imitation.Reinforcement can be internal (feeling proud or satisfied) or external (receiving praise, money, or recognition).Outcome ExpectationsExpectations refer to a person’s anticipated consequences of engaging in a specific behavior.More specifically, outcome expectations are the beliefs about what will happen if a certain action is performed.Behavioral Driver:Individuals typically anticipate the consequences of their actionsbeforeengaging in a behavior. These expectations play a central role in influencing the choice, initiation, and successful completion of a behavior (Bandura, 1989).Sources and Subjectivity:Expectations are largely formed from previous experience and observation. Crucially, they also incorporate the subjective value an individual places on the anticipated outcome.Example:A student who highly values academic success will place a higher value on the outcome of “achieving high grades” and is therefore more likely to develop positive outcome expectations related to studying diligently than a student who places a low value on high grades.Self-Efficacy (Belief in One’s Capabilities)Self-efficacyis a person’s belief in their ability to successfully perform a specific task or handle a particular situation. It is not the same as actual skill.In other words, it’s the confidence you have in your own skills to take action and achieve a desired outcome.Albert Bandura introduced this concept as a core part of Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). It explains why people behave differently even with the same knowledge or opportunities.Two people might both knowhowto do something. Only the one who believes they can succeed will actually try, and persist when it gets hard.Self-efficacy is central to SCT for several reasons:Motivation and persistence:People with high self-efficacy are more likely to setchallenging goals, stay motivated, and recover from setbacks.Behavioral choice:It influences which activities or situations a person chooses to engage in or avoid.Effort and performance:Strong self-belief improves focus, effort, and problem-solving under pressure.Feedback loop:Success increases self-efficacy, while repeated failure can lower it, showing the reciprocal interaction between personal beliefs, behavior, and environment.In short, self-efficacy is what turnsknowingintodoing. It’s the psychological engine that drives learning, resilience, and growth in Social Cognitive Theory.Self-efficacy can be developed through:Mastery experiences (succeeding in tasks),Vicarious experiences (watching others succeed),Verbal persuasion (encouragement from others),Emotional/physiological states (managing stress and building resilience).Higher self-efficacy often leads to greater motivation, perseverance, and ultimately better performance outcomes.Self-efficacy is often said to be task-specific, meaning that people can feel confident in their ability to perform one task but not another.For example, a student may feel confident in their ability to do well on an exam but not feel as confident in their ability to make friends.This is because self-efficacy is based on past experience and beliefs. If a student has never made friends before, they are less likely to believe that they will do so in the future.Moral DisengagementMost people avoid seriously harmful behaviour because it triggers guilt and self-condemnation. Bandura’s concept ofmoral disengagementexplains how otherwise decent people override that internal brake. It is a set of cognitive tricks that switches off a person’s moral standards for one act, without the usual guilt.How People Disengage Their MoralsBandura grouped these mental shortcuts into four families, based on where each one interrupts the link between a harmful action and its consequences:Reframing the act:Calling harmful conduct justified, using softened language such as “collateral damage,” or comparing it to something worse to make it look minor.Blurring responsibility:Blaming orders from an authority figure, or spreading responsibility across a group so no one person feels accountable.Downplaying the damage:Minimising, ignoring, or simply disbelieving the harm the behaviour causes.Devaluing the victim:Denying the target’s humanity, or deciding they deserved what happened.People rarely give up their moral standards altogether. Instead, they switch them off selectively, for one act or context. That is why someone can seem kind in most of life yet act cruelly in a narrow role.Evidence: The Execution-Team StudyAim:Osofsky, Bandura, and Zimbardo (2005) tested whether moral disengagement actually operates among people who carry out a legal, state-sanctioned killing.Method:They surveyed prison staff at three US state penitentiaries that carried out executions. Execution-team members were compared with fellow officers who had no role in executions, using scales for each disengagement mechanism.Results:Execution-team members scored markedly higher on moral disengagement. The strongest gaps were on displacement of responsibility, seeing themselves as merely carrying out the court’s decision, and on dehumanisation of the condemned person.Conclusion:Institutions can facilitate lethal conduct through exactly the self-exonerating thinking Bandura’s theory predicts.It lets otherwise humane staff take part in killing without the self-condemnation that would normally stop them.Moral disengagement research now reaches well beyond the death penalty. The same mechanisms help explain bullying, corporate wrongdoing, terrorism, and everyday dishonesty.This shows that the machinery which normally keeps behaviour moral can be switched off, in the right circumstances, for a bounded part of a person’s life.ApplicationsAlbert Bandura’s social cognitive theory reaches far beyond academic psychology. It offers a flexible framework for understanding how people learn, adapt, and thrive across a wide variety of real-world settings.EducationFostering Self-Efficacy:In classrooms, teachers can boost students’ confidence in their abilities by setting manageable challenges, offering constructive feedback, and modeling effective learning strategies.Peer and Adult Role Models:When learners observe knowledgeable or skilled models, they become more motivated to acquire similar abilities. This can lead to improved academic achievement, greater persistence in difficult tasks, and a stronger sense of ownership over the learning process.Bandura and Schunk (1981) showed the mechanism directly: children who struggled with subtraction received proximal, session-by-session sub-goals, a single distal end-goal, or no goals. The proximal-goal group finished with the highest self-efficacy, subtraction skill, and intrinsic interest.Achievable steps, in other words, build both competence and the belief needed to sustain effort — a distal goal alone was little better than none at all.Programs that teach self-regulation explicitly extend this beyond any single skill.Students learn to monitor their own progress, judge it against a personal standard, and generate their own satisfaction from improvement, turning them into agents of their own motivation rather than learners waiting on the next instruction.Public HealthHealth Campaigns:From anti-smoking efforts to nutritional guidelines, public health initiatives frequently leverage Social Cognitive Theory by featuring relatable role models, such as celebrities or respected community figures, to demonstrate healthier behaviors.Community Engagement:By understanding how beliefs, self-efficacy, and social norms influence behavior, policymakers and health educators can design interventions that remove environmental barriers, provide social support, and establish clear behavioral expectations.Bandura’s own applied framework (1998, 2004) treats self-efficacy and outcome expectations as the two levers that must both move for health behavior to change: people need to believe they can act, and that acting is worthwhile.This underlies the entertainment-education strategy, which weaves modeled healthy behaviors and their rewarding consequences into popular radio and television serial dramas, reaching mass audiences a single advertisement cannot.At the community level, collective efficacy — a group’s shared belief in its own capacity to organize and act — has similarly informed campaigns that mobilize whole neighborhoods rather than individuals alone.Organizational BehaviorTraining and Development:Employers use modeling, mentorship programs, and supportive feedback to encourage skill-building and cultivate a sense of efficacy among employees. With the right modeling and feedback, workers become more innovative and proactive.Leadership and Motivation:Managers who exemplify effective performance and resilience can profoundly influence employees’ attitudes and commitment, shaping an environment that supports both individual growth and collective success.Behavior-modeling training follows the sequence Social Cognitive Theory specifies for observational learning generally: employees watch a skill demonstrated by a credible model, practice it, and receive feedback that builds mastery experience, the single strongest source of self-efficacy.Bandura (2000) extended this to collective efficacy, a team’s shared belief in its capability to coordinate and execute a course of action.It is not simply the sum of members’ personal efficacies but an emergent group property.Collective efficacy predicts what a team attempts, how much effort it sustains, and how well it withstands setbacks — a group-level echo of what self-efficacy predicts for an individual.Media StudiesRole of Observational Learning:Audiences learn by watching characters on TV shows, movies, or social media. Whether modeling beneficial behaviors like resolving conflicts productively or counterproductive ones such as aggression, media can reinforce or reshape viewers’ attitudes and actions.Responsible Content Creation:Knowing that people often imitate what they see, content creators can harness social cognitive principles to produce messages that empower and educate, spreading positive behaviors at scale.Bandura (2003) formalized this into a dual-path model of media influence. In the direct path, media inform, model, and motivate audiences straight toward a behavior by showing the skill, the confidence to enact it, and the rewards that follow.In the socially mediated path, media instead connect viewers to social networks that supply the ongoing support needed to sustain change.The same mechanism has been tested for harm: a meta-analysis of over 17,000 participants by Prescott, Sargent, and Hull (2018) found violent video-game exposure prospectively predicted later physical aggression, even controlling for baseline aggression, though the effect was small.Designing Interventions, Training, and Learning StrategiesMultilevel Focus:Because Social Cognitive Theory involves the continuous interplay between personal beliefs (e.g., self-efficacy), observable actions (e.g., practicing a skill), and external conditions (e.g., accessibility of resources), its principles can guide a holistic approach when tackling real-world challenges.Customized Approaches:Interventions based on Social Cognitive Theory can be adapted to diverse populations. By first assessing people’s current self-efficacy levels, perceived barriers, and social supports, change-makers can introduce targeted feedback, skill-building opportunities, and environmental supports to sustain behavior change.Self-efficacy is one of the few change mechanisms with strong causal evidence behind it.Sheeran et al. (2016) meta-analyzed 204 experiments that randomly assigned participants to interventions raising self-efficacy, finding that induced gains produced medium-sized increases in intention and smaller, still reliable increases in behavior.Effective designs typically combine mastery experiences structured so early attempts are likely to succeed, credible models similar to the target audience, and proximal, specific, challenging goals.These give people a near-term standard to judge progress against, rather than one distant target offering no feedback until the end.StrengthsSCT offers a holistic view by introducing triadic reciprocal determinism, asserting that personal factors (e.g., beliefs, self-efficacy), environmental influences, and behavior are all interlinked.SCT does not treat the environment as the only cause of behaviour. A person’s internal cognitions, such as self-efficacy and outcome expectancies, both shape and are shaped by their actions and their social environment.This feedback loop is dynamic: each of the three components continuously influences the other two.The theory therefore goes beyond a linear “stimulus–response” approach. It highlights human agency, since people can proactively change their environments to reach the outcomes they want.ConsequencesBecause SCT addresses these reciprocal influences, it is especially useful for designing interventions that target multiple levels at once. For instance, to promote regular exercise, one can:Strengthen self-efficacy through small successes (personal factor).Use social or peer modeling (environment) to demonstrate positive routines.Encourage consistent behavioral practice (the action itself).This integrated approach means SCT-based programs often show long-lasting results because they consider the person’s thoughts, the social climate, and the behavior simultaneously.However, it can be more complex and time-consuming to implement interventions, because multiple elements (environmental, behavioral, cognitive) must be addressed.SCT placesself-efficacyat the core of motivation and behavior change, making it a crucial predictor of whether someone will initiate and persist in a challenging action.Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to carry out a particular behavior successfully.Bandura showed that people high in self-efficacy generally set higher goals, are more resilient to setbacks, and are likelier to sustain efforts toward achievement.SCT thereby clarifies the cognitive underpinnings of motivation and highlights preciselywhysome learners or patients seem to progress faster than others, even when their abilities are ostensibly the same.ConsequencesInterventions that bolster self-efficacy (through mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion), often see improved outcomes, whether in mental health treatment, academic achievement, or lifestyle changes.This makes SCT-based strategies appealing to teachers, clinicians, and coaches aiming to maintain participants’ engagement.There is a flip side, though. Measuring self-efficacy can be tricky, since it is typically task-specific rather than a single global trait. Not all real-world contexts allow for easy, direct manipulation of someone’s confidence either.Yet, when done carefully, building self-efficacy is a powerful tool for sustained behavior change.SCT is backed by real-world evidence beyond the laboratory, from intergenerational violence to prosocial skills training.Mihalic and Elliott (1997) found that people who experienced more childhood violence went on to show higher rates of marital violence as adults. SCT explains this as the modelling and vicarious reinforcement of aggression passing down across generations.Perry, Perry, and Rasmussen (1986) found that children’s self-efficacy for aggression, and their expectation that it would pay off, predicted how aggressive they actually were.ConsequencesThe theory’s reach extends well beyond aggression.Sheridan et al. (2011) used a modelling-based programme called Skillstreaming with 647 kindergarten children. It produced real gains in listening, following directions, problem-solving, and knowing when to ask for help.Together, these findings show that SCT’s mechanisms can build prosocial skills, not just explain how harmful behaviour spreads.WeaknessesSocial cognitive theory can be criticized for being too broad. It addresses so many factors (personal, behavioral, and environmental) without always specifying how to prioritize or measure them.While SCT’s comprehensiveness is a strength, it also makes the theory challenging to operationalize in tightly controlled research.The theory says that beliefs, environment, and behavior all interact. It does not always specify which variable matters most in a given context, or how to isolate the strongest driver of change.In some studies, researchers end up selectively measuring only certain aspects (like observational learning or self-efficacy), leaving others (like social norms, emotional states) less explored.ConsequencesResearchers and practitioners may find it difficult to design interventions or measure outcomes consistently.This complexity can result in somewhat vague or diluted programs, where it’s unclear which elements of the approach produced success (or caused failure).Moreover, when theories encompasseverything, critics argue they can become less predictive or falsifiable, making it harder to refine or test them rigorously.SCT can appear more like a guiding framework than a precise predictive model.SCT places significant emphasis on conscious cognitive processes (e.g., self-efficacy, expected outcomes) and can underestimate subconscious, biological, or emotional influences on behavior.Bandura does acknowledge affective and physiological factors, but the main focus is on how people deliberately process information about themselves and their environment.Modern research, however, highlights the importance of automatic processes, genetics, and hormonal influences, factors often operating beneath conscious awareness.For example, certain addictive behaviors or strong emotional responses might not fit neatly into self-efficacy and observational learning frameworks.Habits formed through repeated automatic reinforcement might not be as easily changed by cognitively based interventions.ConsequencesBecause SCT interventions focus onrational and consciousprocesses, they may be less effective for deeply ingrained or biologically influenced conditions. Examples include some forms of addiction, anxiety disorders, or depression, when driven mainly byautomatic or unconsciousprocesses.Practitioners may need to supplement SCT-based strategies with other approaches (e.g., exposure therapy, medication) that address emotional regulation, biological predispositions, or unconscious triggers.In real-life, structural or societal factors can overshadow personal cognitive determinants, meaning SCT might inadvertently place too much responsibility on the individual without adequately addressing external constraints.Though SCT’s concept of environment is broad, it tends to focus on how people perceive and respond to their immediate social surroundings.Some critics argue that deeper structural issues, like poverty, discrimination, lack of resources, cannot be sufficiently tackled only by boosting self-efficacy or providing good role models.For example, if a person lives in a food desert, all the self-efficacy in the world will not help them eat a balanced diet if healthy foods are inaccessible.Thus, the emphasis on individual agency can downplay systemic barriers to making healthy or adaptive choices.ConsequencesIf an intervention is designed primarily around teaching individuals coping or observational learning strategies butignores real socioeconomic or institutional obstacles, its success may be limited.Furthermore, critics raise a different worry: “victim blaming.” A person may be seen as failing simply for lacking motivation or self-efficacy, when in fact larger external forces are to blame.When implementing SCT-based programs in low-resource settings, it’s crucial to consider how broader socio-political contexts intersect with personal and behavioral factors.The Bobo doll experiments that the theory’s observational-learning claims rest on were conducted in a tightly controlled laboratory, described by later critics as an “arguably very unnatural environment.”Children were exposed to a single, brief encounter with a model and an inflatable toy built to be hit. That set-up looks little like how aggression actually develops in everyday life.Real aggression unfolds over repeated exposures, in homes, schools and peer groups, shaped by relationships the lab setting cannot recreate.ConsequencesThe lab setting raises a real question: does striking an inflatable doll designed to be hit tell us much about genuine social aggression?Some critics argue the effect is partly an artefact of the setting itself, rather than pure observational learning. Others note that later, more naturalistic studies have found similar patterns, which softens the concern without erasing it.Contemporary ResearchSelf-efficacy’s link to behaviour has moved from correlational evidence toward a genuine causal test. Sheeran et al. (2016) meta-analysed 204 experiments that randomly assigned participants to interventions raising self-efficacy, attitudes, or perceived norms.Boosting self-efficacy produced a medium-sized increase in intention (d+ = .51) and a smaller but still reliable increase in actual behaviour (d+ = .47). That is causal evidence, not just correlation.Moral disengagement has grown from a single dramatic case study into a well-tested organisational predictor. Ogunfowora et al. (2022) meta-analysed workplace studies covering thousands of employees across many organisations.Moral disengagement reliably predicted misconduct and turnover. It also reduced citizenship behaviour. The effect worked through reduced moral awareness, weaker moral judgement, and blunted anticipated guilt, exactly the self-regulatory route Bandura’s theory predicts.Bandura’s claims about learning from symbolic, on-screen models have also been tested at scale. The evidence is now longitudinal.Prescott, Sargent, and Hull (2018) meta-analysed 24 longitudinal studies of more than 17,000 participants, following them for between three months and four years. Exposure to violent video games predicted later physical aggression, even after controlling for participants’ baseline aggression.The effect size was small (β ≈ .08–.11). ### The Dynamic Interaction

Individuals are active agents, not just passive recipients of their environment.

Rather than being a one-way path (e.g., environment → behavior), reciprocal determinism emphasizes continuous, bidirectional feedback loops among these three elements.

Not only does the environment shape an individual’s behavior and internal processes (e.g., thoughts, emotions, beliefs), but individuals also influence and reshape their environment through their behavior.

They interpret their surroundings and make choices that, in turn, adjust their behavior and modify the environment itself.

- Central Role of Cognition:Personal factors, such asself-efficacy(one’s confidence in their own abilities), are crucial. High self-efficacy influences which behaviors a person chooses to attempt (often learned throughobservational learning), and how successfully they perform them.

- The Continuous Loop:Your beliefs influence your actions, your actions alter your environment, and your environment, in turn, modifies your beliefs and future behavior.

These three factors don’t operate equally at all times. Their relative weight shifts with the situation, and can change over time too.

### Free Will and Determinism

Reciprocal determinism is also Bandura’s answer to the free will–determinism debate. Rather than picking a side, he argued that the environment is a potential force, not a fixed one.

That is because behaviour helps create the very conditions that then act back on it (Bandura, 1978). This is soft determinism: fully caused, but partly self-caused.

Bandura summed this up in a single phrase: people are “both products and producers of their environments” (Bandura, 1978).

### Example 1. Bungee Jumping

A helpful example illustrating the simultaneous influence of these three factors is considering whether a person decides to bungee jump at a festival:

- Behavior (Bungee Jumping):The actual decision and action of jumping.

- Cognitive Factors:These influence the behaviour, perhaps including personal beliefs and values (e.g., “I am adventurous”) and past experiences with similar challenging behaviours (e.g., prior success climbing mountains).

- Context:This refers to the specific environment and the reward structure for the behaviour (e.g., the thrilling atmosphere of the festival, the encouragement of friends, or the feeling of accomplishment associated with the jump).

According to reciprocal determinism, the interaction is continuous. The festivecontextmight activate the cognitive factor of “adventurous beliefs,” which increases the likelihood of thebehavior(jumping).

Completing the jump (thebehavior) then strongly reinforces the cognitive factor (“I am capable”), potentially altering the individual’s futurecontextchoices toward more thrilling environments.

### Example 2. High School Basketball Student

- Personal Factors: A student believes they are good at basketball (high self-efficacy).

- Behavior: Because they’re confident, they practice regularly and volunteer for extra drills.

- Environment: Coaches notice their dedication and give them more playing time. Teammates give positive feedback.

- Cycle of Influence: The student’s confidence (personal) drives more practice (behavior), which earns praise from peers and coaches (environment). That praise, in turn, raises the student’s self-efficacy further, and they practice even more diligently.

### Example 3. Office Presentation Anxiety

- Personal Factors: An employee feels nervous about speaking in front of groups (low self-efficacy for public speaking).

- Behavior: Because of their anxiety, they avoid volunteering for presentations or may deliver them with shaky confidence when forced.

- Environment: Coworkers and supervisors observe this hesitance, and the employee is rarely asked to present important information.

- Cycle of Influence: The environment (lack of opportunities to present) inadvertently reinforces the employee’s fear. Limited practice opportunities mean the individual’s anxiety (personal factor) never improves, which then continues to shape their avoidance behavior.

### Example 4. Healthy Eating at Home

- Personal Factors: A parent values good nutrition and believes they can prepare healthy meals (high self-efficacy in cooking).

- Behavior: They spend time planning meals, shopping for fresh ingredients, and cooking balanced dinners.

- Environment: The family’s kitchen is stocked with healthy foods, and family members offer positive feedback about the meals. Children may begin to request more nutritious options at school or with friends.

- Cycle of Influence: The positive feedback from the environment (family enjoyment, requests for more healthy dishes) further encourages the parent to keep cooking healthy meals, reinforcing their confidence and interest in healthy eating.

### Human Agency

What most separates social cognitive theory from behaviourism is human agency: the idea that people intentionally shape their own lives, rather than reacting to events around them (Bandura, 1989).

Earlier behaviourist models treated the person as little more than a stimulus-response machine, with no real say in what happened to them.

### Four Properties of Agency

Bandura (2001) identified four properties that make a person an agent rather than a passive reactor:

- Intentionality:Forming intentions, proactive plans and strategies for bringing something about, rather than simply expecting an outcome to happen.

- Forethought:Anticipating likely consequences and visualising future courses of action, so an imagined future guides and motivates present behaviour, not just the past.

- Self-Reactiveness:Motivating and executing a plan once made, monitoring and adjusting one’s own conduct against personal standards through self-influence.

- Self-Reflectiveness:Reflecting on the soundness of one’s own thoughts and actions, especially on one’s own self-efficacy, which Bandura saw as the most distinctly human part of agency.

Because people can forethink and reflect, they can also learn vicariously.

Watching what happens to someone else can teach a lesson without ever performing the behaviour themselves.

### Three Modes of Agency

Bandura (2001) also distinguished three ways agency is exercised. Most people blend all three in daily life.

- Personal Agency:Bringing one’s own influence directly to bear on one’s own functioning and environment, rather than acting through others.

- Proxy Agency:Acting through someone else who has resources, expertise, or power a person lacks, such as lobbying an official or relying on an expert.

- Collective Agency:A group acting together on a shared belief in its collective efficacy, its joint capability to organise and carry out a shared goal.

Collective efficacy is not just the sum of a group’s individual beliefs. It is an emergent property instead.

How well a group coordinates predicts what it attempts, how much effort it sustains, and how well it withstands setbacks (Bandura, 2000).

This is the same active picture that runs through reciprocal determinism.

People actively select and shape the environments that act back on them, which is why individuals are, in Bandura’s phrase, active agents rather than passive recipients of their environment.

### Observational Learning

Bandura emphasized that people often learn new behaviors by watching others rather than by direct experience alone.

Observational learningunderscores the importance of role models, whether they be parents, teachers, peers, or media figures, in transmitting knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors.

This concept became especially famous through Bandura’s Bobo doll experiments, in which children imitated aggressive behaviors displayed by adult models.

### Origins: The Bobo Doll Experiments

Social cognitive theory traces its origins to a series of well-known studies on observational learning: Bandura and colleagues’Bobo Doll experiments.

In these experiments, researchers exposed young, preschool-aged children to a live adult model acting violently toward a large, inflatable doll (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961). A follow-up study two years later repeated the demonstration using a filmed model instead.

The setup was deliberately simple.

This aggressive behavior included verbal insults and physical violence, such as slapping and punching.

After being exposed to this model, the children were placed in a room where they were given the same inflatable Bobo doll. Children who watched the aggressive model were far more likely to imitate the specific acts of aggression they had seen (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961).

The effect was strong and consistent.

In a separate follow-up study, Bandura (1965) showed children a film in which the aggressive model was rewarded, punished, or received no consequence.

The punished group changed how they acted.

They imitated significantly less aggression than the other two groups, showing that vicarious consequences shape performance, not just learning.

This experiment introduced the concept of observational learning to humans.

Bandura’s ideas stood in stark contrast to those of previous behaviorists, such as B.F. Skinner. According to Skinner (1950), learning can only be achieved through individual action.

He claimed that people and animals can also learn by watching and imitating the models they encounter in their environment, enabling them to acquire information more quickly.

### The Role of Cognitive Processes

Bandura identified four cognitive processes that must occur for observational learning to take place: attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation (Bandura, 1986).

These processes show that learning is not automatic. It depends on the learner’s capacity and incentive.

The individual needs to pay attention to the behavior and its consequences and form a mental representation of the behavior. Some of the things that influence attention involve characteristics of the model.

This means that the model must be salient or noticeable. If the model is attractive, prestigious, or appears to be particularly competent, you will pay more attention.

And if the model seems more like yourself, you pay more attention.

One notable exception is the televised model. A person on screen can hold your attention even though you have no real relationship with them at all (Bandura, 2003).

Storing the observed behavior in LTM where it can stay for a long period of time. Imitation is not always immediate. This process is often mediated by symbols. Symbols are “anything that stands for something else” (Bandura, 1998).

They can be words, pictures, or even gestures. For symbols to be effective, they must be related to the behavior being learned and must be understood by the observer.

The individual must be able (have the ability and skills) to physically reproduce the observed behavior. This means that the behavior must be within their capability. If it is not, they will not be able to learn it (Bandura, 1998).

Self-efficacy gates this stage. People rarely attempt a behaviour they expect to fail at, so low confidence can block reproduction even when attention and retention are perfectly intact.

The observer must be motivated to perform the behavior. This motivation can come from a variety of sources, such as a desire to achieve a goal or avoid punishment.

Bandura (1977) proposed that motivation has three main components: expectancy, value, and affective reaction. Expectancy is the belief that one can successfully perform the behavior. This is self-efficacy in miniature.

Value is simply the importance of the goal the behavior is meant to achieve. Affective reaction is the emotions tied to the behavior itself.

Positive emotions make a behavior more likely to be learned than negative ones. Reinforcement and punishment both shape motivation this way.

Individuals must expect to receive the same positive reinforcement (vicarious reinforcement) for imitating the observed behavior that they have seen the model receiving.

Imitation is more likely to occur if the model (the person who performs the behavior) is positively reinforced. This is called vicarious reinforcement.

Imitation is also more likely if we identify with the model. We see them as sharing some characteristics with us, i.e., similar age, gender, and social status, as we identify with them.

### Reinforcement

Reinforcement refers to the outcomes or consequences that follow a behavior, such as rewards, approval, or success, and how they affect future behavior.

Bandura argued that reinforcement doesn’t just strengthen behavior directly (as in Skinner’s behaviorism).

Instead, it works indirectly by influencing what peopleexpectto happen if they act a certain way.

Seeing someone else rewarded for a behavior (called vicarious reinforcement) can motivate an observer to imitate that behavior. Conversely, seeing punishment can discourage imitation.

Reinforcement can be internal (feeling proud or satisfied) or external (receiving praise, money, or recognition).

### Outcome Expectations

Expectations refer to a person’s anticipated consequences of engaging in a specific behavior.

More specifically, outcome expectations are the beliefs about what will happen if a certain action is performed.

- Behavioral Driver:Individuals typically anticipate the consequences of their actionsbeforeengaging in a behavior. These expectations play a central role in influencing the choice, initiation, and successful completion of a behavior (Bandura, 1989).

- Sources and Subjectivity:Expectations are largely formed from previous experience and observation. Crucially, they also incorporate the subjective value an individual places on the anticipated outcome.

- Example:A student who highly values academic success will place a higher value on the outcome of “achieving high grades” and is therefore more likely to develop positive outcome expectations related to studying diligently than a student who places a low value on high grades.

### Self-Efficacy (Belief in One’s Capabilities)

Self-efficacyis a person’s belief in their ability to successfully perform a specific task or handle a particular situation. It is not the same as actual skill.

In other words, it’s the confidence you have in your own skills to take action and achieve a desired outcome.

Albert Bandura introduced this concept as a core part of Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). It explains why people behave differently even with the same knowledge or opportunities.

Two people might both knowhowto do something. Only the one who believes they can succeed will actually try, and persist when it gets hard.

### Self-efficacy is central to SCT for several reasons:

- Motivation and persistence:People with high self-efficacy are more likely to setchallenging goals, stay motivated, and recover from setbacks.

- Behavioral choice:It influences which activities or situations a person chooses to engage in or avoid.

- Effort and performance:Strong self-belief improves focus, effort, and problem-solving under pressure.

- Feedback loop:Success increases self-efficacy, while repeated failure can lower it, showing the reciprocal interaction between personal beliefs, behavior, and environment.

For example, a student may feel confident in their ability to do well on an exam but not feel as confident in their ability to make friends.

This is because self-efficacy is based on past experience and beliefs. If a student has never made friends before, they are less likely to believe that they will do so in the future.

### Moral Disengagement

Most people avoid seriously harmful behaviour because it triggers guilt and self-condemnation. Bandura’s concept ofmoral disengagementexplains how otherwise decent people override that internal brake. It is a set of cognitive tricks that switches off a person’s moral standards for one act, without the usual guilt.

### How People Disengage Their Morals

Bandura grouped these mental shortcuts into four families, based on where each one interrupts the link between a harmful action and its consequences:

- Reframing the act:Calling harmful conduct justified, using softened language such as “collateral damage,” or comparing it to something worse to make it look minor.

- Blurring responsibility:Blaming orders from an authority figure, or spreading responsibility across a group so no one person feels accountable.

- Downplaying the damage:Minimising, ignoring, or simply disbelieving the harm the behaviour causes.

- Devaluing the victim:Denying the target’s humanity, or deciding they deserved what happened.

People rarely give up their moral standards altogether. Instead, they switch them off selectively, for one act or context. That is why someone can seem kind in most of life yet act cruelly in a narrow role.

### Evidence: The Execution-Team Study

Aim:Osofsky, Bandura, and Zimbardo (2005) tested whether moral disengagement actually operates among people who carry out a legal, state-sanctioned killing.

Method:They surveyed prison staff at three US state penitentiaries that carried out executions. Execution-team members were compared with fellow officers who had no role in executions, using scales for each disengagement mechanism.

Results:Execution-team members scored markedly higher on moral disengagement. The strongest gaps were on displacement of responsibility, seeing themselves as merely carrying out the court’s decision, and on dehumanisation of the condemned person.

Conclusion:Institutions can facilitate lethal conduct through exactly the self-exonerating thinking Bandura’s theory predicts.

It lets otherwise humane staff take part in killing without the self-condemnation that would normally stop them.

Moral disengagement research now reaches well beyond the death penalty. The same mechanisms help explain bullying, corporate wrongdoing, terrorism, and everyday dishonesty.

This shows that the machinery which normally keeps behaviour moral can be switched off, in the right circumstances, for a bounded part of a person’s life.

At the community level, collective efficacy — a group’s shared belief in its own capacity to organize and act — has similarly informed campaigns that mobilize whole neighborhoods rather than individuals alone.

### Organizational Behavior

- Training and Development:Employers use modeling, mentorship programs, and supportive feedback to encourage skill-building and cultivate a sense of efficacy among employees. With the right modeling and feedback, workers become more innovative and proactive.

- Leadership and Motivation:Managers who exemplify effective performance and resilience can profoundly influence employees’ attitudes and commitment, shaping an environment that supports both individual growth and collective success.

Behavior-modeling training follows the sequence Social Cognitive Theory specifies for observational learning generally: employees watch a skill demonstrated by a credible model, practice it, and receive feedback that builds mastery experience, the single strongest source of self-efficacy.

Bandura (2000) extended this to collective efficacy, a team’s shared belief in its capability to coordinate and execute a course of action.

It is not simply the sum of members’ personal efficacies but an emergent group property.

Collective efficacy predicts what a team attempts, how much effort it sustains, and how well it withstands setbacks — a group-level echo of what self-efficacy predicts for an individual.

### Media Studies

- Role of Observational Learning:Audiences learn by watching characters on TV shows, movies, or social media. Whether modeling beneficial behaviors like resolving conflicts productively or counterproductive ones such as aggression, media can reinforce or reshape viewers’ attitudes and actions.

- Responsible Content Creation:Knowing that people often imitate what they see, content creators can harness social cognitive principles to produce messages that empower and educate, spreading positive behaviors at scale.

Bandura (2003) formalized this into a dual-path model of media influence. In the direct path, media inform, model, and motivate audiences straight toward a behavior by showing the skill, the confidence to enact it, and the rewards that follow.

In the socially mediated path, media instead connect viewers to social networks that supply the ongoing support needed to sustain change.

The same mechanism has been tested for harm: a meta-analysis of over 17,000 participants by Prescott, Sargent, and Hull (2018) found violent video-game exposure prospectively predicted later physical aggression, even controlling for baseline aggression, though the effect was small.

### Designing Interventions, Training, and Learning Strategies

- Multilevel Focus:Because Social Cognitive Theory involves the continuous interplay between personal beliefs (e.g., self-efficacy), observable actions (e.g., practicing a skill), and external conditions (e.g., accessibility of resources), its principles can guide a holistic approach when tackling real-world challenges.

- Customized Approaches:Interventions based on Social Cognitive Theory can be adapted to diverse populations. By first assessing people’s current self-efficacy levels, perceived barriers, and social supports, change-makers can introduce targeted feedback, skill-building opportunities, and environmental supports to sustain behavior change.

Self-efficacy is one of the few change mechanisms with strong causal evidence behind it.

Sheeran et al. (2016) meta-analyzed 204 experiments that randomly assigned participants to interventions raising self-efficacy, finding that induced gains produced medium-sized increases in intention and smaller, still reliable increases in behavior.

Effective designs typically combine mastery experiences structured so early attempts are likely to succeed, credible models similar to the target audience, and proximal, specific, challenging goals.

These give people a near-term standard to judge progress against, rather than one distant target offering no feedback until the end.

### Strengths

SCT offers a holistic view by introducing triadic reciprocal determinism, asserting that personal factors (e.g., beliefs, self-efficacy), environmental influences, and behavior are all interlinked.

SCT does not treat the environment as the only cause of behaviour. A person’s internal cognitions, such as self-efficacy and outcome expectancies, both shape and are shaped by their actions and their social environment.

This feedback loop is dynamic: each of the three components continuously influences the other two.

The theory therefore goes beyond a linear “stimulus–response” approach. It highlights human agency, since people can proactively change their environments to reach the outcomes they want.

Because SCT addresses these reciprocal influences, it is especially useful for designing interventions that target multiple levels at once. For instance, to promote regular exercise, one can:

- Strengthen self-efficacy through small successes (personal factor).

- Use social or peer modeling (environment) to demonstrate positive routines.

- Encourage consistent behavioral practice (the action itself).

This integrated approach means SCT-based programs often show long-lasting results because they consider the person’s thoughts, the social climate, and the behavior simultaneously.

However, it can be more complex and time-consuming to implement interventions, because multiple elements (environmental, behavioral, cognitive) must be addressed.

SCT placesself-efficacyat the core of motivation and behavior change, making it a crucial predictor of whether someone will initiate and persist in a challenging action.

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to carry out a particular behavior successfully.

Bandura showed that people high in self-efficacy generally set higher goals, are more resilient to setbacks, and are likelier to sustain efforts toward achievement.

SCT thereby clarifies the cognitive underpinnings of motivation and highlights preciselywhysome learners or patients seem to progress faster than others, even when their abilities are ostensibly the same.

Interventions that bolster self-efficacy (through mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion), often see improved outcomes, whether in mental health treatment, academic achievement, or lifestyle changes.

This makes SCT-based strategies appealing to teachers, clinicians, and coaches aiming to maintain participants’ engagement.

There is a flip side, though. Measuring self-efficacy can be tricky, since it is typically task-specific rather than a single global trait. Not all real-world contexts allow for easy, direct manipulation of someone’s confidence either.

Yet, when done carefully, building self-efficacy is a powerful tool for sustained behavior change.

SCT is backed by real-world evidence beyond the laboratory, from intergenerational violence to prosocial skills training.

Mihalic and Elliott (1997) found that people who experienced more childhood violence went on to show higher rates of marital violence as adults. SCT explains this as the modelling and vicarious reinforcement of aggression passing down across generations.

Perry, Perry, and Rasmussen (1986) found that children’s self-efficacy for aggression, and their expectation that it would pay off, predicted how aggressive they actually were.

The theory’s reach extends well beyond aggression.

Sheridan et al. (2011) used a modelling-based programme called Skillstreaming with 647 kindergarten children. It produced real gains in listening, following directions, problem-solving, and knowing when to ask for help.

Together, these findings show that SCT’s mechanisms can build prosocial skills, not just explain how harmful behaviour spreads.

### Weaknesses

Social cognitive theory can be criticized for being too broad. It addresses so many factors (personal, behavioral, and environmental) without always specifying how to prioritize or measure them.

While SCT’s comprehensiveness is a strength, it also makes the theory challenging to operationalize in tightly controlled research.

The theory says that beliefs, environment, and behavior all interact. It does not always specify which variable matters most in a given context, or how to isolate the strongest driver of change.

In some studies, researchers end up selectively measuring only certain aspects (like observational learning or self-efficacy), leaving others (like social norms, emotional states) less explored.

Researchers and practitioners may find it difficult to design interventions or measure outcomes consistently.

This complexity can result in somewhat vague or diluted programs, where it’s unclear which elements of the approach produced success (or caused failure).

Moreover, when theories encompasseverything, critics argue they can become less predictive or falsifiable, making it harder to refine or test them rigorously.

SCT can appear more like a guiding framework than a precise predictive model.

SCT places significant emphasis on conscious cognitive processes (e.g., self-efficacy, expected outcomes) and can underestimate subconscious, biological, or emotional influences on behavior.

Bandura does acknowledge affective and physiological factors, but the main focus is on how people deliberately process information about themselves and their environment.

Modern research, however, highlights the importance of automatic processes, genetics, and hormonal influences, factors often operating beneath conscious awareness.

For example, certain addictive behaviors or strong emotional responses might not fit neatly into self-efficacy and observational learning frameworks.

Habits formed through repeated automatic reinforcement might not be as easily changed by cognitively based interventions.

Because SCT interventions focus onrational and consciousprocesses, they may be less effective for deeply ingrained or biologically influenced conditions. Examples include some forms of addiction, anxiety disorders, or depression, when driven mainly byautomatic or unconsciousprocesses.

Practitioners may need to supplement SCT-based strategies with other approaches (e.g., exposure therapy, medication) that address emotional regulation, biological predispositions, or unconscious triggers.

In real-life, structural or societal factors can overshadow personal cognitive determinants, meaning SCT might inadvertently place too much responsibility on the individual without adequately addressing external constraints.

Though SCT’s concept of environment is broad, it tends to focus on how people perceive and respond to their immediate social surroundings.

Some critics argue that deeper structural issues, like poverty, discrimination, lack of resources, cannot be sufficiently tackled only by boosting self-efficacy or providing good role models.

For example, if a person lives in a food desert, all the self-efficacy in the world will not help them eat a balanced diet if healthy foods are inaccessible.

Thus, the emphasis on individual agency can downplay systemic barriers to making healthy or adaptive choices.

If an intervention is designed primarily around teaching individuals coping or observational learning strategies butignores real socioeconomic or institutional obstacles, its success may be limited.

Furthermore, critics raise a different worry: “victim blaming.” A person may be seen as failing simply for lacking motivation or self-efficacy, when in fact larger external forces are to blame.

When implementing SCT-based programs in low-resource settings, it’s crucial to consider how broader socio-political contexts intersect with personal and behavioral factors.

The Bobo doll experiments that the theory’s observational-learning claims rest on were conducted in a tightly controlled laboratory, described by later critics as an “arguably very unnatural environment.”

Children were exposed to a single, brief encounter with a model and an inflatable toy built to be hit. That set-up looks little like how aggression actually develops in everyday life.

Real aggression unfolds over repeated exposures, in homes, schools and peer groups, shaped by relationships the lab setting cannot recreate.

The lab setting raises a real question: does striking an inflatable doll designed to be hit tell us much about genuine social aggression?

Some critics argue the effect is partly an artefact of the setting itself, rather than pure observational learning. Others note that later, more naturalistic studies have found similar patterns, which softens the concern without erasing it.

### Contemporary Research

Self-efficacy’s link to behaviour has moved from correlational evidence toward a genuine causal test. Sheeran et al. (2016) meta-analysed 204 experiments that randomly assigned participants to interventions raising self-efficacy, attitudes, or perceived norms.

Boosting self-efficacy produced a medium-sized increase in intention (d+ = .51) and a smaller but still reliable increase in actual behaviour (d+ = .47). That is causal evidence, not just correlation.

Moral disengagement has grown from a single dramatic case study into a well-tested organisational predictor. Ogunfowora et al. (2022) meta-analysed workplace studies covering thousands of employees across many organisations.

Moral disengagement reliably predicted misconduct and turnover. It also reduced citizenship behaviour. The effect worked through reduced moral awareness, weaker moral judgement, and blunted anticipated guilt, exactly the self-regulatory route Bandura’s theory predicts.

Bandura’s claims about learning from symbolic, on-screen models have also been tested at scale. The evidence is now longitudinal.

Prescott, Sargent, and Hull (2018) meta-analysed 24 longitudinal studies of more than 17,000 participants, following them for between three months and four years. Exposure to violent video games predicted later physical aggression, even after controlling for participants’ baseline aggression.

The effect size was small (β ≈ .08–.11). Critics debate how much real-world harm it represents. Still, the result supports Bandura’s original prediction, using far stronger designs than the 1960s Bobo doll studies could offer.

### Social Learning vs. Social Cognitive Theory

Social learning theoryand Social Cognitive Theory are both theories of learning that place an emphasis on the role of observational learning.

However, there are several key differences between the two theories. Social learning theory focuses on the idea of reinforcement, while Social Cognitive Theory emphasizes the role of cognitive processes.

Additionally, social learning theory posits that all behavior is learned through observation, while Social Cognitive Theory allows for the possibility of learning through other means, such as direct experience.

Finally, social learning theory focuses on individualistic learning, while Social Cognitive Theory takes a more holistic view, acknowledging the importance of environmental factors.

These differences matter. Social learning theory and Social Cognitive Theory offer different frameworks for understanding how learning takes place, with different implications for how each theory is applied (Bandura, 1986, 1989).

### Key Takeaways

- Reciprocal Determinism:Personal factors, behaviour, and the environment continuously shape one another rather than following a one-way cause.

- Human Agency:People intentionally influence their own lives through intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflectiveness, not just react to events.

- Observational Learning:People learn by watching models, through four stages: attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation.

- Self-Efficacy:A person’s belief in their own capability is the theory’s central motivational construct, built from four sources.

- Moral Disengagement:People can selectively switch off their own moral standards for one act, without abandoning them altogether.

- Modern Evidence:Recent meta-analyses give causal support for self-efficacy and confirm moral disengagement predicts real workplace misconduct.

- Applications:The theory underpins interventions in education, health promotion, media, and organisational behaviour.
