Anomie is a state of social instability that happens when society’s norms and values break down or become unclear.

The term, coined by sociologistÉmile Durkheim, describes how people can feel disconnected, purposeless, or uncertain about what’s right or expected. It often emerges during times of rapid change or inequality, when the usual moral guidelines no longer hold society together

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:Anomie describes a state of normlessness when society’s rules, values, and expectations lose their power to guide behavior, leaving individuals feeling adrift or disconnected.

- Origins:The concept was introduced by sociologist Émile Durkheim in the late 19th century and later expanded by Robert Merton to explain deviant behavior in modern societies.

- Causes:Anomie arises during periods of rapid social change, inequality, or cultural conflict, when established norms no longer fit new realities.

- Effects:People experiencing anomie may feel purposeless, isolated, or frustrated, while societies may see higher levels of deviance, unrest, or moral confusion.

- Examples:Economic recessions, technological disruption, or breakdowns in community life can all produce anomic conditions where shared meaning and cohesion decline.

### Durkheim’s Concept of Anomie

Émile Durkheimused the term anomie (literally meaning “without law” or “normlessness”) to describe a social condition where shared norms and values lose their power to regulate people’s behaviour.

In simple terms, it’s when society’s rules break down — people no longer know what’s expected of them, and moral boundaries become unclear.

Durkheim saw anomie as a key problem in modern societies, especially during times of rapid change.

Durkheim traced this distress to a claim about desire itself. Unlike an animal’s appetite, which biological need naturally limits, human wants for wealth or status have no built-in ceiling.

Society normally sets that ceiling. It does so through custom and shared expectation, fixing what a person can reasonably hope for. Rapid change removes this ceiling before a new one forms, so desire runs ahead of what circumstances can supply.

### What Is Anomie?

For Durkheim, anomie means a breakdown ofsocial norms— a situation where people no longer feel guided by a shared sense of right and wrong.

It signals a weakening of moral bonds that normally hold communities together.

- Loss of Shared Values:Anomie happens when society’s “collective consciousness” — the shared norms, values, and beliefs that unite people — becomes weak. When this moral glue fades, individuals become less connected and more self-focused.

- Lack of Moral Guidance:In times of change, old rules may no longer make sense, but new ones haven’t yet formed. This can leave people feeling lost, anxious, or unsure how to behave.

- Weak Social Control:When shared values fade, society struggles to control behaviour. Durkheim believed that moral rules act like a “protective cocoon,” limiting our desires. Without them, people’s wants become unlimited, leading to dissatisfaction and disorder.

- Rise of Self-Interest:Without strong moral guidance, individuals tend to put their own interests first, caring less about how their actions affect others.

Durkheim believed anomie arises when societies go through rapid social or economic change, such as during industrialisation or urbanisation.

These shifts disrupt traditional ways of life and moral expectations.

- Mechanical solidarity:In traditional societies, people share similar work, values, and beliefs.

- Organic solidarity:In modern societies, people perform very different roles and depend on each other through complex divisions of labour.

### The Division of Labour and Its Abnormal Forms

Durkheim’s first sustained treatment of anomie appears in his 1893 workThe Division of Labour in Society. He argued that a growing division of labour is not a threat to social cohesion but its chief modern source.

As people depend on each other’s different skills, this interdependence can generate a new, organic solidarity. That balance can break down two ways.

Theanomic division of labourhappens when economic life expands and specialises faster than the rules of fair exchange and contract can keep pace. Interdependence then produces friction rather than cooperation.

Theforced division of labourhappens when people are sorted into jobs by inherited privilege rather than by their own ability. The resulting inequality feels imposed, not earned.

Either failure, on its own, can stop a complex society from achieving real solidarity.

### Anomie and Suicide

Durkheim famously explored anomie in his studySuicide(1897). He argued that suicide rates are influenced by social forces, not just personal unhappiness.

He identified anomic suicide, which occurs when people’s lives are disrupted by sudden change and they lose their sense of moral direction.

Anomic suicide tends to rise:

- During economic downturns— when people lose jobs, income, or status.

- During economic booms— when rapid success or social mobility disrupts familiar routines and expectations.

During economic downturns— when people lose jobs, income, or status.

During economic booms— when rapid success or social mobility disrupts familiar routines and expectations.

In both cases, people experience a breakdown in their social ties and limits on desire, leading to frustration and hopelessness.

As Durkheim put it,“The bond attaching people to life slackens because the bond attaching them to society is itself slack.”

Anomic suicide is only one of four types in Durkheim’s wider theory. He organised suicide’s causes along two dimensions. One issocial integration, the strength of a person’s bonds to a group. The other issocial regulation, how far shared norms check and direct their desires.

Too little integration producesegoistic suicide. Too much producesaltruistic suicide, where the group’s demands override self-preservation.

Too much regulation, the mirror image of anomic suicide, producesfatalistic suicide: oppressive control that leaves a person feeling their future is blocked entirely.

### Merton’s Strain Theory of Anomie

Merton’s Strain Theoryis one of the most important explanations of why people break rules or commit crime.

It comes from thefunctionalist perspectivein sociology, and it builds directly on the work of Émile Durkheim.

Merton argued that deviance and crime are not simply about bad people — they arise from the way society itself is organised.

### The Basic Idea

Merton suggested that most societies, especially modern capitalist ones, encourage everyone to pursue the same goals: wealth, success, and happiness. Not everyone has the same access to the legitimate ways of achieving them, though.

This mismatch createsstrain.

People still want success, because society teaches them to want it. When they can’t achieve it through approved routes like education and hard work, they may turn to other, less legitimate means.

Society tells people to “dream big” — but not everyone gets a fair chance to make those dreams come true.

### From Durkheim to Merton: Rethinking Anomie

Durkheim originally used the term anomie to mean a state of normlessness — when society’s rules and values break down, leaving people feeling lost or disconnected.

This often happens during times of rapid change, such as industrialisation or economic crisis.

For him, the problem wasn’t that norms had disappeared, but that people couldn’t live up to them.

Society still tells everyone what theyshouldwant, but it doesn’t give everyone equal access to the legitimate ways to get there.

This tension — between the goals we’re taught to value and the limited means available to achieve them — is what Merton called strain.

### Goals and Means: The Source of Strain

Merton described two key parts of this social system:

- Cultural Goals– the things society encourages us to strive for, such as wealth, success, power, and happiness.

- Institutionalised Means– the socially approved ways to achieve these goals, like education, employment, and hard work.

Cultural Goals– the things society encourages us to strive for, such as wealth, success, power, and happiness.

Institutionalised Means– the socially approved ways to achieve these goals, like education, employment, and hard work.

When people can’t reach these goals through legitimate means, strain builds up.

They still feel the pressure to succeed but may resort to deviant or criminal behaviour to get there.

For example, someone might deeply value financial success (goal) but, after being denied opportunities for education or fair employment (means), may turn to theft or fraud instead.

### The Five Ways People Adapt

Merton identified five modes of adaptation to explain how people can respond to this tension.

Merton believed these adaptations explained why some peopleconformwhile others deviate — not because of individual weakness, but because of the pressures built into society.

### Strain, Social Class, and Crime

Merton used his theory to explain whyworking-classand poorer people are often overrepresented in crime statistics.

- The working class are taught the same goals as everyone else — to be successful and make money — but they have fewer opportunities to do so legitimately.

- When they fail to reach success through accepted routes,illegitimate means(like theft or drug dealing) can seem more realistic or rewarding.

- Over time, these alternative routes to success may becomenormalised within subcultures, so that deviant behaviour feels less wrong.

### Crime, Deviance, and the Functions of Anomie

Durkheim’s account of anomie has a second, less familiar half. He also argued that crime and deviance are a normal, even useful, feature of every society.

### Crime as a Normal, Functional Feature of Society

Rule-breaking persists in every kind of society Durkheim studied. He reasoned that it must serve some positive social function, or it would not persist at all.

Durkheim even suggested that a hypothetical “society of saints” would still end up treating minor faults as serious deviance. Some threshold of moral boundary-marking, he argued, is a structural necessity for any society.

Deviance can provoke social change. It draws attention to an unjust or outdated law and can help bring about reform.

A serious offence can also produce social integration, when shared outrage draws a community together in condemnation of the act. This effect is especially visible when the victims are seen as particularly vulnerable.

Trial and public punishment matter too. They reassure a community that its moral order is real and actively defended.

Kingsley Davis (1937) extended this logic to a striking case: prostitution, though widely stigmatised, could act as a“safety valve”. It channelled certain demands in a way that paradoxically helped stabilise the family system it seemed to threaten.

### The Limits of the Functionalist Reading

Durkheim was clear that this reading has limits. Too much crime signals that too many people have stopped following shared norms, putting social order itself at risk.

Too little crime can be just as telling. It can signal an overbearing system of control, since Durkheim believed a society needs some criticism and rule-breaking in order to adapt.

Sutherland’s (1940) work onwhite-collar crimeraises a related challenge.

Many damaging or unethical practices among the wealthy and powerful are less likely to be defined, detected, or prosecuted as crime at all.

Power plays a part here. People with social and economic power can shape which acts the law captures in the first place — a pattern known as thesocial construction of crime.

Marxist critics press a related point. They argue that Durkheim’s picture of shared moral consensus understates the class conflict built intocapitalist societies.

Anomie itself sits at the destabilising end of this balance. It names the moment when the normal level of deviance a society can absorb has been exceeded, not merely tested by isolated rule-breakers.

### Durkheim’s View: Rapid Change and Weakened Solidarity

Durkheim saw anomie as a result of large-scale social disruption.

He believed it was most likely to occur during periods of rapid social change, when societies transform faster than their moral order can keep up.

Durkheim argued that anomie arose as societies shifted from mechanical solidarity – typical of small, traditional communities – to organic solidarity, characteristic of modern industrial life.

### Merton’s View: Strain Between Goals and Means

Robert Merton expanded Durkheim’s idea to explain why individuals break rules in modern capitalist societies.

His strain theory reframed anomie as a tension between cultural goals and the legitimate means to achieve them.

### 1. The Gap Between Aspirations and Opportunities

In Merton’s model, anomie occurs when people are taught to desire success but lack fair opportunities to achieve it through accepted channels.

- Cultural Goals:Society encourages everyone to pursue wealth, success, and happiness—the “American Dream.”

- Blocked Opportunities:Yet not everyone has equal access to quality education, stable jobs, or fair wages.

- Resulting Strain:This mismatch creates frustration and alienation, leading some individuals to seek alternative, often deviant, paths to reach their goals.

Modern consumer culture intensifies this strain by glorifying wealth and possessions.

### Critical Evaluation

Both Émile Durkheim and Robert Merton made major contributions to understanding how social breakdown and inequality can lead to crime.

However, their theories of anomie and strain have also faced strong criticism for being vague, overly structural, and limited in scope.

### Criticisms of Durkheim’s Concept of Anomie

Durkheim’s original idea of anomie and its link to deviance has been challenged on several grounds.

Durkheim never clearly definedhowanomie could be observed or measured.

It’s unclear exactlywhyweakened norms would lead specifically to crime, rather than to other forms of social change such as protest or withdrawal. This makes the theory hard to test scientifically.

Critics argue that Durkheim focused too heavily on society’s structures and not enough onindividual meaning and motivation.

He didn’t explain why some people commit crimes while others—facing the same social conditions—do not. This makes his theory feel overly general.

Durkheim suggested that crime can sometimes serve a positive function by clarifying moral boundaries or strengthening social solidarity.

However, critics point out that some crimes—likerape, child abuse, or domestic violence—have no such function.

These acts are purely destructive and cannot be justified as “useful” to society.

### Criticisms of Merton’s Strain Theory

Robert Merton’s version of anomie, known asstrain theory, focused on the tension between cultural goals (like success) and the unequal opportunities people have to reach them.

While influential, his theory also has several weaknesses.

Merton’s theory explainseconomic crimes—like theft, fraud, or robbery—quite well, but it struggles to explainnon-economicforms of deviance.

- It doesn’t fully account for crimes motivated byanger, emotion, or power, such as violent assaults, hate crimes, or vandalism.

- Nor does it explainrecreational deviance, like joyriding or football hooliganism, which are not directly linked to blocked economic opportunities.

### Contemporary Research

Recent work has moved away from testing Durkheim’s original four-fold typology directly. Two narrower questions matter more now.

Does large-scale disruption reliably raise anomic distress? Does chronic weak integration still carry the risk Durkheim proposed?

Aim:to establish whether suicide rates rose during the acute early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, when disruption was at its most severe.

Method:Pirkis and an international team (2021) gathered official suicide-count data from 21 countries and areas, covering January 2019 to July 2020. They used interrupted time-series analysis to compare the suicides actually observed against the number pre-pandemic trends would have predicted.

Results:there was no overall rise in suicide. Across the 21 locations, about a dozen, including parts of Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan, and the United States, showed a significant decrease instead.

Conclusion:suicide rates held steady or fell in the pandemic’s early months, rather than rising as the disruption itself might predict. Still, the authors called for continued surveillance.

One likely reading: the pandemic’s disruption came bundled with unusually visible collective purpose, such as lockdowns and solidarity campaigns. That may have briefly increased people’s sense of integration even as it disrupted daily life, echoing Durkheim’s own point that suicide falls during wartime.

This finding should be read alongside a separate, more consistent pattern. Chronic social isolation remains a robust suicide risk factor.

Motillon-Toudic and colleagues (2022) screened 2,684 articles and synthesised the 46 that met their criteria. They concluded that social isolation shows a substantial, consistent link to suicide risk, with family relationships especially protective for both elderly and adolescent groups.

Together, the two studies separate two mechanisms a loose use of “anomie” can blur. A chronic deficit of integration remains a robust risk factor. A sudden disruption of regulation does not automatically raise suicide if it comes with a compensating surge in solidarity.
