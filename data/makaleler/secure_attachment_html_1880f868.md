Relationships»Adult Attachment

Secure attachment is a bond in which people feel safe, supported, and free to express their emotions. It lets them seek comfort from a partner and confidently explore the world, trusting they have a reliable base to return to.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition: Secure attachment is characterized by feelings of trust, safety, and comfort in relationships, a willingness to rely on others, and a belief that one is loved and valued.

- Benefits: Secure attachment is associated with numerous positive outcomes, including improved emotional well-being and the ability to form strong, healthy bonds with others throughout life.

- Signs in children:Securely attached children tend to be confident explorers who easily seek comfort from caregivers when distressed. They typically develop positive self-esteem and form healthy relationships.

- Parenting strategies:To foster secure attachment, be responsive to your child’s needs, provide consistent care, and remain emotionally available. These approaches help children feel safe, loved, and supported.

- Signs in adults: Those with secure attachment usually maintain healthy relationships, trust others, and comfortably express emotions. They also exhibit a positive self-image and resilience to stress.

### Why Secure Attachment Matters

Secure attachment is important because it establishes the expectation that emotional support is available when needed.

This frees up mental energy for everything else: learning, exploring, building relationships, and bouncing back from setbacks.

Think of secure attachment as the foundation of a house.

You can’t see it from the outside, but everything else depends on it.

When children feel safe and understood by their caregivers, they develop an internal sense of security. This stays with them, shaping their confidence, relationships, and emotional health well into adulthood.

### Benefits and long-term impacts

- Sense of Safety (Felt Security):People with secure attachment feel that the world is generally safe and that others can be trusted. This helps them stay calmer and more grounded in daily life.

- Independence:Secure attachment supports independence. It gives children (and adults) the confidence to try new things because they know support is available if needed.

- Healthy Self-Esteem:Securely attached people tend to feel worthy, capable, and good enough, laying a foundation for stableself-esteem. Early interactions help them develop a coherent sense of who they are.

- Relationship Quality:Secure individuals are comfortable with closeness, intimacy, and are less worried about abandonment. It leads to the most stable and predictable long-term relationships.

- Healthy Coping Strategies:Secure individuals are more likely to use effective coping strategies: problem solving, planning, reaching out for support, instead of avoidance or catastrophising.

- Buffer Against Trauma:Security acts as a buffer against the lasting effects of trauma. Secure individuals are somewhat less likely to develop anxiety, depression, or PTSD after frightening experiences. Research finds this protective effect real but modest, not a guarantee against distress (Sroufe, 2005).

- Better Parenting in the Next Generation: Secure adults tend to be warm, responsive, and structured parents, passing on the benefits of security to their children.

A securely attached child is like someone with a reliable phone signal.

They can venture out and explore because they know they can always “call home” if something goes wrong.

An insecurely attached child is like someone with spotty coverage, they’re always checking for signal instead of focusing on what’s in front of them.

### How Psychologists Identify Secure Attachment: The Strange Situation

Psychologists do not diagnose secure attachment by asking a child how they feel. They observe it directly, using a structured laboratory procedure called the Strange Situation. In this procedure, a child is briefly separated from their caregiver and then reunited, while researchers watch how the child responds.

### Ainsworth and Bell’s (1970) Founding Study

Aim:Ainsworth and Bell wanted to see how a child’s drive to explore and its drive to stay close to a caregiver interact under rising stress. They also wanted to describe how individual infants differ in their use of the mother during separations and reunions. Two systems were in tension.

Method:One-year-olds were observed through a one-way mirror across a set sequence of brief separations from, and reunions with, their mother in an unfamiliar room.

Trained observers rated how strongly each infant sought proximity and contact, avoided or resisted the caregiver, explored, and cried, episode by episode. The setting was tightly controlled.

Results:Exploration dropped sharply when the mother left and recovered when she returned. Most infants showed a secure pattern: confident exploration when she was present, visible distress at her absence. Crucially, these infants sought her out on reunion, were readily comforted, and returned to play. The reunion told the story.

Conclusion:What marks a secure infant is not the absence of distress but the reunion: active proximity-seeking that is quickly satisfied, followed by a return to exploration. This reunion behavior became the basis for classifying infants as securely attached, or Type B.

### How Common Is Secure Attachment?

Secure attachment is the most common pattern wherever it has been studied.

Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg’s (1988) meta-analysis pooled 32 Strange Situation studies across eight countries, covering more than 2,000 infants.

Roughly two-thirds of infants were classified as securely attached in every country examined, making security the modal pattern worldwide. In Ainsworth’s original North American samples the rate ran a little higher, around 62-70%.

Insecure patterns varied more by culture. Avoidant classifications were somewhat more common in Western European samples, sometimes read as a cultural push toward early independence.

Resistant classifications were more common in Israeli and Japanese samples, linked to infrequent separation from the mother and limited everyday contact with strangers.

Even so, the differences between individual families within a single country were about one and a half times larger than the differences between countries. Culture shapes the details of insecurity more than it explains why security itself is so common.

### Signs in children

For a child to develop a secure attachment, they need to be raised in an environment where they feel protected and seen by their caregivers.

If a caregiver is not responsive to a child’s needs, the child may not be able to form a secure and stable bond.

Children with a secure attachmentsee the worldas a reliable and friendly place. This outlook is protective. They learn to trust that people around them are kind and dependable.

Securely attached childrenuse the caregiver as a secure base, a trusted starting point they explore from and check back to. The caregiver also serves as a safe haven, the figure they return to for comfort before exploring again. Both roles matter equally.

Children with a secure attachment have been regulated by their caregiver in times of stress. This teaches them to self-regulate their own social, emotional, and cognitive behaviors.

### Early signs include:

- Positive response to the return of parents

- Comfortably interacts with others

- Comfortably explores and plays in new areas

- Prefers parents over strangers

- Seeks comfort from parents

Securely attached children show balanced behavioral strategies, expressing their need for both intimacy and autonomy.

Autonomy is particularly significant as it facilitates interaction with the environment.

Theattachment styleyou develop in early childhood is thought to have a lifelong influence on you. It can shape how you communicate your emotions and needs, how you respond to conflict, and how you form expectations about your relationships.

### Benefits of secure attachment in children

- Emotional Regulation: Children with secure attachments develop a better ability to manage and express their emotions, leading to more stable moods.

- Social Skills: Securely attached children show more empathy, social competence, and conflict resolution skills, helping them form and maintain relationships.

- Self-Esteem: Secure attachment fosters a positive self-image, giving children more confidence in their abilities and decisions.

- Independence: Children with secure attachments balance seeking support with self-reliance, giving them confidence to face new situations independently.

### How to raise a securely attached child

If a child is brought up in a nurturing and supportive environment where caregivers are responsive to the child’s emotional needs, a secure bond is formed.

However, if a child perceives that their emotional needs are not met, the child is not able to build a secure and stable bond with their caregivers.

### 1. Be a secure base physically and emotionally

Attachment figures can be seen as a ‘secure base’ that infants use to explore their social world.

The more assured the infant is in the emotional availability of their attachment figure, the more likely they will interact with others. This is truest in times of stress.

Caregivers who provide a secure base allow infants to become autonomous, inquisitive, and experimental.

When around their caregiver, the child should feel assured that no harm will come to them. They should know they will be fed, kept warm, and protected.

The caregiver is the child’s barrier against harm, so letting them know they are protected and loved is important in making them feel safe.

The child should be allowed the chance to develop freedom. At the same time, they should get reassurance that their caregiver is nearby if they need to check in.

Children should be supported in exploring their world in a way that makes them feel secure. Caregivers should aim to reassure the child that they believe in their abilities but stay close if something goes wrong.

Avoid being overbearing or constantly telling them what they should be doing. Give them room. Instead, give gentle guidance if they get stuck and allow them to grow while watching from a safe distance.

In this way, the child should develop a sense of freedom to explore their world and increase their confidence in their own skills.

Limiting a child from exploration, being overprotective, or keeping them boxed in may lead to the development of an anxious attachment pattern. The balance matters. Children need to learn to explore independently and feel safe doing so.

The independence and individuality that come from childhood exploration contribute to a secure attachment style into adulthood.

### 2. Ensure they feel seen and understood

A child’s signal for attention, such as crying, is their way of telling the caregiver they need something.

The child should know that if they seek comfort, they will receive it from their caregivers.

Secure attachment develops when infants repeatedly experience a caregiver who responds accurately and consistently to their signs of distress. Consistency teaches trust.

Through these interactions, infants learn that their caregiver is available and will comfort them when needed. This sense of security allows them to feel safe and encourages more exploration of their environment.

If the caregiver responds correctly, most of the time, the child should understand that their world is reliable and they can have some control over it. Control feels safe.

If the caregivers are there to help soothe the child’s distress, they learn to see this as normal. When they grow up, they can use their caregiver’s actions as the template for managing their distress.

Interactional synchrony focuses on coordinating nonverbal behaviors during social interactions, while emotional attunement is about the empathetic understanding and response to another person’s emotions.

Interactional synchronyis a form of rhythmic interaction between infant and caregiver involving mutual focus, reciprocity, and mirroring of emotion or behavior.

Infants coordinate their actions with caregivers in a kind of conversation.

From birth, babies move in a rhythm when interacting with an adult, almost as if they were taking turns.

The infant and caregiver are able to anticipate how each other will behave and can elicit a particular response from the other.

Interactional synchrony is most likely to develop under certain conditions. The caregiver must attend fully to the baby’s state.

They should provide playful stimulation when the infant is alert and attentive. When an overexcited or tired infant is fussy, the caregiver should avoid pushing things further.

In effect, the caregiver reads the baby’s signal as saying, “Cool it. I just need a break from all this excitement.”

Interactional synchrony can facilitate emotional attunement, as coordinated nonverbal behaviors may help individuals better understand and connect with each other’s emotional states.

A caregiver can be very loving yet still mistime or misread a child’s cues, while a more reserved caregiver can respond accurately and reliably. Warmth and sensitivity are not the same thing.

Attunement is a subtle process in which the parent is “tuned in” to the child’s emotional needs.

A caregiver needs to be good at noticing the tiny, quick changes in a baby’s emotions.

They then show the baby, through facial expressions, voice tone, and body language, that they understand those emotions. This lets the baby know the caregiver shares the experience with them.

When things go smoothly, attunement helps a child feel truly understood, accepted, and supported by their mom or caregiver.

But no one can be perfect all the time. Misunderstandings and small ruptures, sometimes called “mis-attunement,” are normal.

What matters most is repair. A caregiver who notices a rupture and reconnects teaches a child that relationships can survive mistakes.

This is the real basis for the popular idea ofgood-enough parenting: caregivers do not need to be perfect. The evidence is about reliable repair, not a fixed one-third accuracy threshold.

According to Daniel Stern (2018, p. 139.), professor of Psychiatry at Cornell University Medical Center, sensitive (‘attuned’) responses from a caregiver follow three steps:

- “First, the parent must be able to read the infant’s feeling state from the infant’s overt behavior.

- Second, the parent must perform some behavior that is not a strict imitation but nonetheless corresponds in some way to the infant’s overt behavior.

- Third, the infant must be able to read this corresponding parental response as having to do with the infant’s original feeling experience and not just imitating the infant’s behaviour”.

Many parent-child relationships have strong love but lack emotional attunement. In these non-attuned relationships, children may feel loved but not genuinely understood or appreciated for their true selves.

They often repress emotions or aspects of themselves that they perceive as unacceptable to their parents.

Research by Lynne Murray (1985) has demonstrated that even warm responses to infants are not regulating unless they are exactly timed with their cues.

In 1975, Edward Tronick and his team introduced the “Still Face Experiment” at a child development research conference.

They showed what happens when a baby interacts with an unresponsive caregiver who has no facial expression for three minutes. The baby quickly becomes sad and cautious.

When the mother “fails to respond appropriately,” the infant rapidly sobers and grows wary. He makes repeated attempts to get the interaction into its usual reciprocal pattern.

When these attempts fail, the infant withdraws [and] orients his face and body away from his mother with a withdrawn, hopeless facial expression (p.452).

A child’s perception of being emotionally understood and connected with others is crucial.

However, certain stressful circumstances can cause caregivers to be emotionally distant, even if physically present. This condition of being physically close but emotionally separated is called “proximate separation.” It still hurts.

Proximate separation can be as distressing to a child as physical separation. Examples include a parent breaking intense eye contact with a child or overstimulating a resting child.

These interactions register on an unconscious physiological level, even if the child isn’t consciously aware of the emotional disconnection. Such experiences shape a child’s future personality and emotional development.

Research by Allan Schore (2001) suggests these emotional disconnections cause physiological stress comparable to physical separation.

Schore’s model proposes a specific mechanism. Repeated experience of a sensitive, regulating caregiver helps shape the developing right-hemisphere brain circuits that later regulate stress and emotion.

On this account, attuned care does not just feel good in the moment. It helps wire the infant’s emerging capacity to manage their own feelings.

These early experiences of proximate separation can also influence adult relationships. People may seek out partners who repeat these non-attuned dynamics, continuing a cycle of emotional misattunement into adulthood.

### Signs in adults

John Bowlbyargued that one’s sense of security as a child is critical to their attachment style as an adult.

Adult relationships are likely to reflect a person’s early attachment style. The experience they had with a caregiver in childhood shapes what they come to expect from later relationships.

In Hazan and Shaver’s (1987) original study of adult romantic attachment, about 56% of respondents described themselves as securely attached. This closely matches the infant rate.

### How to know if someone has a secure attachment style

- Comfort with closeness and intimacy: Secure adults are comfortable being close to others and letting others be close to them, at ease with both intimacy and independence.

- Trust:They’re comfortable opening up about their feelings and asking for help when they need it, without worrying too much about being let down.

- Emotional expression:People who feel secure in relationships are open about how they feel. They don’t hide their emotions or bottle things up.

- Resilience: Secure adults may experience negative attachment-related events, yet they can objectively assess people and events and assign a positive value to relationships in general.

- Healthy interdependence: Securely attached individuals balance relying on their partner with meeting their own needs, creating deeper intimacy through vulnerability.

### Benefits of secure attachment in adults

- Healthy relationships: People with secure attachments find it easier to trust others andmaintain healthy boundaries. Their empathy and conflict resolution skills lead to more stable relationships.

- Emotional well-being: Secure attachment fosters resilience and better stress management, helping people bounce back from setbacks and regulate their emotions.

- Parenting: Adults with secure attachments are more likely to create secure attachments with their own children, perpetuating positive relationship patterns across generations.

### Is it too late to form a secure attachment?

There appears to be a continuity between early attachment styles and the quality of later adult romantic relationships.

This idea is based on theinternal working model, where an infant’s primary attachment forms a model (template) for future relationships.

Attachment security is the stability and trust one feels in interpersonal relationships. Itcan be developed within a consistent and dependable relationship, though building this kind of security is generally believed to take significant time.

During adulthood, new attachment bonds are formed, which may become a significant source of support during periods of distress, goal achievement, and exploration.

Romantic partners function as attachment figures and can become a source of comfort and felt security for the other member of the relationship.

As covered above, this same early-attachment template extends to friendships and romantic partnerships, not just parent-child bonds.

However, other researchers propose that people do not hold a single internal working model that applies to every relationship. Instead, each type of relationship has its own working model.

This means a person could be insecurely attached to their parents but securely attached to a romantic partner or friend.

A high-level working model holds general beliefs across all relationships. Lower-level models hold more specific rules, such as those for romantic or parental relationships. These, in turn, are shaped by experiences within a single relationship (Collins & Read, 1994; Main, Kaplan, & Cassidy, 1985).

### How can I identify my attachment style?

Toidentify your attachment style, consider the following key characteristics:

### Secure attachment

- I feel comfortable expressing my emotions and thoughts to others

- I am comfortable depending on others for help when necessary

- I can maintain a healthy balance between intimacy and independence in relationships

- I feel comfortable being vulnerable with my partner

- I respect my partner’s needs and boundaries and am comfortable setting my own

### Anxious attachment

- I worry whether people like me or not

- I rely heavily on my partner’s approval and validation to feel good about myself

- I often use physical closeness (like hugging) to reduce my anxiety in relationships

- I often feel that I’m not good enough to be in my relationship and question why my partner would want to be with me

### Avoidant attachment

- I feel uncomfortable when others try to get emotionally close to me

- I prioritize self-reliance over seeking emotional support from others

- I find it difficult to trust others completely

- I value my independence more than close relationships

- I tend to downplay or suppress my emotions

- I have conflicting desires for closeness and distance

- My behavior in relationships may be unpredictable

- I may have trouble regulating emotions

### Critical Evaluation of Secure Attachment Theory

Secure attachment theory is well supported, but researchers have raised several important questions about what the evidence actually shows.

### Contemporary Research

If sensitive caregiving, a caregiver who reads a child’s signals accurately and responds to them promptly, only partly explains security, what else builds it? A 2025 meta-analytic review tested whether how parents talk about emotion, beyond moment-to-moment sensitivity, also predicts attachment. The findings were mixed.

Aim:Rodrigues and colleagues (2025) tested whether supportive emotion socialization, non-supportive emotion socialization, and parental elaboration (talking about and labeling a child’s feelings) predict attachment security.

Method:Three meta-analyses pooled studies of children under 18, relating each parenting variable to attachment security measured behaviorally, representationally, or by questionnaire (roughly 9–11 samples and 576–1,763 participants per analysis).

Results:Supportive emotion socialization was only weakly related to security (r ≈ .06). Non-supportive socialization showed no reliable link (r ≈ −.05). Parental elaboration, however, showed a medium association with security (r ≈ .24), roughly as strong as classic sensitivity.

Conclusion:Naming and reflecting on a child’s emotions together is a meaningful, independent route to security, not just a proxy for sensitivity.

A related large-scale synthesis confirmed that a caregiver’s own secure state of mind predicts their infant’s security (Verhage et al., 2016).

But observed sensitivity explained only part of that link. Researchers call this the “transmission gap”: security passes from caregiver to child through more than the caregiving behaviors psychologists can currently measure.

### The Temperament Confound

Jerome Kagan argued that Strange Situation scores partly reflect an infant’s own inborn temperament rather than caregiving alone (Kagan et al., 1984).A highly reactive baby, on this view, may simply be harder to soothe, and so appear less secure, regardless of how sensitive its caregiver actually is.

Jerome Kagan argued that Strange Situation scores partly reflect an infant’s own inborn temperament rather than caregiving alone (Kagan et al., 1984).

A highly reactive baby, on this view, may simply be harder to soothe, and so appear less secure, regardless of how sensitive its caregiver actually is.

Later meta-analytic evidence has narrowed this critique substantially. Temperament turns out to be largely unrelated to security overall. Where it does predict anything, it is mainly the resistant pattern specifically, not insecurity in general (Groh et al., 2017).

This matters for how the confound should be read. If security were purely a fixed trait an infant carries into every relationship, it should generalize across caregivers.

It does not: the same child can be securely attached to one parent and insecurely attached to the other, which points to caregiving history, not temperament, as the larger influence.

Security is best understood as a property of a specific relationship. Temperament plays a role, but a limited one. It is not a fixed trait produced solely by the caregiver’s behavior.

### Cultural Bias in the Strange Situation

The Strange Situation assumes a single, sensitive caregiver and rewards independent exploration, assumptions that reflect a Western, individualist view of childhood.

Secure attachment is the majority pattern everywhere it has been tested (van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg, 1988), which supports the theory’s claim to universality.But the specific behavioral markers of an “optimal” secure infant may travel less well to societies where children are raised by multiple caregivers rather than one.

Secure attachment is the majority pattern everywhere it has been tested (van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg, 1988), which supports the theory’s claim to universality.

But the specific behavioral markers of an “optimal” secure infant may travel less well to societies where children are raised by multiple caregivers rather than one.

The procedure can also be thrown off by things that have nothing to do with attachment quality. In one Japanese sample, an unusually high rate of resistant classifications turned out to reflect separations that ran longer than was typical for those infants. This was not a genuinely different attachment pattern.

That does not make the construct culturally arbitrary. Security remains the most common pattern in every country studied, which is itself evidence for a species-typical process rather than a purely Western artifact.

What travels less well are the specific behavioral markers used to code an “optimal” secure infant, calibrated on a single-caregiver assumption that fits some societies better than others.

### Correlational Evidence and Continuity

Much of the evidence linking early security to later wellbeing is correlational. Children who experience sensitive care in infancy often keep experiencing supportive relationships afterward. This makes it hard to separate the effect of early attachment from everything that follows (Sroufe, 2005).

### A Socially Sensitive Emphasis

Attachment theory ties security closely to maternal sensitivity. This has historically invited mother-blaming and can understate other influences, such as poverty, parental mental health, and the role of fathers and other caregivers.

Later work broadened the picture, showing that infants form real attachments to fathers and other consistent caregivers too.

The size of the sensitivity effect matters here as well. Sensitivity is only a moderate predictor of security, not close to the whole story (De Wolff & van IJzendoorn, 1997). Treating any one family’s outcome as a simple reflection of parental effort goes beyond what the evidence actually supports.

The constructive reading is that sensitivity is modifiable. It is the basis for supportive, non-blaming parenting programs, not a verdict on individual parents. It leaves room for structural factors like poverty and mental health support to matter too.
