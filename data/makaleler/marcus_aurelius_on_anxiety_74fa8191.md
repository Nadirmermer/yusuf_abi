### Key points

*   Anxiety points towards the future: what might happen or go wrong.
*   We often try to outrun anxiety by keeping busy instead of confronting it.
*   Focus on what is within your control—and let go of what is not.

Whereas [depression](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/depression "Psychology Today looks at depression") weighs upon life as it is, [anxiety](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/anxiety "Psychology Today looks at anxiety") points towards the future: what might happen, what might go wrong, what might be expected of us.

Of course, some concern for the future is necessary. It motivates us to do what’s needed, pay our bills, and avoid danger. So the aim cannot be to abolish anxiety altogether—simply to prevent occasional alarm from becoming a permanent state.

**Alt Maddeler:**
- Anxiety points towards the future: what might happen or go wrong.
- We often try to outrun anxiety by keeping busy instead of confronting it.
- Focus on what is within your control—and let go of what is not.


### Modern Life

The modern world makes this difficult because threats are seldom all-or-nothing. Our ancestors faced a predator, or did not. We have emails, deadlines, mortgages. And when we look on [social media](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/social-media "Psychology Today looks at social media"), we feel we are falling behind.

Because the pressure is diffuse and continuous, we internalize it. Eventually, nobody needs to chase us; we carry the pursuer in our head.

Once the pursuer is inside us, we try to outrun them by keeping busy. Activity gives us a sense of control, but often we are not addressing the anxiety so much as preventing ourselves from feeling it. We run faster and faster so that we never have to ask where we are going.

A Zen story tells of a horseman galloping down a path at speed. It seemed as if the rider was on some urgent and vital mission.

“Where are you heading?” hollered a bystander.

“I don’t know!” came the already distant reply. “Ask the horse!”

### How to Reduce Anxiety

The first step, then, is to stop running and name the anxiety. A vague anxiety can fill the whole sky; a named [fear](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/fear "Psychology Today looks at fear") becomes one object within it. Once identified, it can be worked with, accepted, or dismissed.

In particular, we can determine what part of the anxiety is actually within our control. The ancient Stoics emphasized this distinction between what is and is not in our control. What is not in our control must simply be let go.

The Stoics had this image of an archer. The archer does all he can to shoot accurately. But once the arrow leaves the bow, its fate is no longer within the archer’s control. So the right-thinking archer focuses on the action, not on its result—because the first is in his control, whereas the other is not.

When dealing with our anxiety, we should all become like the Stoic emperor Marcus Aurelius, who wrote, “Today I escaped anxiety. Or no, I discarded it, because it was within me, in my own perceptions—not outside.”
