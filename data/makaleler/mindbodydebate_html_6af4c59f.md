The mind and body problem concerns the extent to which the mind and the body are separate or the same thing.

The mind is about mental processes, thoughts, and consciousness. The body is about the physical aspects of thebrain-neuronsand how the brain is structured.

Is the mind part of the body, or the body part of the mind? If they are distinct, then how do they interact? And which of the two is in charge?

Many theories try to explain the relationship between what we call the mind and the brain. The mind is the conscious, thinking “you” that experiences your thoughts; the brain is part of your body.

### Key Takeaways

- Monism:The view that only one fundamental kind of thing exists, so mind and body are, at bottom, the same substance.

- Dualism:The view that mind and body are two fundamentally different kinds of thing, most famously argued by Descartes.

- Materialism:The dominant form of monism in psychology; it holds that mental states are ultimately physical brain states.

- Interactionism:Dualism’s most common form: a non-physical mind and a physical body that causally influence one another.

- The Evidence:Brain-imaging studies support materialism, but findings like hypnotic pain control are harder for a purely physical view to explain.

- Modern Views:Cognitive neuroscience, embodied cognition, and panpsychism all offer newer, more nuanced takes on the debate.

### What is Monism?

This view asserts that there is only one fundamental substance (physical or mental).

There are two basic types of monism:

### 1. Materialism(or Physicalism)

Materialism asserts that everything in the universe is ultimately physical or material, including the mind.

Mental states, consciousness, and everything we associate with the mind are seen as arising from or reducible to physical processes in the brain.

Materialism is the belief that nothing exists apart from the material world (i.e., physical matter like the brain); materialist psychologists generally agree that consciousness (the mind) is the function of the brain.

Thomas Hobbes, the 17th-century philosopher, held that everything that exists is body or matter.

All reality (including the mind) is physical. Mental states are brain states or are caused by physical processes​.

Materialism denies any immaterial mind or soul separate from the body.

Mental processes can be identified with purely physical processes in thecentral nervous system. Humans, on this view, are just complicated physiological organisms.

For example,reductive materialismposits mental phenomena can be reduced to or identified with physical processes (e.g. the experience of pain just is certain neural firings​).

Eliminative materialismclaims that our common concepts of mind (beliefs, desires, etc.) are flawed and will eventually be eliminated in favor of neuroscientific explanations.

In short, materialists argue the mindjust iswhat the brain does.

### 2. Idealism

This is a less common form of monism.

This view holds that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual.

The physical world, in this view, is either a construct of the mind or somehow less real than the mental realm.

Reality, as we perceive it, is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent. This means that what we consider the “external world” is, in some way, a product of our minds or consciousness.

Idealism has profound implications for our understanding of:

- Perception:How our minds construct our experience of reality.

- Reality:The very nature of what is “real.”

- Consciousness:The central role of consciousness in shaping our world.

Phenomenalism (also called Subjective Idealism) believes that physical objects and events are reducible to mental objects, properties, and events.

Ultimately, only mental objects (i.e., the mind) exist. Bishop Berkeley claimed that our body is merely a perception of the mind.

That claim sounds strange. But consider a real study.

Scientists studied three stroke victims with hemiplegia, meaning they had lost movement down one side of the body. Each had damage to the right hemisphere of the brain. Researchers asked them about their ability to move their arms.

All three claimed they could move both hands equally well. This was despite clear evidence to the contrary in a mirror placed in front of them.

Two of the three stroke victims went further. An experimental stooge faked paralysis in his left arm. The victims claimed he could still move it satisfactorily.

This view suggests that there is an objective, universal mind or consciousness that underlies reality.

Individual minds are seen as manifestations of this larger, cosmic mind.

### Implications of Monism

- Mind-Body Relationship:Monism, particularly materialism, suggests a close relationship between mind and brain. Mental states are seen as dependent on or identical to brain states.

- Scientific Approach:Materialism aligns well with thescientific worldview, as it allows for the study of the mind through the investigation of physical processes in the brain.

- Parsimony: Materialism offers a simpler and more parsimonious explanation of reality compared to dualism (the view that mind and body are two fundamentally different kinds of thing).

- Evidence: Studies using techniques like fMRI and EEG show specific brain regions activating during particular thoughts, emotions, and experiences.

- Challenges:Some philosophers argue that monism, especially materialism, struggles to account for subjective experience (qualia) and the unique nature of consciousness.

### What is Dualism?

Dualism, in the context of the mind-body problem, is the view that mental phenomena are, in some respects, non-physical, or that the mind and body are not identical.

A prominent dualist view holds that the mental and the physical are two fundamentally different categories of entities or properties. This is called substance dualism.

Its most famous example is Cartesian dualism, named after René Descartes. He argued that the mind is a non-physical substance, distinct from the physical substance of the body.

Substance dualism contrasts with monism, which holds that only one fundamental kind of substance exists.

Another form is property dualism. It holds that mental properties are distinct from, and not reducible to, physical properties, even if only one kind of substance exists.

Property dualism avoids the problem of explaining how two different substances interact. But it faces its own challenge: explaining how a mental property can have any causal effect in a world that is fundamentally physical.

A third dualist position, epiphenomenalism, denies this causal role outright. It holds that mental events are real, but causally inert by-products of physical brain processes. Like the whistle on a steam engine, it reflects what the engine is doing but does not drive it.

### Implications of Dualism

Dualism has shaped philosophy and religion, but many scientists see it as incompatible with a scientific worldview.

The main challenge is the interaction problem. Descartes proposed that mind and body meet at the pineal gland, but naming a location does not explain a mechanism. The puzzle just moves inside the skull.

Consider a student who decides to raise a hand in class. On Descartes’s view, that decision happens in a non-physical mind. It has no size or location at all.

The arm-raising, though, happens in a physical body: muscles and nerves that can be measured and placed in space. Explaining how the first causes the second is the problem dualism has never fully solved.

Despite this, dualism remains a popular view, defended by many contemporary philosophers. Two arguments recur most often.

The argument from qualia holds that subjective experiences, like the redness of red, cannot be reduced to physical properties. The argument from intentionality holds that mental states are always about something, while physical states are not.

- Dualists emphasize the unique and irreducible nature of subjective experiences, often referred to as qualia.

- Qualia are the qualitative aspects of consciousness, such as the redness of red, the feeling of pain, or the taste of chocolate.

- Dualists argue that these subjective experiences cannot be fully captured or explained by purely physical descriptions of brain states.

- They contend that qualia possess a distinctly mental quality that resists reduction to physical properties.

- Intentionality is the “aboutness” of mental states, their ability to represent or be directed at something beyond themselves.

- Dualists point out that mental states, such as beliefs, desires, and intentions, have this representational quality, while physical states do not.

- They argue that intentionality is a unique feature of the mental and cannot be explained in purely physical terms.

### Interactionism

Interactionism posits that the mind and body are distinct substances (as in dualism), but they causally interact with one another.

It recognizes that mental states, such as thoughts, emotions, and consciousness, possess qualities that are fundamentally different from the physical properties of matter, such as size, shape, and motion. Yet, it also asserts that these two realms are not isolated but rather intertwined in a causal relationship.

This means thatmental events can cause physical events, and physical events can cause mental events.

The mind is non-physical, but itinfluencesthe physical brain/body (and receives input from it).

A major challenge for interactionism is the interaction problem.

How can two fundamentally different substances, one physical and one non-physical, causally interact? This has been a source of much philosophical debate.

In the 20th century,Karl Popper& neurophysiologist John Eccles argued for dualistic interactionism (inThe Self and Its Brain, 1977), positing that the mind interacts with brain physiology.

René Descartes is the most famous proponent of interactionist dualism.

He argued that the mind (a non-physical “thinking substance”) and the body (a physical “extended substance”) interact, with this interaction occurring at the pineal gland in the brain.

### Critical Evaluation

- Interactionism aligns with our common-sense intuition that our thoughts, feelings, and intentions can influence our actions and that our physical experiences can affect our mental states.

- We experience this interaction daily: a desire (mental) leads to reaching for a glass of water (physical), or a stubbed toe (physical) causes pain (mental).

- This intuitive appeal and alignment with everyday experience provide a degree of support for interactionism.

- The placebo effect, where a person’s belief in a treatment can lead to real physiological changes, provides evidence for the mind’s influence on the body.

- Psychoneuroimmunology studies how psychological factors, such as stress and emotions, can affect the immune system, demonstrating a clear interaction between mental states and physical health.

- These findings support the notion that mental events can have tangible effects on the physical body.

- Phenomenology, a philosophical approach that focuses on subjective experience, emphasizes the unique and irreducible nature of consciousness.

- From a first-person perspective, it seems undeniable that our thoughts and feelings play a role in shaping our actions and experiences in the world.

- While not empirical evidence in the traditional sense, the phenomenological perspective lends support to the idea that mental states have causal efficacy in the physical world.

- The “hard problem of consciousness,” as defined by philosopher David Chalmers, highlights the difficulty of explaining how subjective experience arises from physical processes.

- Some philosophers argue that the inability of purely physicalist explanations to fully account for consciousness leaves room for interactionist perspectives.

- They suggest that the unique qualities of consciousness may require a distinct mental entity that interacts with the physical brain.

### Linguistic Behaviourism

A third answer to the mind-body problem comes from philosophy rather than psychology: linguistic behaviourism, developed by the later Ludwig Wittgenstein and, most influentially, by Gilbert Ryle.

### Ryle’s “Ghost in the Machine”

InThe Concept of Mind(1949), Ryle attacked the Cartesian picture of mind as a “ghost in the machine” trapped inside the body.

On this picture, mind is a private, inner realm of ghostly events, running alongside physical events in a kind of second theatre only its owner can see.

Ryle called this a category mistake. He compared it to a visitor touring a university’s colleges and libraries, then asking to be shown the University itself, as though it were one more building on campus.

The University is not a separate building. It is the collective name for how the colleges are organised. Treating “mind” as a separate thing alongside the body, Ryle argued, is the same kind of error.

Mind is not one more item in the world, alongside brains and bodies. It is a way of talking about how people behave.

### Mental Words Describe Behaviour

Ryle’s positive claim is that mental concepts refer to behaviour and behavioural dispositions, not to hidden inner episodes. Calling glass “brittle” means it will shatter when struck; calling someone “intelligent” means they tend to solve problems well and score highly on relevant tests.

Being angry, on this view, just means being disposed to shout, clench your fists, or storm off, given the right circumstances. Nothing more is needed.

Ryle also argued that the Cartesian picture makes psychology impossible in principle.

If inner mental events are private and accessible only to their owner, no one else could ever check a claim about them. Locating the causes of behaviour in an unobservable mind, he argued, blocks explanation rather than providing it.

This, for Ryle, makes behaviourism more scientific.

### Linguistic Behaviourism vs Skinner’s Radical Behaviourism

Ryle’s linguistic behaviourism is often confused with Skinner’sradical behaviourism, but the two solve the mind-body problem in different ways.

Ryle’s is a claim about meaning. A sentence like “John is hungry” was never really about a private inner episode, so there is no ghost to locate.

Skinner’s is a claim about causation. Private events like hunger do occur, but they are physical events, governed by the same laws of reinforcement that govern public behaviour. So there is no need to treat them as evidence of a separate mental substance.

Both positions reject Cartesian dualism. Both also try to make the mind safe for a science built on observable evidence, even though they get there by different routes. Each is a reaction against the same Cartesian picture, just approached from a different direction.

The differentapproaches to psychologytake contrasting views of whether the mind and body are separate or related.

Thinking (having freedom of choice) is a mental event, yet it can cause behavior to occur (muscles move in response to a thought).  Thinking can therefore be said to make things happen, “mind moves matter.”

Behavioristsbelieve that psychology should only be concerned with “observable actions,” namely stimulus and response.

They believe that thought processes such as the mind cannot be studied scientifically and objectively and should therefore be ignored. Radical behaviorists believe that the mind does not even exist.

Biologists take a similar line. If there is no mind, they argue, then the brain itself must ultimately be the mind.

This, they argue, is only a matter of time. The brain’s structures, cells, and neural connections will eventually reveal it.

Behaviorists and biologists both believe only one type of reality exists: what we can see, feel, and touch. That makes their approach a form of monism, specifically materialism. The mind and brain are, on this view, the same thing.

However, biologists and behaviorists cannot account for the phenomenon of hypnosis. Hilgard and Orne studied this effect directly.

They placed participants in a hypnotic trance. Using unconscious suggestion, they told each participant they would be touched with a “red hot” piece of metal. In fact, the researchers touched them with a pencil.

Participants in a deep trance showed a skin reaction, including water blisters, just as if they had been burned. This is one example of the mind controlling the body’s reaction. Similar results have been found in patients given hypnosis to control pain.

This contradicts monism. The body should not react to an unconscious suggestion. Instead, it supports dualism: mind and body can act separately.

In the same way, humanists likeCarl Rogerswould also dispute materialism monism.

Humanists believe that subjective experience is the only way to study human behavior. They do not deny that the real world exists. Instead, they argue that each person’s unique, subjective view of reality is what matters most.

This shapes how humanists view mental illness. ASchizophrenicperson might not define their own actions as ill. They may believe instead they have insight others lack.

Yet a purely subjective view of reality has problems. The paranoid schizophrenic who believes the postal service “are agents for the government and trying to kill him” is still mentally ill. They still need treatment if they are not to be a danger to themselves or the public.

Recent research offers a new angle. Cognitive psychologists have applied it to this debate.

They compare the brain to computer hardware, “wired” or connected to the human body. The mind, in turn, is like the software running on it.

This software analogy can explain why people react differently to the same stimulus. It ties in with cognitive mediational (thinking) processes. In effect, it offers a modern version of dualism, replacing Descartes’s “I think therefore I am” with the language of computers and software.

Contemporary psychology and neuroscience tend to favor monistic views, particularly materialism, emphasizing the brain’s role in mental processes.

### Contemporary Debates

Modern perspectives on the mind-body link:

### Cognitive Neuroscience

Uses brain imaging andneuroscienceto study how mental processes relate to brain activity.

It provides evidence of tight mind-brain correlations (specific thoughts, emotions, or behaviors correspond to activity in specific brain regions) and seeks theneuralbasis of consciousness.

In essence, cognitive neuroscience aims to explain mental operations via the brain’s information-processing architecture​, bridging the gap between mind and body through empirical research.

### Embodied Cognition

The view that the mind is not an isolated “brain in a vat” but is shaped by the body’s interactions with the environment. Cognitive processes depend on the body’s movement, perception, and context.

Thus, thinking isembodied– the brain alone is not the whole story.

This challenges traditional cognitive theory and mind-body dualism by arguing that an organism’s bodily experience is fundamental to its mental life. Physical gestures, for example, can influence problem-solving, suggesting that cognition is grounded in action.

### Panpsychism & Integrated Information Theory

These emerging views consider consciousness as a fundamental aspect of reality.

Panpsychismholds that consciousness is a basic feature of the universe. On this view, even elementary particles have a very rudimentary, mind-like quality. In other words, some trace of mind pervades all matter.

Integrated Information Theory(IIT) takes a different approach. Proposed by Giulio Tononi, it suggests that consciousness corresponds to the degree of integrated information in a system.

It treats consciousness as an intrinsic property of certain organized networks. Take any system, a brain or even a simple circuit. Once it integrates information above some threshold, it has a degree of experience.

These theories are controversial. But they offer an alternative to the mind-body problem: consciousness may not be an accidental fluke of brains. Instead, it could be an inherent property of physical systems under the right conditions.

### Critical Evaluation of the Mind-Body Problem

None of the positions above has fully solved the mind-body problem. Two long-standing difficulties, and one large modern study, show why the debate is still open.

### The Interaction Problem

Descartes placed the meeting point of mind and body at the pineal gland, but naming a location does not explain a mechanism. The puzzle just moves inside the skull.

Popper and Eccles (1977) tried to update interactionism, arguing that a self-conscious mind acts on “modules” within the brain. Critics point out that this still requires an unexplained moment where something non-physical produces a physical effect. That sits awkwardly with the assumption that the physical world is causally self-sufficient.

Kim (2010) presses the point further. The physical world looks causally closed, so adding a non-physical cause risks over-determining events physical causes already explain.

Not every philosopher accepts that the interaction should be framed as two substances meeting at all. Davidson’s anomalous monism and Dennett’s explanatory pluralism both try to dissolve this problem rather than solve it.

### Multiple Realisability

A second problem faces materialism specifically. The identity theory claims that a mental state, like pain, simply is a particular type of brain state, such as the firing of certain nerve fibres.

Putnam (1961, 1967) pointed out that very different physical systems can share the same mental state. Consider three cases.

A human, an octopus, and even a suitably built machine could all plausibly be “in pain” or “want” something, despite having no neural structures in common. If pain just is one specific brain process, creatures without it cannot be in pain by definition. That looks like the wrong result.

This challenge is why many philosophers moved from strict identity theory toward functionalism. Functionalism defines a mental state by what causes it and what it causes, not by its physical make-up.

### Contemporary Research

Recent research puts rival physicalist theories of consciousness to a direct empirical test, rather than settling the debate by argument alone.

Aim:The Cogitate Consortium (2025) tested two rival theories of consciousness, global neuronal workspace theory and integrated information theory. The test compared each against the other’s own predictions, not just against a null hypothesis.

Method:256 healthy adults viewed visual stimuli of varying duration while their brain activity was recorded using fMRI, MEG, and, in some participants, intracranial EEG.

Results:Neither theory came through unscathed. The predicted sustained synchronisation in posterior cortex, expected under integrated information theory, was not confirmed. Global workspace theory’s predicted neural “ignition” at stimulus offset was only partially supported. The evidence, overall, was mixed.

Conclusion:Because both theories had pre-registered their predictions, the study could disconfirm specific parts of each rather than simply fail to support a preferred one.

It stands as one of the most rigorous tests yet of how consciousness relates to measurable brain activity, even without settling which theory, if either, is correct.

Philosopher David Chalmers (1995) argues that even a complete account of what a cognitive system does would leave one question unanswered. Why is any of that processing accompanied by subjective experience at all, rather than happening “in the dark”?

Chalmers’s own preferred answer (Chalmers, 2015) takes panpsychism seriously. Physics, on this view, describes only the causal structure of matter, never its intrinsic nature, which could itself be proto-experiential.

On this view, consciousness is not something that suddenly switches on once neurons are wired correctly. It is a complex arrangement of simpler, more basic properties already present in matter.
