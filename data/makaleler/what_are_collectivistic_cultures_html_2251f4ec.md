### Giriş

Psychology»Social Psychology

Collectivism is a cultural orientation in which people define themselves as members of a group, valuing shared harmony and mutual obligation above individual goals and personal achievement.

### Key takeaways

- Collectivism:the tendency, at individual and societal level, to view oneself as interdependent and a member of a group rather than as an independent being.

- In-Groups:in collectivist cultures, people belong to larger in-groups that care for them in exchange for loyalty (Hofstede & Bond, 1984), valuing collaboration, communalism, and conformity to roles and norms.

- Group Needs:collectivist cultures especially emphasize social harmony, respectfulness, and group needs over individual needs.

### Collectivist cultural traits

Collectivism has four important cultural traits.

- Firstly, the definition of the self as interdependent — in relation to other people rather than in the abstract traits of an individual (Markus and Kitayama, 1991b; Reykowski, 1994).

- Secondly, an alignment of personal and communal goals — meaning that the individual makes decisions accounting for the wants of the collective and what they are bringing or taking away from the group.

- Thirdly, there is a greater consideration of social norms than individual attitudes when making decisions. Lastly, there is an emphasis on relationships, even if disadvantageous for individuals.

These cultural traits result in societies being divided into ingroups. While any given person is likely to belong to a few ingroups, the dynamics and closeness of those in ingroups are closer than those in individualistic cultures (Triandis 1995).

Collectivism has roots stretching back to Plato’s Republic. Interpretations of Confucian ethics — which emphasize goodwill, filial piety, balance, and norms — have shaped far-eastern cultures for nearly 25 centuries (Arcodia).

However, it was not until the mid-to-late 20th century that social scientists began to define and quantify collectivism in its current form.

Geert Hofstede’scultural dimensions theory(1980) examined people’s values in the workplace and identified four dimensions: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity/femininity, and individualism/collectivism. The framework became hugely influential.

The core of collectivism is the idea that groups bind together and mutually obligate individuals (Kemmelmeier 2002). Collectivists also value security, good social relationships, and harmony within ingroups.

Personalized relationships matter too (Triandis, McCusker, & Hui, 1990). Status shapes many of these obligations (Schwartz 1994).

The personal is merely a component of the social. Ingroups, rather than individuals, are considered the basic unit of a collectivist society.

These ingroups can be as diverse as family, clan, ethnicity, or religion, so collectivist values can be broader than those of individualism. Society sets the terms.

Ingroups are malleable and can occasionally extend to an entire society. For example, collectivism was a powerful predictor of mask use during the COVID-19 pandemic, both among individuals and cultures (Jin & English 2021).

### Individualistic vs. Collectivist Cultures

Individualist culturesand collectivist cultures place emphasis on different basic values.

People who live in individualist cultures tend to believe that independence, competition, and personal achievement are important.

Individuals in Western nations such as the United States, England, and Australia score high on individualism (Oyserman, Coon, & Kemmelmeier, 2002).

People who live in collectivist cultures value social harmony, respectfulness, and group needs over individual needs. Individuals who live in countries in Asia, Africa, and South America score high on collectivism (Hofstede, 2001; Triandis, 1995). These values influence personality.

Traditionally, collectivism stands in contrast to individualism as one of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (1980). Those in collectivist cultures value group cohesion, even at the expense of the individual, while those in individualistic cultures value the self over the group.

However, the difference between collectivism and individualism lies more in the issues each stance focuses on rather than in having opposite positions on the same issues (Kagitcibasi, 1987). The consequences of individualism and collectivism are as far-reaching as language, views of the self, and mobility at work.

### Examples of Collectivist Cultures

Individualism and collectivism are so deeply ingrained in a culture that they mold our very self-conceptions and identities.

### Self-Construal: East and West

According to Markus and Kitayama (1991), most North Americans and Europeans hold an independentview of the self. This self is distinct, autonomous, and self-contained, with its own unique dispositions. The contrast with collectivist cultures is stark.

In much of Asia, Africa, and Latin America, people instead hold an interdependent view of the self. This self is part of a larger social network that includes family, co-workers, and others to whom people feel connected. The emotional consequences differ too.

The two selves rarely meet halfway.

Markus and Kitayama report that Americans are more likely to express jealousy, pride, and other ‘ego-focused’ emotions that affirm the self as autonomous. Non-westerners, by contrast, more often experience ‘other-focused’ emotions that promote social harmony. Language reflects this difference too.

In Japan, for example, people often report feelings of oime (‘indebtedness to someone’), fureai (‘connection with someone’), and shitashimi (‘familiarity to someone’).

Hofstede also ranked whole countries on the same underlying dimension.

Hofstede’s original survey rated countries on a scale from 1 to 100 based on aggregated responses (1980). Guatemala, Ecuador, Panama, Venezuela, and Colombia emerged as the most collectivistic countries in his survey.

Eight of the ten most collectivistic cultures were in South and Central America, with the other two, Indonesia and Pakistan, in Asia and the Middle East (Hofstede, 1991).

### Country Examples

Malaysia is a prototypically collectivist culture. From birth, families encourage children to make ingroups the center of their lives. Many families live in intergenerational households, and child-rearing emphasizes both warmth and control (Kesharvarez & Baharudin, 2011).

Employer-employee relationships exist on moral terms, and promotions often depend on one’s status within the employer ingroup. Malaysia is changing quickly. Levels of collectivism are lower among the young, the wealthy, and the urbanized in Malaysia, as in other fast-developing Asian economies like China and Japan (Cao, 2009).

Even so, Malaysian employees report a surprisingly high degree of competition with one another, a distinctly individualistic trait (Noordin, 2004).

Turkey is another highly collectivist culture, where decisions ranging from marriage to travel plans depend heavily on their impact on the family (Göregenli, 1997).

Turkish communities in Western Europe show this especially clearly. They maintain notably rigid social norms, a cultural “tightness”, compared with the general population.

Triandis (1995) reports a stark example: the killing of a Turkish-born teenage girl living in France, carried out because she had integrated too much into French society. Her family judged that the family’s need for cohesion outweighed her life.

India tells a different story. Some collectivist cultures, such as parts of India, place a high value on hierarchy. Triandis (1995) calls these vertical collectivist cultures.

Indian society encourages cohesion and interdependence across generations. Approximately 90% of Indian marriages are arranged by families (Jaiswal, 2014), and a strict caste system separates the roles and occupations of families and ingroups (Barreman, 1966).

Israel looks different again. In contrast, horizontal collectivist cultures — such as Israeli kibbutzim — emphasize equality rather than hierarchy. Kibbutzim are traditionally agrarian settlements where each member contributes to a collective goal, and children are raised communally.

This equal, collective commitment gives kibbutzim high levels of gender and material equality (Agassi, 1989).

### Causes of Collectivism

There are three main factors that lead to collectivism: (i) complexity of a society, (ii) class, and (iii) heterogeneity, such as child-rearing practices (Triandis, 1994).

The majority of the world, about 70% of the population, was collectivist at the end of the 20th century (Triandis 1995). The pattern held fairly consistently worldwide. Countries with lower wealth tend to be more collectivistic; economic growth tends to make cultures more individualistic.

Conversely, economic recession correlated with increased collectivism (Bianchi 2016). Historically, collectivistic communities have been agrarian, where those in, for example, a village hold interdependent roles in a common farm. Philosophies, such as Confucianism, have a heavy hold on contemporary Asian collectivism.

Women tend to be more collectivistic on average than men (Zeffane 2017). Collectivism can sometimes manifest in very-involved child-rearing.

For example, Chinese families do most activities together. Parents stay in close proximity to their children at all times, especially before the child turns five. This fosters mutual dependency, making it desirable for a child to live with older parents or visit frequently.

Discipline still stays warm. After scolding a child, in Chinese and Russian cultures, the parent attempts to reestablish closeness through affection, resulting in a personality that is optimistic, trusting, and sociable (Rohner 1986). Collectivist child-rearing tends to be more affectionate but more controlling than individualistic child-rearing.

Family size matters too. On the reverse, collectivistic child rearing can also be the result of a large family.

Extended families make collectivism more likely because any one child cannot receive special treatment. Parents in large extended families do not have the resources to cater to the exacting needs of any one child when, for example, cooking (Triandis 1995).

### Self Concept

Collectivists tend to define themselves in relation to others, and group membership is a central aspect of collectivist identity (Hofstede, 1980).

The self does not encompass abstract traits, as it does for individualists. Group traits matter more instead. The collectivist’s life satisfaction comes from successfully carrying out social roles and obligations tied to that group (Markus & Kitayama 1991).

This manifests literally in the naming conventions of some collectivist cultures. For example, in traditional Indonesian culture, people have first names but are referred to more commonly by their birth order (Triandis 1995).

In collectivism, people are interdependent rather than independent. The well-being of the group defines the success and well-being of the individual, and as such, one protects oneself by considering the needs and feelings of others.

History is more important to collectivists than individualists, as collectivists see themselves as part of a long chain of relationships over hundreds and thousands of years.

The values of this historical collective supersede those of the individual, and thus, the decisions of the collective can trump those of the individualist (Triandis 1995).

### Relationships

Collectivist relationships are centered around the ingroup. However, these ingroups vary dramatically between individualists and collectivists.

Individualists maintain ties to a large number of ingroups, each revolving around a shared interest. Collectivists belong to a more limited number of ingroups instead, defined by a diverse set of values and interests (Hofstede 1980).

Collectivists value interpersonal harmony and will maintain established relationships even if these relationships are not in their best interests. Not every bond is unconditional.

This is not to say, however, that collectivists do not make some calculations as to whether or not to maintain a relationship. Loyalty still has its limits.

Regardless, collectivists’ relationships tend to be deeper and more emotionally invested than those of individualists. These relationships are tied to the belief that helping others contributes to the well-being of the self.

This is reflected in the language of traditionally collectivist cultures, such as Japan. Shinyuu, which translates to “best friend,” carries deep emotional investment. Aaron Cargile’s studies found the term leads people to make major life decisions around a friend’s well-being.

The pattern is not unique to Japan.

This feeling of shinyuu is often described by Japanese individuals as being willing to give up one’s life for another (Cargile 2012). Collectivism gives little credence to the idea that relationships happen between individuals. Rather, relationships happen in groups.

Adults from Asian collectivist cultures, for example, are more likely to do activities, such as skiing, in groups. Those from individualistic cultures, by contrast, tend to do such sports alone or in couples. The pattern repeats elsewhere.

Sport is rarely a solo pursuit.

The Japanese word Nakama, meaning friend group, describes interpersonal relationships. It carries the connotation that friendship happens interdependently rather than between any two individuals (Cargile 2012). Word choice reflects this too.

This sense of closeness and interdependence extends to the collectivist extended family and the child-rearing practices of collectivist societies. For example, a stranger may scold a Russian mother for not wrapping up her baby tightly. Parenting is seen as communal.

Even discipline stays relational.

The outcomes and ways of parenting children are seen as an extension of the group as a whole (Triandis 1995). While individualistic relationships tend to work as social contracts, collectivist relationships work on agreeability (Kemmelmeier 2002). Harmony often wins out.

While an individualist may believe that making one’s intentions clear through an argument is beneficial to a relationship, collectivists stray away from conflict. Agreeability often comes at the cost of honesty, and specific forms of dishonesty can be socially sanctioned. Politeness can mask the truth.

For example, in traditional Greek culture, a surprise visitor can be turned away by the owner shouting, “I am not here,” through the door.

This is clearly not a true statement, since the guest can presumably recognize the unwilling host’s voice. It is, instead, the social script for revealing disinterest.

Collectivism also alters how people see social situations. For example, those in Hispanic collectivist cultures are disproportionately likely to associate social situations with positive feelings and outcomes (Triandis 1995).

Chinese collectivists tend to emphasize common feelings, social usefulness, and acceptance of authority when analyzing social situations. Australians, a highly individualistic culture by contrast, emphasize competitiveness, self-confidence, and freedom instead (Kagitcibasia & Berry, 1989).

### Conformity

Collectivistic cultures tend to be “tight,” meaning that there is a very limited acceptable range of behaviors for any given situation. The rules are strict.

For example, the traditional Japanese tea ceremony involves a group sitting in an uncomfortable position for about an hour around a fascinating object. The highest-ranking member sits closest to the object, and the lowest-ranking member sits farthest away.

The group members listen to the distinctive sounds of hot and cold water falling before the tea is offered to the highest-ranking member. Rank shapes the order.

He must decline a specific number of times before accepting, after which the tea is offered to the second-highest ranking member. This calculated process of denial continues with each member and follows a strict sense of customs that must not be broken.

Custom governs every step.

However, the formalized rituals of collectivism are fading as countries undergo rapid economic growth. For example, few in the younger Japanese population have experienced a tea ceremony (Triandis 1995).

Modernization is changing this. This parallels a general decline in traditional roles, such as Japanese adults caring for elders (Bellah 1985). Collectivists value conformity within the ingroup, but this is not so in relation to outgroups.

Outgroups are treated differently. Counterintuitively, those in highly collectivistic cultures can display anti-conformity at higher levels than those from individualistic cultures. A classic study probed this pattern directly.

- Aim:Berry (1967) tested whether a culture’s collectivism or individualism predicts how much its members conform to group pressure.

- Method:He compared the Temne of Sierra Leone (collectivist rice-farmers) with the Inuit of Baffin Island (individualist hunter-fishers) and a sample of Scots, using anAsch-style line-judgment taskwith a misleading hint about the group norm.

- Results:The Temne strongly accepted the suggested norm, while the Inuit almost entirely disregarded it.

- Conclusion:Berry argued the difference reflects how much conformity each culture’s social and environmental conditions demand, supporting the idea that a culture’s individualism shapes members’ behaviour.

Frager’s (1970) replication of the Asch conformity experiment tested this directly. In the task, subjects either went along with or against an incorrect group when comparing the shapes of lines. Japanese subjects conformed less than their US counterparts, an extraordinarily individualist culture (Triandis et al. 1988).

Although collectivism values harmony, more recent studies complicate this picture. Members of collectivist ingroups can be more vigilant toward other group members than those in individualistic ingroups. They stay mindful of others’ unethical intentions (Liu et al. 2009).

As the people in these groups are interdependent, the unethical behavior of an individual can be the downfall of all.

### Mental Health

Collectivistic cultures sometimes consider individuality a form of disharmony, and this can have major effects on “acultured” individualists within a collectivistic culture. Triandis (1995) recounts the story of a Japanese schoolchild who had lived in Tokyo but returned to his rural town. The consequences can be severe.

Not speaking the local dialect, he was bullied by the group of schoolchildren as a whole and eventually found dead in a utility closet. Despite the potentially dire consequences of being excluded from the ingroup, those in collectivist cultures tend to be bullied in lower numbers overall.

This holds even compared with highly individualistic countries such as the United States (Smith & Robinson 2019). The costs are real.

“Acultured” individuals also suffer from depression and suicidal ideation at higher rates than individualists in individualist cultures. However, collectivist cultures tend to have lower suicide rates as a whole (Eskin et al., 2020).

Not all outcomes are negative.

Matsumoto (1989) discovered that collectivists more readily perceive sadness than individualists and are less likely to perceive happiness. Aaron Cargile (2012) has theorized that this is because collectivists tend to find rating oneself as better than others undesirable. Modesty is often the norm.

Japanese men and women tend to underrate, or more realistically rate, their skills in comparison to others. While the average American university student said he was better than 63% of the population at a given task, Japanese students averaged 47% (Triandis 2005). The gap held broadly.

As a result, collectivist culture is associated with lower levels ofself-esteemand feelings of mastery (Yetim 2003).

### Critical Evaluation of Collectivism Research

Hofstede’s framework is highly influential, but it has attracted sustained methodological criticism. Three issues matter most: how the original data were collected, whether a single national score can represent an entire culture, and whether decades-old findings still hold today.

### Methodological Critique

Sociologist Brendan McSweeney (2002) argued that Hofstede’s national culture scores are an artefact of the method rather than a discovery about culture itself.

The original IBM sample was not nationally representative: respondents worked for one multinational company, mostly in marketing and service roles, and some country samples held fewer than 100 questionnaires. Sample sizes varied widely by country.

McSweeney also argued that averaging survey answers guarantees a national mean whether or not it reflects anything genuinely shared.

Corporate and occupational influences from working at IBM, he added, cannot be cleanly separated from national ones. IBM itself had a strong culture.

He concluded the model was, in his own words, “a triumph of faith” over rigorous analysis. Hofstede’s defenders counter that the samples only needed to be comparable across countries, not representative of them, since the theory measures differences between nations rather than absolute values.

### The Nation vs Culture Problem

Giving each country a single score implies a cultural uniformity that does not really exist. Within any nation, values vary by region, class, generation, and subculture, and cultural groups often spill across national borders altogether. This is not just a theoretical worry.

The evidence bears this out.

Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg’s (1988) meta-analysis of attachment studies using theStrange Situationacross eight countries found that variation within a country exceeded variation between countries. Oyserman, Coon, and Kemmelmeier’s (2002) meta-analysis of individual-level individualism-collectivism measures reached a similar conclusion, finding several counter-stereotypical results. The pattern held broadly.

European Americans, for example, did not consistently score lower on collectivism than some East Asian samples. National averages, in other words, conceal a great deal of internal diversity behind a single number.

Averages can mislead.

### Contemporary Research

Do Hofstede’s dimensions still hold up against more recent data? Santos, Varnum, and Grossmann (2017) analysed 51 years of survey data across 78 countries. They found individualist practices and values rising in most of them, with socioeconomic development the strongest predictor of the shift.

This is evidence that the dimension scores are moving targets rather than fixed traits. Not entirely, though.

Minkov (2018) ran a fresh, purpose-built replication using new probability-style consumer samples from 56 countries. His analysis recovered a robust individualism-collectivism dimension, which absorbed power distance, and a second flexibility-monumentalism dimension resembling long-term orientation.

Masculinity and uncertainty avoidance, though, failed to re-emerge as coherent national dimensions in the new data. Not every dimension survived the retest.

Muthukrishna and colleagues (2020) used World Values Survey data to build a cultural-distance metric measuring how psychologically far any two societies are from each other. This gave the WEIRD critique (Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010) a concrete, quantifiable measure and confirmed that American samples remain an extreme outlier.

Individualism-collectivism looks like the best-replicated part of Hofstede’s model. The other dimensions need more caution.
