### ABC Model of Attitude

The ABC Model of Attitudes, also known as the tri-component, tripartite, or tricomponent model, describes three components of attitudes (Eagly & Chaiken, 1998):

- Affective component:a person’s feelings or emotions about the attitude object. For example: “I am scared of spiders.”

- Behavioral component:how the attitude leads someone to act. For example: “I will avoid spiders and scream if I see one.”

- Cognitive component:a person’s beliefs or knowledge about the attitude object. For example: “I believe spiders are dangerous.”

These three components collectively form an individual’s attitude toward an object, person, issue, or situation.

Origins of the model.Rosenberg and Hovland (1960) first formalized this tri-component structure as part of a Yale research program on how persuasive communication changes attitudes.

- Aim:To give attitudes a formal, response-based definition that unified three separate research traditions on affect, cognition, and behavior.

- Method:A theoretical chapter synthesizing a decade of Yale-group research on communication and attitude change.

- Findings:Rosenberg and Hovland proposed that an attitude is a predisposition that triggers three types of response to a stimulus: affective, cognitive, and behavioral.

- Conclusion:An attitude is a hypothetical construct inferred from the pattern across these three responses. Consistency between them was the working assumption, and inconsistency later became the puzzle that inspired cognitive dissonance research.

### Affective Component

The affective component of an attitude refers to the emotional reactions or feelings an individual has towards an object, person, issue, or situation.

This component involves feelings or emotional responses like liking, disliking, love, hate, fear, etc.  It is essentially the emotional aspect of an attitude that can influence an individual’s behavior.

For instance, if someone feels positive about exercising, this is an affective response that may make them more likely to engage in physical activity.

Suppose someone has a fear of spiders (the affective component). They believe spiders are harmful (the cognitive component). As a result, they avoid places where spiders might be present and scream if they see one (the behavioral component).

### Behavioral Component

The behavioral component of an attitude refers to how one behaves or acts towards an object, person, issue, or situation based on their attitude.

It involves an individual’s tendency to behave in a certain way toward the attitude object.

For example, suppose a person has a positive attitude toward healthy eating (the affective and cognitive components). This shows up in their behavioral component: they choose fruits and vegetables often, avoid fast food, and cook meals at home.

### Cognitive Component

The cognitive component of an attitude refers to the beliefs, thoughts, and attributes that an individual associates with an object, person, issue, or situation. It involves the mental processes of understanding and interpreting information.

For example, suppose a person believes that recycling benefits the environment and effectively conserves natural resources. In that case, this represents the cognitive component of their positive attitude towards recycling.

This cognitive component can influence their feelings about recycling (affective component) and their likelihood of engaging in recycling behaviors (behavioral component).

Theknowledge functionties directly to the cognitive component, since it shapes how people interpret and make sense of their beliefs. When the attitude object is a social group, the cognitive component works like a stereotype: beliefs about the group’s typical traits.

### Attitude Strength

The strength with which an attitude is held is often a good predictor of behavior. The stronger the attitude, the more likely it should affect behavior. Attitude strength involves:

Importance / personal relevancerefers to how significant the attitude is for the person and relates to self-interest, social identification, and value.

If an attitude is held by a group someone belongs to, or wants to belong to, it carries high self-interest. That makes it extremely important, since it connects directly to the person’s own values.

As a consequence, the attitude will have a very strong influence on a person’s behavior. By contrast, an attitude will not be important to a person if it does not relate in any way to their life.

Theknowledgeaspect of attitude strength covers how much a person knows about the attitude object. People are generally more knowledgeable about topics that interest them and are likely to hold strong attitudes (positive or negative) as a consequence.

Attitudes based on direct experience are more strongly held and influence behavior more than attitudes formed indirectly (for example, through hearsay, reading, or watching television).

### Principle of Consistency

One of the underlying assumptions about the link between attitudes and behavior is that of consistency.

This means that we often or usually expect a person’s behavior to be consistent with their attitudes. This is called the principle of consistency.

The principle of consistency assumes that people behave rationally, so their actions should match their attitudes.

This principle may sound reasonable, but people do not always follow it. Someone might keep smoking cigarettes while knowing smoking causes lung cancer and heart disease.

The clearest evidence against strict consistency comes from LaPiere’s (1934) classic study of prejudice and behavior.

- Aim:To test whether verbal expressions of racial prejudice actually predict how people behave toward the group in question.

- Method:Richard LaPiere traveled around the United States with a young Chinese couple, visiting 251 hotels, auto-camps, and restaurants between 1930 and 1932. Six months after each visit, he mailed a questionnaire asking whether the establishment would serve Chinese guests.

- Results:The couple was refused service only once across all 251 encounters. Yet over 90% of the 128 establishments that returned the questionnaire said they would not serve Chinese guests.

- Conclusion:LaPiere concluded that attitudes measured through questionnaires do not reliably predict what people actually do in real situations.

This gap appears partly because the two measurements did not match. Staff served a specific, well-dressed couple, but the questionnaire asked about the abstract category of “Chinese guests.”LaPiere’s finding is best read as evidence that loosely matched measures of attitude and behavior correspond poorly, not that attitudes never matter.

### Critical Evaluation of the ABC Model

The ABC model has held up well as a starting framework. Decades of research, though, have exposed real limits in how consistently its three components work together.

### Strengths of the Model

Rosenberg and Hovland’s (1960) tri-component structure gave psychologists a shared vocabulary that still organizes textbook discussions of attitudes six decades later. It also set up one of social psychology’s most productive research questions: what happens when feelings, beliefs, and behavior do not line up?

Part of the model’s staying power comes from how precisely it was specified. Rosenberg and Hovland did not just name three components; they paired each one with its own measurement class, so a researcher testing the model always knew what evidence would count as affective, cognitive, or behavioral.

Affect was tied to verbal reports of feeling and physiological reactions, cognition to verbal beliefs and perceptual judgments, and behavior to overt actions and verbal statements of intent. That specificity turned a loose three-part label into a testable research program.

That question directly inspired Festinger’s (1957) cognitive dissonance theory, which has since generated well over a thousand experimental studies, making it one of social psychology’s most productive theoretical frameworks. Few three-part typologies from the early 1960s have kept generating new empirical work six decades on.

### Limitations of the Model

The model’s biggest weakness is measurement. Self-report questionnaires are easily distorted by social desirability, since people often say what sounds acceptable rather than what they truly feel.

Indirect measures like the Implicit Association Test avoid this problem, but they bring their own limits, including modest test-retest reliability (Kurdi et al., 2019).

Cross-cultural research also challenges the model’s consistency assumption. Heine and Lehman (1997) found that Canadian participants shifted their attitude to match a difficult choice, but Japanese participants did not. This suggests some “consistency effects” reflect Western, individualist assumptions about the self rather than a universal feature of attitudes.

Taken together, these findings challenge the assumption that an attitude is a fixed trait. Much of what looks like a stable evaluation is actually constructed at the moment someone is asked for it.

### Contemporary Research

Albarracín and Shavitt’s (2018) review of the field treats attitudes less like fixed, stored evaluations. Instead, people construct their attitudes in the moment, shaped by context, mood, and goals.

On this view, the affective, cognitive, and behavioral components are flexible inputs a person draws on, not permanent features of a single underlying attitude.

The same review points to a narrowing gap between implicit and explicit attitude measures.Cognitive dissonanceand dual-process persuasion still explain most of how attitudes change. Modern work has added evaluative conditioning and source-similarity effects to the toolkit, without replacing these classic mechanisms.

Digital and networked communication has made source cues more powerful than ever. Algorithmic curation and online echo chambers now shape which arguments people encounter, though the underlying mechanisms of change stay the same.

### Key Takeaways

- Three Components:The ABC model explains attitudes through three parts: affective (feelings), behavioral (actions), and cognitive (beliefs), first formalized by Rosenberg and Hovland (1960).

- Attitude Strength:Attitudes held with high personal relevance or built from direct experience are stronger and predict behavior more reliably.

- The Consistency Gap:People do not always act in line with their attitudes. LaPiere (1934) found that hotels who said by mail they would refuse Chinese guests had, months earlier, actually served that same couple in person.

- Measurement Matters:Self-report scales can be distorted by social desirability, and even indirect measures like the Implicit Association Test have real psychometric limits.

- Modern View:Contemporary research treats attitudes as judgments people construct in the moment, shaped by mood and context, rather than fixed evaluations stored permanently in memory.
