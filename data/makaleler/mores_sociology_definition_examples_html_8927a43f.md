## 📝 Makale Bölümleri ve Detaylı İçerik

### Giriş

Mores(pronounced “MOR-ays”) are a specific type ofsocial normthat carries significant moral weight and is considered essential to the well-being and stability of a society.

### 1. Definition and Characteristics

- Moral Weight:Mores define the moral beliefs, customs, and ideals that determine acceptable and expected behavior within a group.

- Stronger Norms:They are preferred and socially sanctioned ways of behaving that involve fundamental habits; they are much stronger than “folkways” (casual daily customs).

- Unwritten Rules:Unlike laws, which are formally codified, mores are often unwritten rules of social behavior backed by a sense of morality.

- Social Approval:They tend to enforce themselves through the collective approval or disapproval of the community.

### 2. Origin and Development

- Shared Understanding:Mores arise when groups live together long enough to develop shared understandings of how membersshouldbehave.

- Foundational Sources:They can be based on long-standing customs, religious foundations, or peer pressure within a society.

- The “Sumner” Connection:The term was coined by sociologistWilliam Graham Sumnerin his 1906 workFolkways.

- Not Deliberately Designed:A more is not created on purpose. It builds up through everyday practice until a judgement about group welfare or moral rightness becomes attached to it.

- From Folkway to More:The same custom can be promoted into a more once that moral judgement takes hold. That is why a more’s strength varies across societies and eras.

### 3. Consequences of Violation

- Strong Social Response:Failure to conform to mores results in a much harsher social reaction than violating a simple folkway.

- Sanctions:Punishment for violating mores is used to enforce social order and preserve group cohesion. This can include being shunned, banned, or viewed as “shameful” by the public.

- Informal vs. Formal:Most everyday enforcement isinformal: gossip, exclusion, being labelled a liar. A serious violation can still escalate toformal sanction, such as expulsion for academic dishonesty.

- Modern Example:Telling a teacher to “Bugger off” when asked to stop talking violates the moral principles of respect and authority within the school system.

### 1. Moral Significance and Function

- Standards of Morality:Mores are deeply held standards of what is right and wrong. Unlike folkways, which govern politeness (e.g., table manners), mores define behavior as moral or immoral.

- Regulators of Social Life:They act as the primary regulators of behavior by tying morality directly to action, strongly influencing how individuals conduct themselves within a community.

- Protectors of Values:They function to protect the underlying values of a society and are viewed as social requirements rather than personal choices.

- Universal Understanding:They are usually unwritten rules that are instinctively understood by everyone in a community.

### 2. Stability and Institutional Support

- Persistence and Stability:Mores are passed down through generations. While they can evolve over time, they are much more stable and persistent than casual folkways.

- Spiritual Foundation:Major institutions like religion often provide the spiritual or moral foundation for a community’s mores.

- Religious Sanction:Many mores around sexuality, honesty, and the sacred trace their force to a religious framework that treats a violation as sin, not just a social misstep.

- Educational Reinforcement:The education system reinforces these values by teaching them to children through both the formal and “hidden” curriculum.

- Hidden Curriculum:Beyond formal lessons, schools reinforce mores informally through everyday routines: the punctuality, deference, and academic honesty students absorb simply by attending school.

- Legal Codification:The legal system ensures mores are obeyed by transforming some of them into formal laws.

### 3. Sanctions and Social Enforcement

- Severe Sanctions:Because they are essential to a group’s welfare, violations elicit strong social disapproval and severe negative sanctions.

- Social Ostracism:Violators may be shunned, banned from social groups, or labeled as “shameful” by the public.

- Loss of Status:Breaking these norms can lead to public ridicule, a “spoiled identity,” or the loss of employment.

- Institutional Punishment:In formal settings, such as the U.S. school system, violating mores like academic integrity (e.g., plagiarism) can result in extreme consequences like expulsion.

### The Mores-to-Law Pipeline

Some mores stay informal forever. Others get absorbed directly into formal law. That turns an unwritten moral standard into a codified, state-enforced rule.

### Moral Entrepreneurs and the Making of Law

Formal rules rarely emerge automatically from shared morality. The sociologist Howard Becker (1963) named the people who do this. He called themmoral entrepreneurs: individuals or groups who actively campaign to get their own view of right and wrong written into law.

A moral entrepreneur also decides who counts asdeviantfor breaking that new rule.

Their campaign is rarely neutral. It is often the mechanism by which one group’s more becomes a rule binding everyone else, including people who never agreed with it.

University honour codes are a familiar, everyday example of the same process. A committee decides that plagiarism deserves expulsion, not just a lower grade, and that decision becomes binding on every student who never had a vote in it.

### Case Study: The American Temperance Movement

Prohibition is the clearest example. Gusfield (1963) traced how a more held intensely by one native-born, Protestant, middle-class group was converted, through decades of campaigning, into the federal Eighteenth Amendment.

Was this really about public health? Gusfield argued it worked just as much asstatus politics.

It was a way for that group to reassert its own social standing against the immigrant, largely Catholic, urban culture displacing it. Legislating a more into law can work as symbolic reassurance for the group whose standard gets enshrined.

Prohibition itself did not last. Once enforcement proved unworkable, it was repealed within little more than a decade. That is the pipeline running in reverse: a law had outrun the more it was built on.

The same reverse pattern shows up elsewhere. Laws criminalising behaviour once treated as a serious more-violation get repealed once public attitudes shift ahead of them (Manning, 2017).

### Examples of Mores

Mores are norms that are widely observed and have great moral significance. Mores include gossiping, stealing, lying, bullying, and breaking a promise.

### 1. Sexual and Relationship Mores

How much have sexual mores actually changed?A decades-long U.S. survey put this to a direct test.

Aim:Did American sexual attitudes genuinely change between the early 1970s and the early 2010s? Twenge, Sherman, and Wells wanted to know.

They also asked a second question. Did any change come from each new generation holding different attitudes, rather than existing adults changing their minds (Twenge, Sherman, & Wells, 2015)?

Method:The researchers analysed General Social Survey data. Over 33,000 American adults were surveyed between 1972 and 2012. Statistical modelling separated the effects of historical period, birth generation, and a respondent’s own age.

Results:Acceptance of premarital sex rose sharply. It climbed from 29% of respondents in the early 1970s to 58% by 2010–2012. Acceptance of extramarital sex, by contrast, stayed low across the same four decades.

Conclusion:Generational replacement drove the shift, not individuals changing their own minds. A more’s content can change substantially within a few generations.

The underlying moral weight of a related more, like marital fidelity, can stay constant at the very same time.

### FAQs

Mores are the regulator of social life whilenormsare expectations that govern the behavior of individuals in a community. Mores are a subset of norms, representing the morality and character of a group or community.

Generally, they are considered to be absolutely right. On the other hand, norms can involve customs and expected behaviors that are more flexible and can change over time.

They usually deal with day-to-day behavior and are not as deeply ingrained as mores. While the violation of a norm may be uncomfortable, the violation of a more is usually socially unacceptable.

Taboos are bans against behaviors considered unclean or immoral, often rooted in religious belief. They carry a strong emotional charge.

Violating a taboo can bring social ostracism, or worse. A taboo is not really separate from a more. It is the most extreme, most intensely sanctioned end of the mores spectrum, where a violation feels unthinkable, not just wrong.

Monogamous relationships were a more in 20th-century European societies, the expected behavior, while polygamy was taboo (Nicolaisen, 2004).

The paradigm case: the incest taboo.Some version of a ban on sex between close kin appears in nearly every documented society. It is the closest thing to a genuine human universal among all mores.

Is that near-universal taboo best explained by a society’s need for cohesion? Or by something more biological? The strongest evidence comes from one unusually well-designed natural experiment.

Aim:Arthur Wolf set out to test the Westermarck hypothesis. The idea, first advanced by Edvard Westermarck, is simple: close childhood contact automatically reduces adult sexual attraction between those who shared it, regardless of moral teaching (Wolf, 1970).

Method:Wolf found a natural experiment in a traditional Taiwanese marriage custom. The custom had two forms.

In “minor marriages,” a young girl was adopted into her future husband’s household as a small child. In “major marriages,” by contrast, bride and groom first met as adults.

Comparing outcomes across thousands of historical records let Wolf isolate childhood association from genetic relatedness.

Results:Minor marriages showed substantially higher divorce rates. Fertility was lower too, despite there being no blood relation at all.

Conclusion:Childhood association alone was enough to reduce adult sexual attraction. Genetics and moral instruction were not required. That is direct evidence for a biological mechanism behind the incest taboo’s near-universal reach.

Evaluation:Wolf measured real marital outcomes, not self-reported attitudes. That is a genuine strength. But it is one historical population studied through archival records, so the finding cannot simply be repeated elsewhere.

Folkwaysare informal rules and norms that are not necessarily offensive to violate, but are nonetheless expected to be followed.

Mores are different. Left unwritten, they can still bring severe punishment and social sanction when violated.

Shaking hands firmly when meeting someone new is a folkway, not a more. Skipping the handshake probably will not cause offense or lead to sanctions.

Not lying, on the other hand, is a more. Being considered a liar can severely damage a person’s ability to communicate and gain others’ trust (Meltzer, 2005).

The line between the two is sharper on paper than it is in practice. Not saying “thank you” for a gift sits closer to folkway-level etiquette than to a more’s full moral weight. So does wearing casual clothes to a formal event.

Plagiarising a term paper sits much more clearly on the more side of that line. So does using profanity during a religious rite.

### Critical Evaluation

Sumner’s account of mores has drawn direct empirical testing since 1906. The core claim holds up well. The claim that mores are slow to change does not.

### Contemporary Research

The strongest recent evidence for how mores are enforced comes from one place: a large, pre-registered, global study.

Aim:Eriksson, Strimling, Gelfand, and colleagues asked a simple question. Do people everywhere judge norm violations by the same rule, or does it vary by culture (Eriksson et al., 2021)?

Method:The study surveyed 22,863 participants across 57 countries. Each person rated vignettes of norm violations, and rated possible responses too: verbal confrontation, gossip, ostracism, or doing nothing.

Results:One pattern held everywhere. The more wrong a violation was judged, the more appropriate direct confrontation and gossip seemed as a response.

Which specific sanction counted as most appropriate still varied by country. Verbal confrontation won in 26 countries. Doing nothing won in 31. Wealthier, more individualistic, more gender-equal countries leaned toward gossip instead.

Conclusion:People everywhere share one basic rule: how wrong a violation seems predicts how strongly it should be punished. But which specific sanction a culture reaches for is not universal at all.

It shifts as societies grow wealthier, more individualistic, and more gender-equal (Eriksson et al., 2021). The pattern repeats worldwide.

Evaluation:This is a large, pre-registered study spanning almost 23,000 people in 57 countries. That is a real strength. Its main limit: the ratings were hypothetical, not observed real-world sanctioning.

### Rival Theoretical Accounts

Sumner’s own account fits a broad functionalist tradition. At least three rival readings compete with it, and a good methods paper weighs all of them rather than picking a favourite.

Here is how each one holds up.

Thefunctionalistreading treats mores as the moral core of group welfare. A more’s sanction is severe in proportion to how much collective cohesion it protects.Value consensusdevelops this mechanism further.

Gusfield’s temperance case study challenges that reading directly. Was Prohibition really about the whole of society’s shared welfare?

Aconflict-theoryaccount says no. It treats mores that succeed in becoming law as reflecting whichever group has the power to make its morality stick, not a consensus everyone shares.

Two more challenges complicate the picture further.

A third account competes for at least one more: the incest taboo. That may be more biological than social. A psychological mechanism could be at work, independent of any society’s institutions or power. See the evidence discussed under Mores vs. Taboos.

A different problem arises here too.

A fourth critique asks a harder question: is there always one shared more to find? A 24-country survey found Spanish respondents split almost evenly on whether homosexuality was always or never wrong, with few people in between (Widmer et al., 1998).

A “shared” more may really be whichever position has the most organised support right now, not a single fact everyone in a culture believes. It is not always one settled fact.

### Key Takeaways

- Definition:Mores are the strongest type of social norm – unwritten moral standards a society treats as right or wrong, not just polite or impolite.

- Vs. Folkways:Folkways are casual customs (table manners, greetings) that draw mild disapproval if broken. Mores draw real moral condemnation.

- Enforcement:Most sanctioning is informal – gossip, exclusion, a damaged reputation – but a serious violation can escalate to formal punishment, such as expulsion.

- Mores Become Law:Moral entrepreneurs can turn a widely-held more into formal law, as happened with Prohibition – and the process can run in reverse.

- Cross-Cultural Variation:What counts as a more, and how seriously it is punished, varies enormously by culture and by era.

- Modern Evidence:A 57-country study confirms that violating a more draws a harsher response everywhere, but which specific sanction a culture reaches for varies with its wealth and individualism (Eriksson et al., 2021).
